BOOK ONE


CHAPTER 1

I had won my last world by becoming a hive of bugs, and I had not meant to wake. I woke anyway, in another body -- a woman with no name in this city, in King's Landing, in the year before the year before the dragons danced. The flies came with me, two hundred metres of them, thick in the air. The first thing I decided was that nothing I had done at the end would be done here.

Three weeks in this ward, and what I had learned was how heavy it is to do nothing. I slept in the angle of a Hook alley where the drain ran out, and the air there was full of small bodies I would not use. I came to be useful without taking control. That was the rule I set before anything else.

It is the oldest thing I have left. It is, most mornings, the only thing.

  

The drain ran thin along the angle of the lane, and the morning smelled of tallow-smoke and fish -- the smoke off the stitch-house two lanes over, where a girl I had not met yet kept her needles. I kept still against the wall. Keeping still was the one discipline I had carried out of the last world that still did what it was built for, and it cost me something to hold, every hour I was awake. The flies wanted the warm bodies down the lane the way water wants the low place. I held them at the edge of where they pulled, and I held my own feet planted, and I let the morning go by without taking any of it.

Then a child went down.

I heard it before the crowd did -- the small wet catch of a breath that could not get all the way in, somewhere past the fish-cart that had stopped crosswise in the lane. The people nearest her did what crowds do. They pressed in to see, and the lane closed over her like water, and the closing was the thing that would kill her: the heat of bodies stacking, the air going foul, the girl too flat to call out under all of it.

I did not so much decide it as find it already decided. The flies were on the walls and in the seams of the stone, and I set the nearest of them crawling -- low and sudden, across the backs of hands and the napes of necks, the kind of thing a body flinches from before the mind has a word for it. The dozen people closest to the girl stepped back without knowing why they stepped. The ones behind them felt the gap and filled it wrong, and I pressed at the edges of them the same way until the opening held. A gap came clear around her. Through it I could see her now: six, maybe seven, her lips gone blue-grey, her mother's hands useless on her chest.

"Fever," I said, loud, over their heads. "Not the croup. She needs air. Stand back -- who knows her? Fetch them."

It was true, and being true it moved them. Hands went up. Someone ran. The girl's chest lifted, caught, lifted again -- and I came the last few strides through the lane I had opened, and stood over her with my own hands raised and empty, touching no one, touching not even the child. I was the foreign woman who had made a road through a crowd with her mouth shut and her palms in the air, and I let them see exactly that and nothing under it.

  

I let the flies settle.

The gap stayed open on its own now, the crowd thinning the way a Flea Bottom crowd thins once a thing is handled and no longer worth standing in. The fish-cart man looked at me, openly, the way you look at something you mean to remember. Two women at the edge of the press looked at the lane and not at me, which was better. They carried the girl out between them, and the people closed the space behind her, and the morning went back to being a morning.

An old man took the mouth of the lane and stood in it.

I knew him a little. Oswyn Mudway -- a ward-elder, thirty years on these streets, the kind of man who notices a thing a week before he has the word for it. He settled his hands at his apron and looked at me the way you look at a tool you have just watched do work you had not expected of it. He did not come closer. He did not have to. He lifted his chin a small degree, the way a man does when something he had only half-believed in has walked up the lane and stood in front of him wearing a face. I turned to the alley-mouth and set my body to it, to give him less to read.

And the stitch-maker's girl -- eleven years old, a watcher, the kind who studies cloth before she cuts it and studies people the same way -- found my face, eyes first. She was small in the thinning crowd and she did not look away when I noticed her looking. I put her where I keep the things I do not say aloud and do not let myself weigh.

Then I held still, and I did not feed anyone, and I did not move a single fly, and that was the whole of what it cost to be a person who had done nothing.


CHAPTER 2

The crowd had broken open around the stitch-maker's ward, and the hands had gone up before mine did. After that I went back to the drain angle and did not feed anyone for three weeks. The late summer ran out somewhere in those weeks, and the Hook began keeping its mornings by the salt-day rhythm -- the fishmongers' three-day, the carter-day, the lay-day the salt-cured trade kept between. I held still, which still cost me something every hour I was awake. What I did instead of feeding was listen -- at first because that is what survives in the Hook, and then because the listening had a shape I had begun to extend on purpose. The rule was the same rule. Reading was not directing. I had not crossed the line.

What I built I did not yet have a name for. The flies told me where heat clustered, and the beetles told me whose feet had crossed which stone and how often, and I kept a count of it in my head -- who was sick, who was hungry, which alley junction emptied before dark and which one never did. When I was being plain with myself I called it a coverage map. The instrument was harm-reduction. That was how it stayed permitted.

  

I woke against the drain angle with the flies already out. They had spread down the lane in the night the way they spread every night now, and the beetles had run the threshold-stones, and two alleys east three bodies were running a heat I could feel through them -- close together, not moving, the particular stillness of the sick. I could not see the alley from where I lay. I did not need to. I stepped the flies toward the warmth and let them carry me the shape of it: three signatures, maybe in one room, maybe split across two, too tangled to call yet. The insects reached the second alley and held there.

Then I got up. I left the drain angle and took the alley-mouth with the wall against my right shoulder, and I pushed the rest of the insects out wide.

I had told myself, the first week, that reading was not directing -- that a woman who lay still in a drain and let the bugs bring her the city was doing nothing a sharp-eared beggar did not do with her ears. The line was real. I drew it carefully every morning, and every morning it sat a little further out than it had the day before.

The insects crossed the threshold-stones and filled the Hook lane by lane and found their count: four hundred bodies, give or take, as far as I could hold them before the far edge began to thin and drop. An alley emptied as I reached for it -- no warmth left in it, only cold stone -- and I drew the line there and held the rest. I felt the whole shape of it then: the foot-traffic knotting at the ward-junction where three alleys gave onto one another, the tallow smoke pooling thick at the lane-mouth, the sick and the hungry and the quiet corners all laid out where I could find them with my eyes shut. I stayed still against the wall and let it settle.

  

I took the drain angle again with the tallow smoke marking the stitch-house lane, and the beetles crossed the stitch-house threshold and settled on its lintel, and that was when I felt her -- not a name, a body. The same weight on the same threshold-stone, day on day, in the same order: a woman crossing the ward-junction, and the alley taking her in.

The insects reached the edge of the junction-lane and I yielded the alley-mouth before the yield was a choice. I could not put the flies down that lane without being seen down it, and being seen by the wrong person at the wrong hour was a cost I had not budgeted. So I pulled the insects back from the junction-lane edge and faced the alley-mouth and held there. I drew them off the stitch-house lane entirely and stepped them past it, out the far side, and let them spread the lane beyond -- and even there I heard her steps sound the far threshold, ahead of me, on ground I had reasoned myself out of crossing.

That was the shape of it. Everywhere she moved was somewhere I would not follow, so the map had a hole in it exactly her size: negative space at the junction where a body plainly was, the one body the coverage could not close over. I gave her a place in the count without going any nearer -- a junction-woman, a connector, the kind a ward flows through without her being its center. The insects returned from the junction-lane sweep, and I turned from the alley-mouth, and filed her where she fit, and did not look closer.

  

I settled against the drain angle as the insects came back from the lane-mouth sweep, and the shadow came across the gap by degrees and filled the angle until the drain was the darkest point in the lane.

By dark I had the whole of it. I walked the corners one at a time, the way you count coin you already know the sum of. Three bodies still running hot in the fever corner. The dark-junction corner gone quiet under its shadow. Forty-three people, each one fixed by where they slept and who they crossed and when -- none of whom had ever seen my face.

I reached the ward-junction lane last, where the stitch-maker walked, and my hand stopped.

It stopped the way a hand stops over a page when the next word is one you did not mean to write. I held the breath I had just taken, and I closed against the wall -- chest into shoulder, shoulder into brick, the weight gone onto the rear foot -- and then I went on. Past the fever corner. Past the dark-junction corner. Past the ward-junction lane and the woman in it, not slowing at any of them, because slowing was the thing I could not afford. They would have died without me. Reading was not directing. The line was where the line was and I had not crossed it.

I let the breath out. The ward-junction lane emptied, and the insects settled back into the angle around me, and in the fever corner three bodies still pressed close in their heat. I had a map now, and no name to put on it, and I did not look at the shape of what the map was, because looking was a door and I had already decided not to open it.


CHAPTER 3

By the end of that day I had a map of the lower city and no name to put on it. The flies brought me the streets, and the streets told me who walked them and who had stopped walking -- the ward-junction body crossing the threshold of the stitch-house at the same hour the dark-junction corner went quiet under its shadow. I did not feed anyone. I did not direct. The rule was still the rule. What I had built I had not yet shown to anyone, and that was the second rule, the one I had never said to myself in those words.

The morning market was where the streets condensed. It was a salt-day, the second of the fishmongers' three, which meant the stalls would run heavy until the third bell and thin sharp after -- the rhythm the Hook kept its weeks by, fixed and unwritten. I walked it because the flies could not give me the foot-traffic at distance, and because moving through the stalls was the cheapest way to refresh what I knew without giving anyone a watcher to find. The work was what I did instead of the thing the work made possible.

  

The morning market filled the Hook shoulder-to-shoulder, tallow-wax and fish-salt thick in the press of bodies, and I moved through the stalls without stopping, the flies riding the stall-edges ahead of me. They found the stitch-maker's girl at the salt-fish row a beat before my eyes did -- the same eleven-year-old I had marked at the stitch-house lane the morning of the cart, her gait crossing the third stall and going on past it without slowing. She did not look up. I let her pass and kept walking.

At a salt-fish stall a man stood with his hands at his sides, his eyes on me before I had marked him as anything -- Jarvis Coin, a courier of the kind the lower city ran for patrons who did not put their names on the routes. Transactional. Paid by the delivery.

The flies caught him three seconds before my eyes did: a stillness against the grain of the market, the one thing that does not move when everything around it is moving.

I slowed my pace by one step, hands loose, eyes on the next stall rather than on him.

He spoke.

"The foreign woman who cleared the crowd off Butcher's Lane the morning the Mudway child took fever. That's you."

I went still, weight on my back foot, both hands visible at my sides. Hands visible was the cheaper answer. The back foot was the one I would not show him.

"You walk the same six streets between dawn and the third bell. The sick cluster where you cluster. Three alley-mouths off Pisswater Bend you check twice before the second bell, and no one else on that route looks at them once."

He had not guessed. He had counted. And he had counted the way I counted -- which meant someone above him had taught him to, or someone above him was the one counting and Jarvis only carried the sum.

I kept three steps of open street between us and let my shoulders stay loose for the crowd passing on both sides. To anyone walking by, two people haggling.

"There is a man who would like to put a question to you. The cooper's yard off Eel Alley, third bell, day after tomorrow."

The rescue I had run was already known to someone who could afford a courier. Him naming the alley only told me how far it had already traveled.

"The cooper's yard. Third bell."

I walked past his stall without looking back, and kept the flies on him down the row until he turned out of the market.

  

The cooper's yard off Eel Alley sat two cuts north of the river-side stalls -- near enough to the Hook for the tidal smell, far enough that the third-bell market did not reach it. Two workers at the near shed, a boy stacking hoops at the far end. No foot traffic past the third bell, no clear sightline from the lane-mouth. The market noise gave way to the low murmur of men at slow work.

I reached the yard before him, set my back to the shed wall with a clear line to the yard-mouth, and spread the flies thin across the two workers and the boy.

Jarvis came in at the third bell with no document, no letter, no seal, and took a position three steps from the yard-mouth.

"Otto Hightower. The Hand who was. The man who still runs the channels the Small Council does not audit."

Otto Hightower had been Hand of the King -- the chief office under the throne, the manager of the realm's business when the king's attention was elsewhere -- until his dismissal two years before. He had not stopped working.

I held my weight still and kept my eyes on the yard-mouth. The name landed clean, the way a name lands when the man saying it has been made to say it precisely.

"What he wants is what you already see. Movement in the wards below Rhaenys's Hill. Where the sickness gathers in the months before winter pressures the wells. Which junctions go restless and which go quiet in the weeks the Small Council sits. Frequency you set. Detail you set. Routed through me."

Rhaenys's Hill rose east of the Red Keep, the Dragonpit at its crown, the wards below it running from the noble-quarter approaches down toward the lower city.

I matched the streets he named to the streets my flies already walked every morning. They were the same streets. He was asking me to sell him something I was already carrying.

"Sera Hightower. Fourteen. A ward of the cadet branch, kept in the Queen Consort's household. Her father's claim has a question in it. If that question reaches the Small Council, she is put out. It goes into the record. The record does not come back out. Three months is all it would take, with the right man to set it there. The patron has kept it from the council twice. He needs the cover to do it again."

A girl placed in Alicent's household for the kind of advancement minor branches bought with appropriately-presented daughters. Fourteen. Put out, the place forfeit, the question set in a record that did not reopen.

I counted the two workers at the near shed and watched the boy lift another hoop to the stack. Then I set the girl beside the streets I already walked -- the ward he named, real, and at real risk; the work he wanted, work I was already doing -- and the two sides sat level. There was no inaccuracy to refuse him on. My hand closed once at the cloth of my dress and opened.

"I have heard you."

The offer hung in the tallow-damp air between us -- a named ward at a named risk, a named patron, a named price, and no answer. At the far end the boy lifted a hoop to the stack and did not look over. Jarvis stood without adding to the proposal, hands at his sides, the weight of a man who had delivered and was waiting. I held my position, back to the shed wall, the yard-mouth in clear line.

  

The yard had the same workers, the same boy at the hoop-stack, the same tallow-damp drifting from the lane-caulking two alleys over.

I did not decide things when I was pressed to. It was a rule I had kept since King's Landing and before King's Landing -- since the last time I made a decision under pressure at a scale that mattered. I said:

"A day."

My eyes stayed on the yard-mouth, weight even on both feet. A day was the smallest deferral the thing would take, and I had already counted what asking for it cost me -- which was that asking was not refusing, and he would carry that back as plainly as if I had said yes.

He held my eyes for a beat -- the beat in which a courier hears that the answer was not no -- and said: "Same place. First bell. Day after."

He did not ask why I needed a day. He did not look relieved or disappointed. He left the yard by the lane-mouth with the same unhurried pace he had come in with.

I held the shed wall until the flies followed him out of the lane.

Then I walked the streets below Rhaenys's Hill the only way I could from a cooper's yard -- through the flies, junction by junction, past the boy still stacking hoops and the two workers at the near shed. I was already doing the work. I had been doing the work for three weeks. The proposal was only asking me to hand a copy of it to someone else, and to take a girl's name in exchange for the copy.

I set the girl I could keep in the household beside the streets I already walked, and they asked me the same question, and I did not have the answer yet. I left the cooper's yard by the lane-mouth, the flies still riding the street beyond it.


CHAPTER 4

I had one day. The Maiden's name day fell on the third of the fishmongers' three, and a sept-bell would carry it across the Hook at first light whether anyone in the Hook marked it or not. I did not decide twice. I had decided in the hour the dark thinned and the flies came back in around me, and what I decided was that the harm I could prevent was the only thing worth the price -- that, and the name I had carried since before I had any flies to carry it with, a child two seasons back the slow way had buried before anyone could fetch help in time. The terms I had set for myself I would say to the courier at first bell, in the yard, in the same place he had first put the question. The rule I had never said aloud now had a price on it and a place to go, both.

  
The tallow-damp off the lane-caulking reached the shed-wall before the yard itself came into view, and the cooper's men were still trading their low first-light talk at the far side of the shed, none of them yet given a reason to look up. I crossed into the yard quiet, set my back to the shed-wall, and let the timber put a stretch of open ground between me and the lane-mouth.

I had Jarvis in the flies a span before my eyes did -- small bodies on the wall and in the seams of the stone, and they placed him in the lane and brought me the shape of him walking. Only then did he step into the lane-mouth in the body I was waiting on, unhurried, checking nothing, the gait of a man with no appointment to be late for.

My hand closed on the shed-wall behind me. The yes went across the yard-air level and plain, and I set against it the one thing I had kept back for myself.

"Yes. The terms hold with two changes. You will have patterns -- what moves through the streets, what does not, where the seams sit -- not raw report of who said what to whom. The interval is mine to set. The volume is mine to set."

I held my feet.

"The man I serve takes those terms," he said. "The volume yours. The interval yours. The substance pattern-reports from the wards you walk. Same place. First bell. Three days."

He said it the way a man signs for a parcel, no triumph in it, the thing settled before my body had finished bracing for it to be settled. The yes was no longer a thing I could not-have-said. That was the whole of it, and the changes I had won did not touch it.

I kept the flies on him as he turned. He walked out of the lane-mouth. I gave it the breath it needed, and then I left the yard.

By the time the light had come up enough to put work into the lanes, the discard-air had taken over from the tallow-damp at the waste-middens junction, the heaviness of it lying over the three alley-mouths the way smoke lies over a cold hearth before the draw catches.

I crossed into Pig Tallow Lane -- the first ward beyond the Hook, named for the rendering trade no one else would carry, three alleys feeding a middens-junction at its mouth -- and took my line along the wall the carter-work faced away from.

I pushed the flies out one ward further. It was no more than I had done in the Hook every morning of these three weeks -- the same walk, the same reading of who was agitated and which passage was being avoided and why. Different ground, the same work. The junction came up in pieces through them: three alleys, the cart-axis, the bodies bent to the rendering at the angles the cart imposed.

The penny-a-barrel carter pulled the middens cart up at the junction-mouth and set the brake.

On the cart's near side I found Oswyn Mudway -- the elder I had met once in the second month, here this hour at penny-a-barrel work, his net-weighing set aside for a day's coin in a ward not his own, the kind of labor a ward-elder picks up when the house runs thin. I knew him by his gait before the flies gave me his face. He did not know I was watching. He did not know he was now a thing on a map that ran upward through a courier to a man in a tower. A body already accounted for, I thought, and did not let the thought go any further. I marked where he stood and the two he ran with, and I walked on.

Down the lane I counted only ward-folk and nothing above them -- porters, renderers, the carter, no one whose business reached past the alley they stood in. At the far edge of where the flies still held, the stitch-house frames stood up at the next ward's line.

There, three streets over, I found Wren -- the same stillness at the frame I would have known anywhere, the two apprentices moving around it. She would not go down on any sheet. The report would carry the patterns the lane held, what moved and what did not and where the seams ran, and her body would not be among them.

I held my feet at the junction-mouth and did not take the lane that ran to the frames. The second ward came up in pieces at the reach of the flies -- the seams, the body-counts, the cart-work this ward carried in place of the rendering. I read it and I did not walk it.

Roper's Court came out of the early-morning grey with the sound emptied out of it. The tributary lanes ran off long and dark; the central court held clear to every mouth. A rope-making ward, its lanes straight enough to walk rope down -- the kind of ground I could read cleanest under the grey.

I pushed the flies out to the fourth ward. It was further than I had ever held them at once, and I felt the reach of it -- small bodies stretched thin across more stone than I had ever asked them to cover, the far edge always wanting to thin and drop. I held it anyway. The four wards stood open to me together -- the Hook, the stitch-house frames, the middens junction, Roper's Court -- one read held across all four footprints at once.

I held the four wards and walked the court.

By the time the first bell came up out of the Hook I was back at the cooper's yard with the shed-wall at my back and a half-yard of open air between the timber and the lane-mouth. Jarvis stepped into the lane-mouth at the count the flies had already given me, half a span before the body reached my eyes.

He raised a folded square of parchment to chest-height between us. The crease ran clean across it, and he held it in plain hand, uncovered. He did not open it. The showing was the message -- Otto's word that Sera was managed, that the three-month window had closed -- and he held the paper at the height a hand at the lane-mouth would have to take it in.

I drew my own sheet from my coat. It was folded twice, the smaller fold turned inward, and I crossed the half-step of yard-air between us and put it into his hand at the height his note had been raised to. His fingers closed on it and mine came away, and the thing was done before I had quite felt it go. I held my feet.

He pocketed the sheet into the inner seam of his coat without opening it. The cloth settled over the place where it had gone, and his hand came back empty to his side.

What sat in his coat now was patterns and nothing else. I had kept the Hook's sick off it -- no fever-counts, no triage, none of the layer that was mine to carry and no one else's. I had kept every name off it that lived above ward-level, and Wren's off it, and Oswyn's. The sheet held the lanes I had walked, which alleys carried what and which seams ran where the cart-work routed around them and the body-counts at the hours I had read them -- and it gave the man above no foothold to climb past any of that.

He touched the brim of his cap the small amount the thing required, turned in the lane-mouth, and walked out at the gait he had come in on.

I let the empty yard hold for the breath it needed, then left on the line away from the lane Jarvis had taken.

I walked back through the Hook with the flies still wide across all four wards -- body-counts near, the middens junction at the mid-range, Pig Tallow's rendering-yard angles beside it, Roper's Court at the outer reach. I was reading the wards and feeding the wards and reading what I fed in the one unbroken act, and the day's work did not come apart into the part that helped and the part that paid. It ran whole or it did not run.

At the stitch-house range the flies brought me Wren again. The print of her came the way it had the day before -- the gait given to the body, the body to the name, the name held inside the reading and given to nothing past it. The four-ward read ran on through her, and the report was already going the other way in Jarvis's coat. I carried both at once and did not slow for either.

The north-end lane-mouth at the frames came up at the edge of the Hook alley. I held my stride past the lamp-frame and the second frame beyond it, and I did not break it for the lane the frames stood on. I crossed past the stitch-house lane and held my line into the Hook. The report did not include Wren. That was the anchor holding. I did not ask myself whether it would hold.

The chandler's-storehouse corner came up at the cross-lane. The flies gave me the Septon at his corner -- Halvard, the Faith's man for this stretch, a fixture since my first week, his gait read against that wall a hundred times. The crossing of our paths gave me nothing new. I gave him the brief nod a person gives a fixture they have long since logged, the nod that costs the same small coin every morning, and the corner went past on my right without breaking my stride.


CHAPTER 5

Two mornings back I had given the four-ward sheet to the courier in the cooper's yard, and by the same afternoon I had named the thing the arrangement was and walked back from it without changing a line of it. The harm I could prevent was still the only harm I let myself weigh. The cold had come in two nights before -- the bay-wind turning, the dry summer-dust on the cobbles gone over to a damp that found the back of your hand on every wall you touched. This morning I pushed the flies past the stitch-house lane into a ward where the alleys lean up toward the hill, ground I had not walked before, ground that backs onto the lower servant-passages of the Red Keep.

The arrangement bought safety for a fourteen-year-old, a ward of some Hightower cadet branch placed in Alicent's household. Her name was Sera. She stood on a question about her parentage that Otto's rivals could raise to wound him, and Otto kept the question quiet by knowing what moved at court before they did. What I sent him through the courier was what paid for the quiet. I had agreed to it. So now I read court bodies.

  

The hill's stone skirt met the lane-mouth at grade, the overnight damp still cool under the paving, the light coming in off the harbor and stopping at the north face of the junction in shade.

I stepped into the Rushwick -- a knot of lanes between the hill and the city's upward lean, alleys too narrow to sell from but wide enough to pass through at a run, the last of them giving onto the Red Keep's lower servant-doors -- and held at the edge of the junction.

I spread the flies across it. They went onto the lintels and into the wall-seams and under the shadow of the carts, the way they always went, small bodies settling into new stone the same as they had settled into the old.

A provisioner-train came up from the south side, four carts and the men who walked them, the wheels grinding over paving not yet worn smooth at the turn. I read them the way I read every body since the flies first went out: the forward-lean of men carrying loads against a tight delivery hour. Nothing in them asked me to feel anything, so I felt nothing.

They turned down the east-lane, the stone narrowing along the hill-skirt as the lane dropped off into the morning past the wall. Their walk was not Hook walking. It was a different kind of body under the same eye -- and I left the thing above that walk, the thing it answered to, unnamed.

A message-runner came across behind them, alone, no load, a tighter and more compressed stride: the body of someone who answers to someone, walking the interval he is paid to walk. He took the lane-mouth at the line where my flies thinned out, and they let him go past the wall before the next man came.

Then the next man came.

He came on at the same threshold the runner had taken, walking the heel-first gait I had seen three mornings now on the same lane -- the courier. A man I had seen and not named, a recurring face with no name on it. I held still at the wall the way I held at every junction, the stillness in the body and not in the head, keeping count of the bodies even where the wall broke my line of sight.

The three figures came in from the side.

They went into the side-alley off the east exit, a passage one man wide, its mouth in plain view from where I stood and its inside not, and they took the mouth at its full width and stood in it.

The third one hit him.

The alley threw the sound back off its own stone. The walls turned the blow inward and then let it out at the mouth -- a low, working sound, no cry in it, the sound a body makes against stone when it is trying not to make any sound at all, only the labor of taking the force and the labor of not answering it. I had eyes on the mouth and not the inside, and what came out of the inside was a thing the flies had no way to carry.

The courier pushed himself up the wall. He found his feet.

I set the beating down the way I set down anything else: three men, working together, the mouth of the alley blocked before they touched him, the whole shape of it wrong for a robbery and right for enforcement -- a specific man, hit for a specific reason. I wrote it into the day's report in the same hand I used for the avoided passages and the agitation at the junctions: how many bodies, how they came on, how long it took, how it ended. The report took it the way the report took everything.

Then I drafted the message for Jarvis -- the message that bought one girl's safety at the length of someone else's arm -- and I drafted it clean.

The three cleared the alley-mouth, the east-lane open behind them, and went off east in the order they had come in.

I marked the courier on the body-map: recurring Rushwick man, the beating attached to him, no name where a name would go. He took the junction-corner forty feet off from me, where the sightline ran clean again, and I let him go the way I had let the runner go.

  

By evening I had the lodging room to myself. I left the lamp unlit because the window's last light was the light I liked to work in, and the room held that still an indoor room keeps when nothing moves through it -- a denser still than the lane's, banked in the corners, the boards dry under me where the lane had been damp at the wall. I sat with my back to the bedframe and laid the day out on the floor in front of me, the way you lay out coin to count it.

I walked the Hook through the flies first, the morning's lane below the Hook brought back the way it had gone out, and it came back the same: the bodies, the gait of each one, the avoided thresholds, all of it closing up clean against itself, the lane settling into the shape it kept when nothing in it had asked to be kept. The stitch-maker's print held a beat longer than the rest at the salt-fish row, a little wider than the bodies around her. I did not name the extra length. I let it sit inside the close and did not work at it.

Then I walked the Rushwick. The provisioner-train crossed the junction again at the hour it had crossed; on past it to the courier, and the three figures, and the report I had written at the line where my filing closed.

It would not lie flat. My breath went shallow at the bedframe. The bay-damp had reached the room since I had come back, and the boards had taken it on. The color that had been on the junction in the morning was on the junction now -- present in the going-back the way it had been present in the seeing, the same architecture coming back with the train every time the train came back. I ran it through a second time to see whether it was me and not the thing I was seeing. The color stayed.

My head had tilted toward it before I knew I had moved.

I lifted my hand off the boards. They were cold under the palm, and where I had lifted it there was a hand-print of the warmth the palm had left. The flies were a thing I had kept neutral, a thing with a use and no opinion, and now they were handing me back the route I had built as the route -- not the lane, not the courier, but the thing the route ran through, the thing I had never asked them to mark and could not now make them stop marking. I had been keeping a neutral read. The neutral read was the thing I could no longer keep.

I closed the day's review. The window's light had thinned to a line above the sill.

I held the courier's face a moment -- the recurring man, the beating still hanging off him -- and then I let it go.

I tried the Rushwick flat. The train crossed the junction at the morning hour and brought the color in with it; the courier's walk came back colored, not flat. It would not run the way the Hook had run. It would not close clean against itself. It would not give me the junction as a count and nothing else. The door I had turned toward did not open at the turn.

I ran the pass once more, slower this time, taking it at a length deliberate enough to let the flat read seat if it was going to seat.

The color held.


CHAPTER 6

The Father's name day was three nights off, and the sept-bells had begun the longer count -- two strokes added at every hour, audible across the Hook in the cold mornings before the carters started their work. For weeks I had been sending Otto the same kind of thing up the channel: who passed which junction, which lane went unused, the shape a week of foot-traffic makes. It bought the quiet on Sera's question and it asked nothing of me a decent person could not have folded into a morning's walk. What he asked for next would not fold so easily.

  

The handcart had thrown a wheel across the mouth of the lane, crosswise, and there was no setting it upright before midday. I came down into a press of people who had already understood as much. They stood the way Flea Bottom stands when a thing it cannot move is in the way -- patient, not frightened, shoulders turned to keep the angle. Tallow smoke pooled at foot level and would not rise. The crowd had backed up solid into the junction, body against body, and the morning that had been passable an hour before was a wall of warmth and cloth and the smell of the boiling-houses. I stood at the edge and looked for the way out.

She found me before her feet had turned. I caught the moment her eyes lifted out of the press and fixed on me, and only then did the rest of her start to move, crossing the backed-up crowd at a slant, the way a child crosses a current she has crossed before. I knew her before she opened her mouth -- the stitch-maker's ward, eleven years old, the small steady profile I had seen at the cart-morning and the salt-fish row and a dozen evening closings since. A ward-body, familiar. What I could not place was the thing she was about to do.

"There's a way past. Cut before the cart, by the tallow-boiler's wall -- the south court. It's narrow, but it's there. I been through."

She said it the way you set down a thing you have already carried, and she did not wait to watch me pick it up. She turned back into the press and found her own gap. A sound route from a stranger is not nothing. I followed where she had pointed.

The south court was just where she had said -- a narrow slot off the tallow-boiler's wall, clear, the blocked lane behind me. I came in out of the smoke and took the wax-leaf from my coat, the small folding one I keep my own notes on, the one no channel ever sees. Ward-resident, Hook, I wrote, and routine. That was true, and it was the whole of what I wrote. The line where a name would go I left bare. I had decided that before the stylus touched the wax -- that her name would not sit anywhere a careful man could one day read it. I folded the leaf and put it away, and went on with my walk.

  

The courier was waiting at the bench when I came back through, a sealed slip set out for me and the man already gone. I broke it and read it. It did not ask for the shape of a week's foot-traffic the way the asks before it had. It asked for names. The Black-faction ward elders in the Hook wards, by name -- four of them. I read it through and then I read it again, because reading a thing twice is how I make sure I have the size of it.

I knew them. Four months of watching those wards, and the four were already faces to me -- who they met, whose errands they ran, the particular way a man moves when he takes his instruction from somewhere other than the ward's own elders. I had been holding them as bodies, as habits. I had never set them down as names. I sorted the four out of memory, one and then the next, and wrote them in order on the blank form the channel wanted, in my own hand. Four names. Then I laid the form face-down on the bench and did not send it. The not-sending sat there between my hands and was the loudest thing in the court.

  

Before I sent it I would do the thing I always did. I flattened my own paper beside the form and set the four names down the one side of it, one under the next, the stylus the only sound now -- scratch, lift, scratch -- and under that sound the four of them stopped being a cluster I watched and became four men in my own letters, which is a different weight. On the other side I wrote Sera's name. Fourteen years old, a ward of Alicent's household, a girl I had never met and never would, exposed in ways she did not know she was exposed; the whole reason the arrangement existed. I drew a line under the two columns.

There was no trick in it. The cost to the four men was real and I did not make it smaller; the danger to Sera was real and I did not make it larger. Both sides stood there in my own hand and the line under them was honest, and an honest line came down on the side of sending. That was the whole horror of it -- not that I had cheated the sum, but that I had not. The stylus stopped a finger's width above the page. My hand stood over what I had written and did not come down, not because anything was still in doubt but because the hand was slower than the verdict, and wanted a moment I would not get to keep. I set the paper aside.

I turned the form face-up and pressed my seal into the wax. Once the wax takes the seal there is no lifting it, and nothing on a sealed form comes back. The courier took it and was gone, the four names off the bench and into the arrangement, past my reach.

I drew out the wax-leaf again and opened it to the morning's entry -- the blocked lane, the south-court route, ward-resident, Hook, routine, and the bare line where a name was not. The same hand had made both that morning: four names sent up the channel, and one name I had refused to write down at all. I looked at the two of them sitting there and said nothing to myself about it, because there was nothing to say I did not already know. I folded the leaf and put it away.


CHAPTER 7

Two months on, and the cost of the thing I was running had gone quiet -- not because it had stopped costing, but because it had become the shape of an ordinary morning, and a cost you stop feeling is the one that has finished its work on you. The bay-wind had turned damp in those weeks; the carters' breath stood over the shafts, and the sept-bells kept the long count of the season the Faith calls the Crone's stretch. Nothing in the lanes that morning told me an argument was waiting at the sept corner. It was not the kind of thing the flies could carry.

There were four men I had grown used to seeing in the morning lanes, and that morning none of the four was where he stood. I had watched them a hundred and eighty mornings running -- a carter at the rendering-yard junction, two others I knew by their lean and their hours, an old one who took the Hook corner at first light -- and the flies went out across the ward and came back without them. I did not let myself wonder long. I had learned not to wonder at things like that out loud, even to myself.

Other men stood where the four had been. A different shoulder leaned at the carter's near side; a patrol came through the Hook at an hour I had not seen a patrol come through in two months, and went back the way it came, and came again. The corners were held. They were only held by a different set of hands than had held them yesterday, and no one had said why, and I did not have anyone to ask. So I marked it the way you mark weather and went on down toward the corner.

The way down was blocked. A handcart sat across the passage where the chandler's storehouse abuts the sept, its bed wide enough that the lane choked to a single cart's width and could not pass. The man who owned it stood at the shafts, and facing him, hearing him out, was Septon Halvard -- the Faith's man for this stretch of the Hook, no proper sept to his name, just the corner of a storehouse and the sick he tends. He has held the precinct seven years. He asks plainness of people, not agreement.

He was naming the man's child. A fever, he said, the way you say a thing that is simply true and known between you -- a boy named Derry, up the lane, two days into it. He said it to me as much as to the carter, plainly, the way you acknowledge someone who belongs where they are standing. I took the acknowledgment and gave nothing back to it. I had the flies on the storehouse eaves and on the cold stone of the corner, and through them I had Halvard the way I'd had him since my first week here -- a fixed point I crossed most days, a man I knew the circuit of and had never once stood close enough to call a man. I knew him the way I knew the candle-smell off the storehouse and the cold coming up off the stone. As a feature of the ground.

  

I came in past the cleared shaft of the cart, into the corner where the sept-bay's shadow lay across the crossing-stone and the lane's cold met the corner's cold at the threshold. I gave him the nod you give a fixture you have decided to greet.

He went on with the boy's fever -- how far it had run, what it had taken, where it sat now -- and then named what a maester would cost to call, plainly, no appeal in it. Then he turned off the account of the sick child and faced me square, and his voice did not change at all, which was how I knew the next part was not about the child.

"There's a man up the Lane runs errands for a house that pays him in bread, not coin," he said. "Good bread. His children eat. I've no quarrel with a man feeding his children. But the house pays him that way because it suits the house, and every loaf he takes, he takes on the house's terms, and the terms don't get smaller for the taking. They stay the size they were. A thing built crooked doesn't come straight because you lean on it gently. It grows crooked at its own rate. The slow way costs him more now -- he'd go hungry a while, refusing. It costs him less after. The debt doesn't sit there gathering."

I went still. Not deliberating -- the body simply stopped, the way it does when a thing you have been carrying gets set down in front of you in another man's hands. A crooked house does not come straight by leaning it gently. The leaning compounds at its own rate. That was the architecture of the thing I had built, exactly, and he was saying it back to me in a stranger's mouth without knowing it was mine, and the figures matched.

I faced him. For two months the resentment I carried had had no object to fasten to -- it had been a kind of weather -- and turning to face him handed it one. Not the man. The structure he had just described. I had tracked his circuit those two months as a node on a map, and I was looking at him as a man for the first time.

I planted my feet. Everything in me wanted to route around the cart and close the circuit and be gone, and staying was the more expensive thing, and I paid it. He saw a woman who had decided not to leave the conversation, and what I gave him was the staying, and not yet the answer.

He let a breath out into the cold and did not press.

The stone's cold came up through the soles of my feet where I stood, the corner-stone hard and unwarmed under the planted weight.

  

I named the dead child. My breath went out before the name did.

"Then the account has to carry the middle," I said. "Your count of graves comes in at the end. There's one in the middle of it. The Cobb girl -- Wenna, six years old, Pig-Tallow Lane in the Hook. The fever season, two years back, before there was anyone here who could fetch the maester in time. By the time someone was found to carry the call, it was a burial, not a call. The slow way kept its principles whole for the eleven days it took her to die. She's the first name I count."

The name landed and stayed landed. A child, a lane, a fever two seasons gone, before my coverage had been here to send anyone running -- the first of them, the one the slow way had left in the ground and I had not, which is the whole reason the counting is mine to do. She was not a category. Saying her exactly was the entire cost of keeping the count honest. And under her, an older shape I did not look straight at: a smaller door that had stayed shut on someone I could not reach in time, the same arithmetic in another tongue.

His hands went still on his knees.

Two accountings stood there in the cold, and neither was paid. His slow way carried a body it could not answer for. Mine carried a coverage that answered for that body and bent the crooked thing further at its own rate to do it -- and what the standing-there cost me was the clean knowledge of the road I had chosen not to take, which I let cost that much and said nothing of.

"I know the slow way has a cost," he said. "I'll not pretend I don't. You've named it, and I'll carry the name. I've buried by the slow way before now. What I can't do is the other thing -- take the quick way and watch the debt go on growing past where I can see the end of it. I don't know where it stops. That's the truth of it. I haven't an answer that makes your dead girl breathe. I've only the one I can live beside."

My feet had started to drift toward the lane, and I set the heel back down instead, the weight pressing into the cobble's edge. The natural thing was to go, and I declined it once more. He read the staying the way he had read the planting -- two people who had taken each other's measure honestly and settled nothing, which was the one thing the architecture I ran was built to prevent and could never have made.

Then I left, before a resolution that was not coming. The leaving was its own kind of choosing: I left the argument standing, not closed, and I knew I was leaving it standing the way I knew the edge of how far the flies would reach. This was the road I was not taking, and the knowing was not the same as the taking. I went out by the cross-lane mouth, where his sight could not follow me.

Behind me the cold settled back into the corner stone where it had held all morning. I cleared the Hook and went up into the rest of the day.


CHAPTER 8

The argument the septon had left in my hand had not resolved, and I had not stopped carrying it. I walked the circuit the way I walked it every morning, and the walking did not put the argument down.

  

I came up into the lane-junction at the Rushwick margin, where the hill's stone skirt cuts off the north view -- broken wall on one side, open lane on the other, the corner I always start from because it tells me where I am before anything else does. I sent the flies ahead to the chandler's corner and they came back with nothing I had not already expected: the chandler at his door, two carts, the lane running ordinary.

The boy was at the water-point. He stood with his weight set on one foot, the way a boy stands when he has been told to hold a spot and means to hold it. The old woman with the basket stood at the lane-mouth with her body turned outward, facing down the open lane -- the angle of her saying a thing she would never have agreed she was saying. The cobbler's apprentice was the third, by the chandler's door, and he dropped his eyes as I came on.

I followed the boy's line of attention, then the woman's, then the apprentice's -- three reads taken without breaking my step. Their sightlines crossed and overlapped, and I saw the shape of it: Oswyn's watchers, set at the three approaches to the lower Hook, covering ground I had already mapped for my own cover. His net and mine lay over the same stone. Mine he could not see; his I could fold into mine without asking.

I rested my hand on the junction-stone and held it there, the cold of it flat against my palm, and I did not take the next step yet. I sent the flies down the three corridors his watchers were minding -- the three lanes my insects did not crowd, where his people saw and I did not. I let the flies fill those corridors and learn them.

His watchers I left out of the count. I could have folded the boy and the woman and the apprentice in as fixed points and let them carry their corners for me. I did not. I kept them outside the thing I was building, the way you keep your hand off a debt you have decided not to take on -- and I knew, the way you know a thing you will not say, that keeping them out was a finer distinction than it felt like, and that I had taken the corridors anyway.

I let the breath I had been holding go out, long, into the lane-air. My shoulder came down. I took the next step.

  

The apprentice brought the Jarvis packet up to the feed-station mid-afternoon -- the usual hand, the usual hour, the room around it gone quiet the way it always goes quiet. I broke the wax with my thumb, the small crack of it loud in the still room, and the pages came open under my hand.

I read the handler-rotation down its column: names and dates in the clipped possessive that scheduling notes use, who answers to whom, this week and the next. Below the rotation I found the gap I had clocked that morning -- the morning's hole in my cover, written down here where I had known it would be, no surprise in finding it.

The Aemond name sat in the rotation-row like any other entry. Aemond Targaryen -- twelve, Vhagar's rider since Driftmark, the boy Otto's people reach for when a thing needs a body behind it. A name in a handler-rotation is the apparatus moving the largest dragon in the sky, written down the way you write down a cart-schedule. I read it once. I did not let it weigh more on the page than it weighed. It was not in my cover, and I left it where it lay.

I set the stylus down. My hand stayed flat on the worked wood of the desk a beat longer than the work asked.

I read the bread-price report from the lower Blackwater approaches next, the numbers tracking near where I had expected them, my finger going down the page entry by entry. I held my eye on the last line a moment past reading it -- the edge of the thing I had just learned still sitting there, lit low, where before there had been nothing lit at all.

  

I came back into the Hook ward in the evening, the lane-light gone to dusk, the water-point lit low and the stones past the chandler's corner already deep in shadow. The flies found Meryn Cobb where she always was at this hour. They found Oswyn at the water-point. They found a third man at his shoulder before I came round the corner to see him.

He stood tall enough that his shoulder set above Oswyn's, solid through the chest, his attention turned not on me but down the lane the way Oswyn was facing -- a man stopped between one errand and the next, not yet moving again. I had carried his face for weeks without a name to set on it.

Oswyn shifted his weight as I came near and spoke past me, into the lane, not toward me and not away:

"Corwick. Runs errands for someone above his station. Up the hill twice this month."

He left his weight on his back foot past the last word and said no more of it. He did not know he had handed me anything. To him it was a man he had placed in a category, mentioned and let go.

To me the name dropped into the empty place I had been holding since the water-point pass -- the face I had been carrying now had Corwick fixed to it, and a pattern with it: above his station, twice up the hill. My step came up short, the heel landing a beat early, the rhythm broken once and then taken up again. I did not ask who up the hill. I did not need to ask tonight.

I walked the rest of the circuit, my line through the ward closing out at the standard corners, the evening pass running to its time.

When I came back past the water-point the boy was gone. The stone he had stood on all month was bare, the angle his weight had made not there to read anymore -- his corner sitting now inside the cover I had set down that afternoon, the approach minded the same as ever, only minded by me. Oswyn did not know that either. He was still talking when I left.


CHAPTER 9

I knew the stitch-maker's days now the way I knew the lanes. Wren kept the Hook's children fed and clothed, and the flies had been crossing her street since the boundary moved three weeks back, and I had not once told them to. They had learned her without my asking -- the door she opened, the corner where she stopped, the one block where she walked alone. I knew her the way I knew which gate took the morning carts and which one took the evening ones. That was the part I did not look at straight. The courier I had carried without a name had one now; I had not yet learned whose errands he ran.

  

I entered the lane south of the Hook, and for a moment the morning let me be nothing else. I pressed my boot to the lane-stone and the cold came up through the sole, and for the length of that step I was only a body standing in a cold lane, which is close to the last thing the morning lets me be. The bay-damp had beaded on the stone where the sun had not yet reached, a thin cold film on the dry grey.

Then I sent the flies down the stitch-shop lane, low along the wall the way I send them every morning now, and the shop door opened at the lane-mouth and Wren came out. She walked the lane toward the water-sellers' row -- the same lane, the same hour -- and stopped at the bread-seller's corner the way she stopped there yesterday and the day before. I knew the whole of it before she reached the corner: the door, the corner, the one block past the corner where the lane has a single entrance and she walks it alone.

The cold had stiffened my fingers, and I held still against the wall and let the flies bring her to me anyway. There was nothing in how I stood that marked this one as Wren. I gave the route the same flat attention I'd give a supply cart or a gate. That was the trouble. A gate I watch because it is a gate. I had started watching her the same way, and I had not decided to.

When I set the ward-summary for Otto's man that night, I put the new lanes in it -- the boundary south of the Hook, the lane-density, the stretch the flies now cover. I left out where Wren went, and when, and the block where she was alone. The notes took everything they always take. The one thing I kept back, I did not write down anywhere I would have to call it kept back.

  

That evening I walked the second circuit out along the Dragonpit margin, where the lanes run south toward the hill and the air goes cool before the lower wards do. A supply cart waited on the road below the lower gate -- the Dragonpit's court-margin side, under Rhaenys's Hill, where the heir's business passes and not the Queen's. Not a Green gate. A second man stood at the stone-post by the side exit.

The other was Corwick.

He stood where a man at that hour had no errand to stand -- wrong ward, wrong hour. The cold had settled on my shoulders where I held still at the edge of the feed, and I went still inside it too as his body turned. He faced the second man and squared his shoulders, the set of a man who has handed something over and is waiting to be told it landed. I could not see the second man's face, or what had changed hands, or whether anything had.

But I knew the hour, and I knew the gate backed onto a household I'd marked Black since the spring, and the squared shoulders told me the rest. Not a name -- a direction. I read the gate's loyalty the way you read a thing already written, from further off than anyone standing in that lane could have stood. It cost me almost nothing to read, and I let it cost me almost nothing.

I kept it to myself. The morning's packet would carry the ward-summary the way it always did, and Corwick's visit to the lower gate would not be in it. I had seen it. I did not pass it on. I finished the circuit and came in.

  

I sat at the feed-station to close the day. The packet for Otto's channel held what it held -- the coverage south of the Hook, the new lane figures, two scraps off the Rushwick margin. I folded it and brought the seal down at the weight I'd already settled my hand to, and the wax took the only shape the channel would ever see. To the left of it lay the ward-report I'd set aside.

What I kept, I kept in my own head, where the flies put the city down for me and Otto's men never look. Wren's lane was there -- the hour, the route, the block where the entrance is unwatched. Corwick's lower gate was there beside it, the same evening, the same quiet shelf I take everything from. Both of them sat in the same place the sealed packet drew from and did not draw from, and I did not turn either one over again.

The ward went quiet. The flies ran their lanes. The seal cooled under my thumb, soft to firm, and dried.


CHAPTER 10

For nine circuits I had carried Corwick in the flies and never sent him up the line. I knew the corridor he worked and where it emptied, the way he stood, the lower gate where his errands faced the wrong way for the Queen's business. A whole man learned circuit by circuit and kept back -- the way I kept the stitch-maker's girl back, the way the arrangement itself had stayed back, never once said out loud. I had told myself the keeping was a choice I went on making each morning. The packet came at the standard hour, in the standard channel, and it would ask me whether that was still true.

  
Jarvis set the packet on the morning stone and put his thumb to the wax, and Otto's seal was under it, and that was the whole of the meeting -- his hands, the seal, the folded weight of the thing on the cold surface, no word passing that I would have had to answer. The text inside did not ask me anything. It told me, and what it told had the weight of a thing already shut: the arrangement named plainly now, an ongoing function, my work the lower city's eyes carried up the line, and the protection over Sera continuing as what the function was owed against. There was no version of any sentence in it I could fold back into a question. I had been carrying a kind of deniability through every prior errand -- I could still tell myself I chose each delivery as it came -- and these words spent it.

The bay-cold had come in overnight and sat on the morning stone under my hand, and I was a woman standing in a cold place reading a page. I folded the packet along its crease and let the folding be what it was, a thing taken and a thing kept. Then I let my breath out over the page without turning the page over in my mind, and the road outside ran the way it ran.

Jarvis set a second sheet beside the first, and it was a description, not a name. Otto's people had the silhouette already -- the shape of a man who moved the wrong faction's goods along the lower-gate road -- but they did not have the man. I had the man. I had carried him for months in the part of the flies that never went to Jarvis: the corridor and where it emptied, the way he held himself, the gate that backed the wrong side, all of it laid down circuit by circuit until he was as fixed in me as the stone-post he set his shoulder to each pass, the way I had watched him square against it nine times running.

I turned the months into the channel's flat lines -- name, the run of his errands, the gate, the contact I had inferred -- and the writing was the one act I could not take back, the stylus going down with a small sound in the morning quiet. That was the first thing. Sending it was the second, and separate: I put the sheet up the line, the giving its own motion, apart from the making. The wax cooled over the seal, and the channel was shut on Corwick and on no other name.

  
Two days on, the supply cart climbed the lower-gate road the way it climbed it every cold morning, its smell of animal and cargo and wet wood reaching the flies before the cart did. Corwick should have been on the corridor by then -- he had walked it every second or third pass since I first picked him up at the Rushwick margin, his shape as steady on the road as the cart, steady enough that I had stopped counting it.

The corridor-mouth stood empty where his shape ordinarily stood. Not gone the way a man is gone who has finished his errand -- the empty had an edge to it, the shape of something that had already happened somewhere I could not reach. The stone-post still squared the side-exit. A machinery this old can empty a corridor of one body and leave the stone unmarked, and it had: the corridor was a way out that the road need not notice, and it had taken him out of it.

Within two passes the flies brought back a Gold Cloak pair -- the City Watch, the crown's men -- standing at the junction where his corridor emptied into the road. Not passing through. Posted, at rest, the way men stand who have been set there after a thing is done and not before. I sent the flies across the corridor and they came back with the same. The work had been done inside two passes of my sending the sheet, and the war's logic had moved through my own flies without my asking it to and without my meaning it. The bay-cold pressed the lower road, and its weight on me was a thing I could hold.

  
At the day's end I sat at the feed-station. I kept the accounting in my head the way I always kept it -- the gain set against the cost. Sera's protection continuing, the arrangement named, the channel working -- and against it Corwick, learned across months, held back through nine circuits, taken inside two days of the line I sent.

I named him to myself: Corwick. Not a shape on the road, not a pattern in the flies -- the man. The feed-station stone cooled under my wrist while I did it. He faced the lower-gate in the flies as he had faced it for me a hundred times, and he crossed the errand-corridor in them -- the same crossing the flies had carried back to me nine passes running, one more pass of a man I had kept without his leave. The architecture that does this to bodies it never asked had done it again, and this time through my hand.

I shut the ledger in my head and did not let it open again -- the gain column right, the cost column right, the thing complete, and complete was the only way I let myself leave it. I pressed my hand flat to the stone. CHAPTER 11

The arrangement had a name now -- a thing written into the channel, owed against the quiet I kept over Sera -- and naming it had changed nothing about how the mornings ran. The merchant's shop sat on the Hook's south end with no relation I could see to any of it. This morning he drew back the narrow worktable at the rear of the shop in the low rushlight, and that small act of opening was the only thing it seemed to be, until a messenger crossed his threshold at an hour no order of cloth is placed. The heat reached the worktable before any of it was meant to reach me. The ash settled in the iron dish and the warmth sat there after, the way warmth sits in a thing that has done its one job. The thread that had run through that shop ran to Dragonstone: the island seat across the bay where Rhaenyra keeps her court and the heir's faction holds, half a day's water from the city. I tied the packet shut. The older question -- the one I used to hold an account like this against before I sent it on -- did not come to stand in the way of any of the three motions. The packet was tied before there was a place in the order of it for the question to be.

CHAPTER 12

One debt, one mechanism, the same act settling both, and no name set down to carry it. I closed it and lifted my hand off the page and held it there a moment before I let it go.

The last open gap in the Flea Bottom coverage closed under them. With it closed, I read all five wards at once -- every ward and the lanes between them, held simultaneous and whole, the first time I had ever carried the whole city in one piece. I could not pull a single node of it without leaving a shape the rest would not hold. I read the Flea Bottom approaches last, the place I had first come to cover, every way into it mapped now and held at full scale.

Then I weighed it the way I always weighed it -- the gain set against the cost, the harm I stopped against the harm I caused, the whole of what I had built against the thing it was starting to look like. And reaching past any name I had for the architecture, I landed on the older word. Not a word for the thing in front of me but the shape of the thing I did when the world was ending, the one this rhymed with. It was there for one count. I held it that long and no longer. I pushed it back down and ran the count on past it -- the gain, the cost, the harm-prevention, the plain operational need of the thing -- and the word was not in any of that, because none of it required the word, and the gap between what I did then and what I did now was real, and the word did not help the count run.

Then I wrote the cost of it down -- the threshold crossed, the scope past walking back -- in the same flat hand as every line above it. The end-of-day light had gone off the surface to dimming. I left the word out of the entry. The entry held what happened: the city carried whole, the count finished, the harm-prevention intact, and the cost set down at its full weight. The insects ran on at full deployment, the way they would run for every circuit after this one -- the coverage complete, the architecture load-bearing, the gap east of the water-gate the one place the map did not go.


CHAPTER 13

In the weeks since I began routing what my insects showed me through Jarvis to Otto Hightower's people, I had handed up the kind of intelligence the arrangement paid for. Among it, at one delivery, was a list of ward-elders in the lower precinct whose lane-contacts my pattern-reading had marked as Black-faction-adjacent. I handed it over in plain words and did not watch where it went. This week I watched.

  

I kept a blowfly on the crate-ledge at the edge of the loading platform, low and back, where the salt-fish brine had thickened the morning air into something I could taste through it, and I held it there while the household agent set himself at the trestle table across from the supplier. He was a Green man, the agent, running a Green household's accounts, and he kept the table between himself and the salt-fish supplier the way a man keeps a counter between himself and a thing he means to handle. The supplier faced him and said nothing I needed to hear. The boy did the rest of the work. Eleven years old, maybe, the supplier's son, and he carried the wrapped crates up onto the platform with his eyes on the wood the whole time, because he had already learned that the looking was the part that cost.

The agent ran the tally. I watched him do it -- his finger going down the figures, the margin pared and pared again, a copper-penny shaving off a load of salt fish that could not have carried more than a few coppers of profit to begin with. A margin that thin was not the point. A man does not work that hard over a few coppers unless the coppers are only the tool and the working is the thing he wants. He was confirming something. The supply ran where it ran and bent where it was told to bend, and the agent already knew this, and the tally was how he made the supplier know that he knew it.

The supplier agreed to the cut. The account closed, and the agent's shoulders dropped -- not slumped, not tired, but let down into him the way weight settles when a thing is finished and finished the way it was wanted. I had the words for him already. What the drop of his shoulders added was only his body confirming a thing that had never been in question. The boy picked up the last empty crate and turned it to stack it, eyes still on the wood. I let the blowfly go.

  

Four days later I held a fly in the ceiling corner of a rented back room, up under the eave-timber of a chandler's house where the Hook frayed out into the broader ward network, and I read the proceeding from above. The Green-faction clerk had set a document at the edge of the table before any of it began -- laid it down flat, squared it, stepped back. The man they brought in took the chair. Aldric, a ward-elder of the lower precinct, one of the men whose name had been on my list, brought up on a charge of receiving stolen cord.

The magistrate began writing on his form before Aldric had finished speaking. I watched the stylus move ahead of the man's mouth -- the verdict going down onto the paper while the answer was still being given, the questions asked in a tone that did not want answers because the answers were already there on the page. Aldric lifted the cord up off the table to show it, holding it out the way a man holds out an object he believes will explain him if he only lifts it high enough. The cord was not what had brought him here. I knew which thing had.

The magistrate set the cord aside without looking at it and picked up the document the clerk had squared at the table's edge. It came up into his hand, and he read down it, and the verdict already on his form took its weight from the page he was holding. The cord was the pretext. The list was the engine. The document in his hand was the one I had written out in plain words and handed to Jarvis -- my own flat sentences about who walked which lanes, lifted now by another man's hand to decide what to do with a person.

I held the connection where it sat. His name on my list, his hands flat on the table now -- I held the two together and did not move them across into the column where that kind of entry goes, did not open that page at all, only kept them in the same look. Across the room Aldric gripped the table edge, both hands gone white at the knuckle, gripping it the way a man grips a rail when the floor has done something the floor is not supposed to do -- not understanding it, only knowing it had happened and was happening to him. I held the fly steady in the corner and held the read to the end.

  

That evening I stopped on the lane.

It was a lane I had walked five hundred times, and I stopped in the middle of it, mid-step, on ordinary stone in an ordinary evening with the tallow-smoke drifting across the open mouth of the street from somebody's rushlight. I caught the smell of it and held still. The two of them came up together and stood side by side in my mind -- the agent's shoulders going down at the account-close, the magistrate's hand lifting my paper to seal a verdict already written -- and a word arrived to meet them. The word was contempt. Not resentment. Resentment is diffuse; it files itself away as temperament and stays there. This was a judgment about quality, and the evidence was already counted and already true: the copper margin worked like a tool, the form written before the answer, the ward-elder list I had handed over turned and used the way it was always going to be used. I had felt the texture of this since the first time the resentment found something to take hold of. The word came now and it stood outward, against the thing, and under it sat the colder knowing that had always sat there -- that the people the apparatus spends are spent the way a coming fire spends what stands in its way, as weather, never as anyone the keepers of it ever count.

The breath went out and my weight settled back onto the heel that had stopped. I set my jaw and held the word inside it, carried to no channel, set down on no page where Otto's people would ever find it.

Then I walked on. The lane ahead was the lane behind, the same before the word and the same after it. The contempt did not bend the route by a single stone. It was a finding, and I knew what gets done with a finding, which is nothing -- you hold it, you walk on under it. A finding, not a decision.

  

Two days later Halvard was at the water-trough at the lower end of the Hook, where the chandler's row gave out onto the open ground before the fishmongery, filling a skin for somebody -- water-carrying on another man's behalf, the way the septons did, in his ordinary round and not lying in wait for me at all. I came up to the trough from the lane with the stone cool and dry under my hand and the trough-rim cooler where I reached it, and he straightened when he saw me, and he spoke.

"They took up Aldric. You'll have heard. I knew him -- knew the lanes he walked and who he talked to on them, and it was not what they put to him. He carried cord. He carried word between two old women who can't walk the hill themselves. That was the worst of him, and they made it the worst of something else. Somebody decided what he was from a long way off, from a street they've never stood in, and they got it wrong. A man who's never set foot in the Hook can't tell you who's a danger in it. He can write a name down. The name is enough for them."

The long way off was me. He did not know he was holding up a mirror.

"It won't be slower because you ask it to be," I said. "It won't be gentler, or pick more carefully whose name it writes down. You can be slower than it is. The people it's already deciding on can't wait for you to reach them. I've counted what your way costs and what mine costs, all the way down. Mine keeps more of them alive. There's no cleaner way that holds the same line for the same price. I know there isn't. I went looking for one."

It was the same answer I had given at this trough before, in other weeks, against the same slower voice that named the wrong and had no colder method to put in the wrong's place. Nothing in the saying of it had ever bent my route, which was why I did not wait for the end of it.

"You can know a street five hundred times over and never know what it weighs to be the name on the page," he said. "I've buried men a wiser hand wrote off. I'll bury Aldric too, like as not. And I'll not have the comfort of telling myself it had to be him."

I left the trough while he was still speaking and crossed past its edge into the open lane-mouth ahead.

I walked the route. My feet stayed on the round, one stone and the next, the way they went every day, and the gap his words would have opened was a gap I did not turn toward -- and the not-turning had the shape of walking, had no shape but that, my body going on along the round it always went.

Behind me Halvard worked the skin under the trough-spout and let it fill, the sound of the water rising and then closing off as the skin came full, and he set it on his shoulder for the next house. His answer went on existing where I had left it, unanswered, in the mouth of a man who did not know whose hand he was naming, and he carried it on into his round the way he carried the water, and neither of us stopped.

CHAPTER 15

I had kept the eastern boundary eleven months -- the ground west of the water-gate read clean by the flies, the ground east of it left blank on purpose, the gap held as carefully as anything I covered. The arrangement still ran. What I gathered went up through Jarvis to Otto's people, and the quiet on Sera's question was bought with it. This morning the passage-adjacent ward gave me a boy.

He reset his front foot and ran the angle again, and the older man yielded the shoulder-line to him; the correction landed in the master-at-arms' frame, not the boy's. He fixed the world and waited for it to come to his fixing, and it came. I eased the flies down to a slower scan. Then he ran the same correction a second time, and the second fit the first -- one I might have read wrong, two was a finding, and the finding matched a thing I had already decided about the people he was being shaped to stand among. I sent none of him anywhere.

The master-at-arms walked him up the passage-slope, and the flies lost him behind the stone. What came next was not something I saw. The flies on the arch began to judder in warm air, and the warmth was the dragon -- Vhagar, settled on the Red Keep's eastern slope, too large to be anywhere without the stone giving back her heat. The flies could not see her. They strained their wings against air that had thickened, and through them I felt the weight of her at a distance, off the warmth alone. Two months of this had taught me the difference: bay-warmth rising off the morning stone, and hill-warmth with a load behind it. The eastern slope carried her heat down to the gutter, and the warm stone pushed it into the small bodies clustered there -- I felt it the way you feel a hand held near a wall in the dark, the stone giving back a heat it had not had an hour before. I named it: Vhagar, resting behind the hill. I marked it a site-condition and wrote nothing of what it meant -- that the apparatus had found my work on its own last week, from the far side, and a thing found from both ends does not come apart again. The same warmth that told me where I stood was the one that had closed the way out. Then it faded from the gutter.

The flies at the eastern fringe carried that warmth worst, and their images blurred in it; at the boundary they lost their resolution altogether. I had run blurred coverage before and knew the look of it. What I did not know was what the blur would show me. East of the water-gate the lanes gave me nothing -- not unclear, empty. I had left that ground uncovered eleven months on purpose, the one place I would not send the flies, because Wren moved there. The emptiness was a choice, not a failure of reach, and against the blurred fringe it read clean -- cleaner than the noise around it, the way silence reads loud in a room full of small sounds. The clean emptiness took an edge. The blur on every side gave it a border, and the border was person-sized: the clearing I had kept blank all those months, showing itself to me for the first time as a shape. Somewhere inside it Wren walked the lanes, unseen, the way I had built those lanes to keep her. My hand stopped over the stylus and held there. I let one breath out above the record, the only sound in the room. Then I wrote eastern-boundary, backwash-effect, and set down no name for what it had looked like.

The flies settled back at the passage-arch gutter, and the blur cleared at the eastern fringe; the hill-warmth fell off toward the morning baseline, and the boundary went back to what it had been -- clean coverage west, clean blank east, the two stone edges holding. The shape was gone. It had lasted as long as the warmth and no longer. I closed the morning circuit and walked back through what the morning had given me, one read at a time. A boy already shaped like the thing he would become. The sapphire holding flat light. The master-at-arms yielding without being asked. A weight behind the stone that meant a dragon. And -- for as long as the fringe stayed blurred -- a gap with a person's shape to it. I wrote the final notation in my record. Aemond's coming changed nothing I was doing; I was not going to hand a patron a twelve-year-old at swordwork. I closed the record and pulled the flies back to routine. The blank ground had been blank eleven months. What had changed was that I had seen its shape now -- a deliberate thing, a stretch of ground I read nothing of so that the one who moved through it moved unseen, held with the same care I gave the ground I did cover. My hand crossed the line where a name would go and found the line below it without catching. I lifted the stylus past it, set no name down, and ran the afternoon circuit. The arrangement went on.


CHAPTER 16

The storehouse threw its shadow across the cleared ground, and the corner was the corner it always was. The reed-mat for the dying lay rolled against the wall, no one on it today. Two crates stood stacked at the wall's foot, the ones the kneelers used for benches. An old man knelt at one of them and straightened his back as I came on; a woman stood near the wall with a child who had stopped growing, and the child pressed itself into her side. I came in on the diagonal I always came in on -- west-precinct coverage, the corner one more point on the pass. I was not there for Halvard.

The flies on the eaves gave me the three bodies and the two lanes beside them before I had crossed half the ground. I had everyone placed while the old man was still finding his back. Then Halvard finished whatever low thing he had been saying and lifted his head, and the morning became the thing it became.

He fell into step with me. Not in front, not blocking -- beside me, matched to my pace, walking the thing he had come to say at the speed I walked, where I could not set it down and move past it.

"The ones who lived through the autumn," he said. "The woman with the chest-complaint in Muggers' Row. The boy from the fishmonger's lane -- he's still on it, still gutting fish, I saw him a sennight past. I'll not claim them. I don't know what you did or didn't do, and I'm not asking. I've not counted them, either. It is not my count to run. The count I run is what it costs them, after. What they understand about how they survived. Some of them understand things about this precinct that I did not think anyone who arrived the way you arrived would come to know."

It was clean and it was aimed at the wrong place. It answered the contempt and not the continuing, and the continuing was older than the contempt; I had had the answer to this since the third time he found me at this corner, the words different each time and what they came down to never different at all.

The rendering-yard fence ran along the clearing's far edge -- the edge of the corner and the way out of it. The old man got to his feet and planted himself near the fence, a body where a body would be. I put my hand to the fence-rail and pressed. The iron held the cold the way the stone under it held the cold, and it took my weight while I did the sum I did not need to do aloud: what the place I held was worth to me now, against what it cost me to hold it. The cost had moved. Not far enough to make me do anything different -- only far enough to notice. It was worth less to hold than it cost. I held it.

Halvard stopped. He turned and faced me, his weight even on both feet, his hands open at his sides. I closed my hand on the rail.

"I am not asking you to do differently," he said. "I've no better way to give you, and I'll not pretend I have. I am asking you to know what you're doing. That's the whole of it."

It was the only honest thing he had said, and it changed nothing. Knowing what I did had never been the thing that stopped me. I let the rail go.

  

I stopped. Not a pause before answering -- a stop, my feet settling even on the dirt, the way a person stands still when they have finished weighing a thing and the weight has come down. There was no answer that was also a continuing, and so there was no answer.

Through the flies on the eaves I could feel him standing where he stood, the particular stillness of a man who has said his thing and waits to see what it does. He was close enough that I had all of it -- the patience in him, the not-pushing. I could see what I would say if I were going to say it. I did not say it. Not because I had nothing to say, but because saying it would mean treating the argument as a live one, and I had already settled that it was not. The contempt and the continuing were one fact, because the continuing came first: I had been running this before I had a word for what I thought of the people it kept alive, and the word, when it came, changed nothing about the route.

He had not moved. He was in the middle of having said his piece, still waiting.

I turned. Not away from the argument -- away from the man, which is a different thing. I walked back across the cleared ground toward the rendering-yard approach, not hurrying, the same pace I had come in with. The reed-mat lay rolled against the wall as I passed it. The old man stood facing the clearing-edge. I reached the rendering-yard fence at the gap.

He kept his place in the cleared ground behind me. He did not call after me -- he was not the kind of man who calls after people, and I had known that about him without knowing I knew it. The flies held him there, standing, until the angle carried him to the edge of what they could give me and then past it. I brought them back to coverage and ran the next segment of the circuit.


CHAPTER 17

The sheet was under the trough-stone where it always was, the agreed mark scratched on the third column. I lifted it and unfolded it against the low grey light coming flat off the waterfront, the kind of end-of-day light the wall-shadow had already begun to eat. The query sat third from the bottom. A height, a gait, six points of observation across eleven days -- a woman who walked the gap east of the water gate on a regular interval, seen twice near my own ward-contacts. They wanted her named. The sheet carried no name of its own. The pattern was the whole of it.

I went still. The flies handed the gait back before I had decided to ask them for it -- the small hold on the second step that came off the scar across her left ankle, the interval that ran the stitch-house rotation. I knew her. I had known her for months, reading that gap in the lane and letting it stand nowhere a record would hold it, the one figure I walked around on purpose and never wrote down. Now the question had come in from the far side of the channel, and the answer was already in my hand. I folded the sheet back into my coat and kept my hand flat on the trough-stone and did not shift my weight toward the next leg of the circuit. The question would be there whether or not I answered it. That was the part that did not wait.

  

I crossed the Hook's southern edge in the dark with the sheet folded against my chest. The stone underfoot had gone to its night-cool -- not the day's warmth, not quite wet -- and I let myself think it the whole way out. If I sent her name back through the channel, she became a woman in their picture: a smallfolk woman who moved through a watched gap on a pattern someone had already noticed. Named meant findable. Findable meant usable. I walked the thought to its end and there was nothing past usable.

So I turned the other way around it. I could put a different figure through the gap instead -- write the movements onto some ward-elder whose own passage through that corridor made the reading plain, close the question with a man who belonged there. That choice would keep her safe. It would also set a false line in their record. It would hang those steps on a person who had never taken them, a man who had not chosen this and would not know he carried it.

That was the place it cracked. Not a feeling I could have named -- only the thing itself, seen plainly: the shape of what I was about to do. I held both choices open and kept walking, the ward gone down to a far ember-glow and the odd footfall, the kind of quiet that lets you hear yourself think. Her gait surfaced again in the flies, unasked, just the particular weight of a particular step. I set the screen-choice down beside the work I had built once and named the thing I would never do again, and the two were the same shape -- smaller now, the size of one corridor, but the same. I did not let myself look the rest of the way into it. I walked on past it and held it at the edge.

  

Before the Hook turned north I had decided. The thinking had run long enough, and there was no version of it where letting her surface cost less than the alternative. I climbed to the room I rent above the rendering works, three coppers a week, and the cold tallow smell sat in the stone the way it always did once the day's render was done. I pulled the coverage log out onto the working surface -- my own hand, a running tally of movement and light, no names, because I never wrote names -- and found the eleven-day cluster that matched her.

Elder Norren was a tanner's-ward contact whose own passage through that corridor, twice a week, I already had on record. Legitimate. His own. I opened his entry.

I wrote the first line. A presence in the corridor, the date, set under the name of a man who had not been in it. Then two more, dated backward, calibrated to the interval they had already flagged -- supplementary observations, the kind that would read like a gap that had quietly healed. The log that had never carried a name now carried one I had put there myself. I set the pen down. Extraction, if it ever came, would mean unwinding what I had just written; the false lines were load-bearing now, part of everything that held me in place. The protection and the trap were the same three lines. I read Norren's name where her movements should have been and saw the shape of it whole -- the thing I had carried the refusal of all the way into this body, smaller in count, identical in kind. I folded the segment for the dead drop and left the log open on the surface, the entry unclosed, and went out.

  

It was three days before the dead drop came back quiet. No follow-up. The channel handed back nothing, and the question shut against Norren's name -- his movements logged, the gap filled, the safety I had bought and the price I had paid arriving as one and the same thing. She crossed the corridor that morning the way she always had, and the ward-read gave me the second-step hesitation again, and now there was a record in their picture that swore the step belonged to a man who had never run it.

I lifted the pen off the cost-ledger and did not bring it down. The column for this stayed open, the slot blank, and I kept the count of it in my head, where no page would hold it and nothing could route it anywhere it might surface. The false line would stay in the corridor's log under Norren's name and gather every observation after it, for as long as the record stood. I had kept her safe the same way I had once built the thing I came here to set down -- not the scale, not the count of bodies, only the shape of the act: moving a person through a record without their knowing, bending what the watchers read, to get the outcome I wanted. I left the column blank. Both of those were true at once.


CHAPTER 18

The wards are mapped now, and the channel runs both ways. The Hand-who-was still works through Jarvis: he takes my counting and pays for it by keeping Sera's parentage quiet, and that holds only as long as I keep delivering. East of the water-gate there is one lane I have never covered, because it is Wren's, and Wren still moves through it screened -- her passage written into the record under Norren's name, a man with a real reason to be there. And this, now, is the call on the arrangement at its full agreed scale: not a ward, not a cluster, but all of it.

  

Before dawn I lifted the bundle off the floor, the paper cold and stiff in the dark, and read the note that had come folded into it. It was written in the margin cipher I had set up with Jarvis in the third month, which I used only when the thing I was sending had come from above his tier -- so this had not started with Jarvis. It had only passed through his hands. What it asked for was not a question. Full coverage. Every ward, every channel, every lane in my map open at once, and held that way for a fortnight, so the whole of the lower city would be clear and current before a particular document moved through the Small Council.

I read it again. The note named the reason the way these men always named a reason, which was to set it just out of reach and let me close the distance: the King's health had stopped being a matter of how long and become a matter of when, and the document could not move until the picture under it was complete. There was no version of the arrangement where I refused this. I had agreed to it at the price I agreed to it, and this was the price.

So I folded the bundle and set it back on the floor without answering, and stood over it with my weight even on both feet. I let myself name the size of the thing it asked for. It was a size I had held in my hand before. I left the answer for the morning, and the morning was already coming.

  

Before first light I opened the bottlefly routes under the Fishmonger Gate, and sent the moths up through the chandler quarter, and reached the flies out along the outer-gate corridors where the stone goes grey before the dawn does. Not gate by gate. The one lane I left dark was the eastern one, because that lane is Wren, and Wren crossed it the way she always crosses it -- the half-step hitch at the scarred ankle, her passage written under Norren's name -- while every lane around her ran thick with bugs. The blank was not a weakness in the thing. It was the most careful part of it.

The moths in the chandler quarter came back fouled: a smelt-fire somewhere below them, bleeding a low acrid haze into what they could tell me. I drew the flies around it and read past it. The friction was real and it stopped nothing.

This time I did not feed in the lanes one cluster at a time, the way I had run it for months. I opened every lane of the city at once. And the bodies I held crossed the number I had set for myself a long time ago, in a different city, for reasons that had not held -- and I held more of them now than I had ever held here, all of them at once, the whole place gone quiet and legible under me the way it had once gone quiet under me before. The bundle stayed folded on the floor where I had left it. I held the city through the fortnight, and did not stop the count, and did not look too long at the shape of having reached that number again.

  

Six days of it, at full density, and what came back was the court entire -- not pieces of it but the machine itself, turning.

A groom shouldering a saddlebag at an hour that did not match the tilting yard. A maid who was not only a maid, her odd corridor running to three contacts, two of them Green and one not. A knight whose left hand closed on the pommel in a way the tilting yard does not teach -- a grip for fighting in a small closed room. A septa crossing the outer court each morning on a timing I knew from Flea Bottom, the exact interval of a ward-elder's handoff: the same machine as the wards below, only the cloth was finer.

The four of them read as one thing. I held the court the way I held the streets, every body doing its work for people who cared as little for the smallfolk under them as the smallfolk care for the stars. I set the day's reading aside and took up the next day's and set that aside the same way. The flies brought me the whole court at once, every part of it turning, and I read it without it ever reading me -- I was its instrument, holding it up to the light for the faction that wanted it held, and despising every body in it, and there was nothing in the despising that opened a door. I set the seventh day's reading aside exactly as I had set aside the first. That sameness was the whole of it.

  

On the ninth day I carried the compiled fortnight to Jarvis. I left it in the trough in three drops across a day and a night -- the corridors, the windows, the contact intervals, each one timed and named, the way he had asked for it -- and he lifted it clear. The document the Greens were moving needed a particular window open in the Small Council on a particular day, and my fortnight was what told them the window stood clear.

Two days after that I lifted the counter-bundle from the trough and read the single line in the margin cipher. The document had moved. The legitimacy question Sera's exposure had been held against was answered, for now, in the Greens' favour. The Small Council had passed it through the window I named, and the succession channel was the Greens' now, and it would not come back. I left the line out of my own record. The thing was true whether I wrote it down or not, and the city was still running thick with bugs while I did not write it.

  

On the fourteenth day I pulled the flies back ward by ward, the way I had built it -- the ward-elder routes first, the eastern lane last and still dark, held that way the whole fortnight under a name I had set down myself that had never been the true one. The moths in the chandler quarter settled back into the eaves, two weeks of held coverage thinning down into the buildings the bugs live in.

Then I counted what the fortnight had cost and what it had bought. The qualifier I had let myself keep -- that what I did here was calibrated, careful, less than what I had once done -- was gone, and there was no honest way to set it down again. I had run a network at this size before, before this city, before any of this was a thing I built to make up for the last of it, and that earlier run sat in the same column as this one now, and I named the number I had crossed.

I set the contempt down beside the protection I had bought, and found no way to argue with what I was looking at. It had all come out even. Sera was safer for a season, the Greens' move had landed, Wren was screened through the whole of it, and one ward-elder still walked under a name that was not his. It had come out even and the deployment could not be taken back, and both of those were true at once. There was no refusing the contempt. There was only somewhere to set it down.

I counted myself heavier in the work now than I had been, and easier to set down for it -- the more of the city I could hold, the more cleanly I could be lifted out of the way once the holding was done. I counted myself nearer the day they would not need me. I knew the number.

The place where the recognition would have gone I left empty. I set everything else down true and left that one line blank, and the blank was the most load-bearing thing on the page. Then I set the stylus down beside the closed ledger, not away from it. The suppression held. Everything else moved.


CHAPTER 19

The flies cover the city at full reach now, and the bargain runs both ways. Otto's people take what I see through Jarvis and pay for it by keeping the question of Sera's parentage quiet, and the trade holds as long as the work holds. I have watched the court at its own scale long enough that my contempt for it sits in me with nowhere to go -- exact, finished, fixed to nothing I do next. And somewhere up in the high city a name has been fastened to the woman who maps Flea Bottom. It has not reached me yet. It is on its way down.

  

Before dawn I lifted the bundle out of the trough and unfolded it. Not the fortnight's weight of the last drop -- a single fold of paper in Jarvis's own hand, and I held it up to the grey-dark that fills the room above the rendering works in the hour before first light. That grey is not full dark and not lamplight. It asks you to tilt the page and hold it close, and I have read every drop of eleven months by it. The sheet named a corridor. A court-tier reading, not the lower-city watching I had done for a year: a chamberlain in the Red Keep's outer administrative ring -- a rank above the wards I map -- whose corridor patterns had run wrong for six weeks. Confirm the role. Map the contact interval. Name the faction. I set the sheet down on the bare floor, where I always set a thing before I take it up, and then I stood and worked out what it would cost.

The shape of the ask was new. What it asked for was not. The chamberlain's corridor sat inside the flies I already keep; I had the protection I owed elsewhere covered the same as ever; nothing in the request fell outside what I had already agreed to carry. I went on with the reading exactly as I had been going to -- the seeing it for what it was had not moved one line of where the work landed. I lifted the stylus and held it a moment, the working-touch of ink on vellum gone for the first time in four days. And the contempt came -- whole, the way it always comes, and with no door anywhere in the work for it to go through. It rests beside what I do and never inside it, the way a dead man's name stays in a count after the body it belonged to is gone: complete, unspoken to, next to every line and not one of them. The little distance the stylus did not travel was the whole of it. I laid it down beside the page. Not down. Not away. Beside. Knowing changes nothing it can reach.

Three days after the drop I went to make my reciprocity round for Daven. He is a tallow-render hand I have worked seven months -- a man down in lower Flea Bottom who passed me his lane's open windows twice a season for a small watch I kept on it; he tells me when the lane is clear, I keep it covered, and that trade is the whole of the thing between us. I took the corner at the Tallow Croft mouth. The cold came up off the stone and the render-smell hung close, the same one that soaks my own room, met out here in the open lane. Second-bell went past the corner and the corner stayed empty.

The vat-house shutter was closed over the east window. Closed, where it has stood open across every one of the forty-three rounds I have made here in seven months. I have the count of them. The open window was the one constant, and a shutter set against that count is not weather and it is not a wall. It is a thing he learned and acted on before I reached the lane. A man who breaks the trade has done the sum anyone with a head would do: if the talk of a witch has come down to the people who know my face, then being one of them has a price now, and a closed shutter is what the price looks like from the lane. I held the corner a second time at third-bell. It stayed empty. Daven never came.

I walked the lane. My stride did not break at the empty corner and my hands stayed loose at my sides, and I went past the place I would have stopped, and the lane ran on under me. Back in the room I cut him from the coverage -- his lane runs now through the standing fly-node at the Croft intersection, without him in it, a lane like any other lane -- and I left it there. The contempt is finished. Nothing about what I do next has changed. The flies are still out over the whole of it, one man lighter than they were this morning, and the morning's loss I have set in no record at all today.


CHAPTER 20

Otto's men carried my readings up to him through Jarvis and paid for them by keeping one question quiet -- whose daughter a certain girl was -- and that held as long as my work held. I had read this court long enough that the contempt for it sat in me whole and went nowhere near my hands. The name they gave the witch-woman who maps Flea Bottom had reached the service-stairs by now; that had cost me a slow seven months already. And the old king was dying. The Dance the realm had dreaded all my life was at its first hour, and Otto's people stood ready to move on the throne the moment the breath went out of him. The map I had made of this city was about to become the map of a war.

  

The servant-passages went empty before any bell. I had the flies thin along the Red Keep's lower walls, and I read it in them -- the late traffic that always ran those corridors ceasing all at once, doors opening that should not have opened, in an order no ordinary night arranged. I knew the king was dead before the city knew. Then the men came up through the Holdfast, the ones whose paths I had traced three months back and sent on through the channel, and they moved like a thing rehearsed -- the right men to the right rooms at the right pace, every step set against a cue I had helped them lay. I sat in the grey room above the rendering-works and let it all come to me through the flies, and I felt the cold of it and nothing I would call by a name. The work was finished and it had not needed me to finish it.

  

Toward morning the bell tolled the succession out across the wards, the deep one, carrying over the rooftops. The armed men filed into the junctions below it, and they took them in the order I had ranked them -- heaviest crossings first, just as my tallies would have set them down, each gate falling in its turn. I held the flies wide and watched one intersection give way to the next. The patron's men ran their sequence and none of it came through my hand anymore. I sent the flies out across the lower city, and the fighters poured down the lanes I had mapped, following my routes as though reading them off a page I had left open for them.

  

By midday the burning reached me -- not the heat yet, not the smoke, only the smell of it carried at ward-distance, the leading edge of a fire too far off to see. Then the fire itself came down the junctions I had catalogued, running the exact lines I had drawn and handed over, my own map laid into flame.

A man brought the message up to the room. It came off no channel the arrangement had ever used, and it named no one. It spoke of a function and said the function was finished; its work, it said, would be folded into a network already in hand. I read it once. The hand that had set it could have been Otto finding me out at last, or Otto simply done with me -- the wording did not say, and I did not ask. The thing the arrangement had bought was a silence around one girl's birth, held against a council that could rule on it. That council was filing into the junctions to take the crown. There was no one left to ask the question. I set the message down on the table. I said nothing, and I asked nothing.

  

The flies still held the lanes east of the water-gate.

That was the one ground I had never let the map reach -- the gap I kept open report after report, for a single child. Wren. The stitch-maker's ward, eleven years old, who had looked at me once in a crowd and worked out something of what I was and chosen, every day after, never to ask. She was the one cost I would not write down, because to write her down was to weigh her against the work, and that was the line I had not crossed. So I moved the flies around her lane, month on month, and left her the only clear ground in the city.

The flies were reading that ground, ordinary and quiet, when the smoke came into it.

The smoke filled the lanes, and the heat came in behind the smoke, and the flies broke apart in it -- scattered, the way small bodies scatter from fire. I waited for them to settle and come back the way they always came back. I held the room still and waited for the lanes to answer.

They did not answer. The flies did not come back from her lanes. I sent more, and the heat took those too, and the ground east of the water-gate stayed empty in a way no fire alone makes ground empty. I held there a long moment, waiting on a return that did not come, and I understood what the silence was. Wren was in those lanes. Wren was not coming out of them.

I did not let it into words. There was a shape the missing flies left in me, and I knew the shape -- I had stood inside it before, in another life, the same reach held open over people who never knew the reach was there -- and I would not look straight at it now any more than I had looked then. I kept the cold over it. I let no part of it become a sentence. A child I had kept clear ground for was dead in the one place I had held open, and I sat with that and said nothing, because there was nothing I could say that would not be a smaller thing than what it was.

  

I called the flies home before I left the room.

Not killed -- only let go, dispersed back to their own ranges, thinning out below any use, eleven months of work coming apart into the ordinary air it had been gathered from. The flies thinned across the wards until there was nothing of mine moving over the city at all. What came apart was what had been mine; the network Otto's people had taken was theirs to keep. I shouldered the pack -- light, a few coins above nothing -- and the room went from the center of a city I could feel to four walls I was leaving.

I walked out the south gate. No one marked me through it. The message had ended a function, not a person, and the person carrying a pack into the dark was on no list the apparatus kept. The gate gave onto the road, and the open sky ran flat past the last of the stone, and the light was going. The wards lay open behind me, the lanes east of the water-gate silent in the way I would carry the rest of my life. I took the road, and I did not stop, because stopping was the one thing left that would have asked me to say it out loud, and I would not.
