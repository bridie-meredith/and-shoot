# Westerosi Superstition Frame — How Uncanny Phenomena Are Categorized (Riverlands, 84–101 AC)

## Description

How Westerosi culture categorizes uncanny events in the Riverlands at 84–101 AC. Covers: the general folk framework for understanding the uncanny, the specific Riverlands superstition layer (mid-region, between old-gods north and Faith-dominant south), and the specific categories through which Taylor's swarm events will be narrated by witnesses.

This card tells impersonators of supporting cast how to narrate Taylor's power to themselves and others. It is the cognitive frame available to Westerosi witnesses who have no access to the concept of "parahuman" and no vocabulary for what they are seeing.

## Sensory Impact

The superstition frame is not itself a sensory condition. It is a cognitive one. Its sensory expression is the pause before a witness speaks — the moment of choosing which word to reach for. "The bees moved wrong." "The flies — they went where she looked." "Something was with her, in the air." These are the words that carry the frame. The frame's presence in a scene is felt in the register of how supporting characters describe what they saw.

## Duration

Persistent. This is the background cultural epistemology of Westerosi witnesses. It does not change within the project window.

## The General Westerosi Folk Framework

Westeros has no unified category for "parahuman" or "person with a biological mutation that grants unusual capability." The available frameworks for categorizing the uncanny:

**Omens:** Events in the natural world that carry meaning beyond themselves. A flock of ravens changing direction, an unusual insect gathering, a fire that burns strangely. Omens are not caused by a person; they happen in relation to a situation. A witness who categorizes Taylor's swarm events as omens is saying: this event has meaning, not this girl caused it. Omens predict or signal; they do not identify the mechanism.

**Blessings:** Intercession by a positive force (the Seven, the old gods, a specific divine aspect) on behalf of a person or cause. A blessing operates through natural elements — the wind, the rain, the creatures of the earth — and directs them in service of whoever is being blessed. Taylor's swarm protecting a smallfolk woman from coercion is legible as a blessing: the Mother's grace expressed through the swarm, protecting the vulnerable.

**Curses:** Malefic working through natural elements. The opposite of a blessing. A curse attaches to a person and makes the world around them go wrong for their enemies or for those who threaten what the curse-bearer carries. This is the adversarial read of the same swarm event.

**Signs from the Seven:** Direct manifestation of one of the Seven faces through a natural phenomenon. More specific than a general blessing — these are attributed to a specific divine aspect. The Maiden's favor expressed through a flight of white butterflies. The Stranger's presence announced by the gathering of crows. The Mother's protection enacted through biting insects that drive away men who threaten a woman and child.

**The Old Gods' work:** In the Riverlands specifically, old-gods attribution is available as a category even for people who nominally follow the Seven. The old gods of the north are associated with weirwood trees, ravens, and the natural world. Phenomena that feel older or stranger than Seven-theology fits are sometimes attributed to the old gods, particularly by older smallfolk who grew up in households where both traditions coexisted.

**Old magic, from before the Conquest:** The broadest and least specific category. "Old magic" is the name for things that don't fit any of the above — events that feel like they belong to a world that predates the Andal Conquest, the Faith, the Targaryen dragons. This is the category that the Riverlands superstition layer is most comfortable reaching for when the other categories don't fit cleanly.

**Witchcraft or diabolism:** Active working by a person who has made a compact with malign forces. The accusatory extreme. Reserved for events where the strangeness appears to serve a person's interests and the power appears to be theirs to deploy, not an impersonal force acting through them. This is the worst-case read for Taylor. It is the read that produces lord's-man involvement, institutional investigation, and possible trial. It is also the read that requires specific evidence of agency and intent, which Taylor's involuntary ignition does not cleanly provide.

## The Riverlands Superstition Layer

The Riverlands is transition territory. South of the Neck, the Seven are dominant. North of the Neck, the old gods. The Riverlands sit between, and their folk tradition carries both:

**Dual-tradition residue:** Older Riverlands families have traces of both traditions in their folk practice. They attend the sept on holy days and they don't cut down a weirwood tree if they find one. They invoke the Seven in formal prayer and they still avoid certain forest clearings "just in case." This is not theological confusion; it is pragmatic folk superstition that covers its bases.

**The specific Riverlands mid-region superstition palette:**
- Rivers have spirits — the Blue Fork and Gods Eye have local spirit-names in folk tradition, mostly invoked around fishing and ferry crossings
- Certain insects carry particular cultural weight: bees are associated with the Maiden (honey, sweetness, the feminine divine); ravens are the Crone's creatures (sight, knowledge, the boundary between living and dead); flies are associated with the Stranger (decay, the threshold between life and death)
- The concept of the "touched" — a person who has been brushed by the divine or the uncanny, carrying some residue of that contact in their daily behavior. Not a witch, not a saint, but someone the world treats slightly differently. A person who is "touched" is avoided in some contexts and sought in others (for blessing, for advice, for contact with the divine they carry).
- Seasonal uncanny: the period around the autumn equinox (when the boundary between living and dead is traditionally thinner) amplifies reports of uncanny events. Taylor's activities in autumn will be more readily attributed to supernatural forces than the same activities in spring.

## How Taylor's Swarm Events Are Categorized

**The ignition event at the market square (~86 AC):**

The collector's retinue leader: "The flies — they moved like a fist. I've never seen insects move like that." His report reads it as unnatural but does not reach for a supernatural category. He is not a Faith man and not a particularly superstitious soldier. He writes "the animals went strange" and files it as an anomaly he cannot explain.

The smallfolk woman who was being coerced: She will categorize it as a blessing — specifically as the Mother's protection, because that is the Seven-aspect that most cleanly covers "protection of a woman being harmed." She will whisper about it in those terms. The whisper chain carries her interpretation. "The Mother's hand was there" becomes the folk narrative of the ignition event.

The market square smallfolk bystanders: they saw something. Their categories are: omen (it means something is coming), blessing (someone was favored), old magic (it was just strange). Most will reach for the "touched" category for Taylor: she's one of those people, the ones the world is slightly different around.

Septon Rowan: he categorizes it theologically, carefully, and builds a layered interpretation. See `cond-faith-of-seven-jaehaerys`. His framework: the Stranger's presence (because the Stranger governs transitions, thresholds, the crossing from normal to uncanny), expressed as protection (which is the Mother's register), enacted through a child who is in a special relationship with the natural world (which is unusual but not unprecedented in faith history). He does not say "witch." He says "she carries something."

The traveling maester (if present): categorizes it as "insect behavior outside established natural parameters" and notes it without theological attribution. He wants to understand the mechanism, not the meaning. He does not have a mechanism that fits. His record reads: "anomalous arthropod aggregation, coordinated, apparently directed by or associated with the Ashford child. No naturalistic explanation available."

## The Narrative Vocabulary for Taylor's Power

Impersonators of supporting cast should draw from this vocabulary when writing how characters narrate Taylor's power to themselves and others:

**Words characters reach for:**
- "the bees moved with her"
- "the Maiden's favor"
- "something touched the air"
- "old magic, from before the Faith"
- "the creatures answered her"
- "the Stranger was walking in that square today"
- "she's got something in her, that girl"
- "the beasts know her for something"
- "it was a blessing" / "it was a curse" (depending on whose side the speaker is on)
- "touched" (the Riverlands folk term for a person who carries the uncanny)

**Words characters do NOT reach for:**
- "parahuman"
- "power"
- "ability" (in the modern sense)
- "mutation"
- "superpowers"
- "she controls insects" (this is too direct and mechanistic; witnesses see the result, not the mechanism)
- "magic" as a self-contained noun (magic in Westeros is usually adjective or qualifier: "old magic," "blood magic," "dragon-magic"; it is not a clean category)

**The effect on Taylor's organizing:** The "touched" category is her most useful one. A person who is "touched" is strange but not threatening by default; they are avoided but not accused; they are discussed but not prosecuted. Taylor's goal in early arcs is to stay in the "touched" category and out of the "witch" category. The ignition event's aftermath narrative (the Mother's hand, not Taylor's power) is the folk frame that accomplishes this — and the smallfolk woman's testimony (or silence) is what anchors that interpretation in the whisper chain.

Both draw from the same cultural vocabulary; the Faith version is more elaborated and more consequential (it generates letters; the folk version generates whispers). Load both for full coverage.

**With Septon Rowan:** Rowan is the character who most explicitly operates in the intersection between these two cards. He applies the Faith's theological framework to events that the folk frame has already begun to narrate. His interpretations reinforce and amplify the folk frame, giving it institutional backing it would not otherwise have.

**With the smallfolk whisper network (`cond-smallfolk-political-physics`):** The superstition frame is what gives the whisper chain its narrative content when it carries information about Taylor. "Did you hear what happened at the market?" needs an interpretive frame to be transmitted. The superstition vocabulary is that frame.

**For auditor use:** Any scene where a Westerosi character (who is not Taylor) attributes her power to a mechanistic or non-supernatural explanation ("she must have trained the insects") is a period-registration violation. The available categories for Westerosi witnesses are supernatural or subcategories of supernatural. Any scene where Taylor's power is casually dismissed as having no meaning is also a violation — in this world, uncanny events have meaning; the question is what kind.
