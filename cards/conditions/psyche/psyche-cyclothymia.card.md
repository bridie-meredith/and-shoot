# Cyclothymic Disorder — Psyche Profile

## Description
This is a psyche-profile condition card: it colors a *character's* behavior, perception, and interiority — not a location. Cyclothymic Disorder is a **chronic, low-grade** mood disorder: numerous periods of hypomanic-type symptoms and numerous periods of depressive symptoms over years, **none of which ever reach the threshold of a full hypomanic, manic, or major depressive episode**. The essence in plain language: the mood is always moving, never level, but never so far that any one swing gets a name of its own.

## Clinical Profile
- **Taxonomy:** DSM-5-TR — Bipolar and Related Disorders, Cyclothymic Disorder (sub-threshold chronic mood instability). ICD-11 — Mood disorders, Cyclothymia (6A62).
- **Core features:**
  - **≥2 years** (≥1 year in children/adolescents) of numerous periods of hypomanic-level symptoms and numerous periods of depressive-level symptoms.
  - The symptoms are **present at least half the time**, and the person is **never symptom-free for more than two months** at a stretch.
  - Crucially, criteria for a full hypomanic, manic, or major depressive episode have **never been met** — every swing stays under the line.
  - The instability is persistent and exhausting rather than dramatic; it can feel like a *temperament* more than an illness.
  - May progress over time into bipolar I or bipolar II in a subset of cases; cyclothymia is sometimes a precursor.
  - Causes real distress or functional impairment despite the muted amplitude — the burden is in the relentlessness, not the height.
- **Onset & course:** Typically begins in adolescence or early adulthood, often insidiously. Course is **chronic and persistent** by definition — a long, undulating baseline rather than discrete episodes.
- **Prevalence:** Approximately 0.4–1% lifetime (likely underdiagnosed; many never seek care, reading it as personality).
- **Common comorbidities:** Anxiety disorders, substance use, and personality features overlapping psyche-borderline-pd. Chronicity invites comparison with psyche-persistent-depressive-disorder.
- **Differential:** Bipolar I (psyche-bipolar-i) and bipolar II (psyche-bipolar-ii) involve at least one *full* episode; cyclothymia never crosses that line. Persistent depressive disorder (psyche-persistent-depressive-disorder) is chronic *low* mood without the up-swings — cyclothymia's defining feature is the alternation. Borderline PD (psyche-borderline-pd) shifts on interpersonal triggers within hours and carries an unstable self-image; cyclothymia's swings are slower, less reactive, and not identity-centered. Normal temperamental variability is distinguished by the persistence, the impairment, and the two-year duration.

## Sensory / Behavioral Impact
The character lives on a **moving floor**. There is rarely a stable, neutral middle to plant on.

Mood drifts up and down in muted waves — a few good, energized, sociable days, then a stretch of flat, irritable, leaden ones — without either pole ever announcing itself as a clear "episode." Energy, sleep, appetite, confidence, and sociability all undulate together, slowly enough that the character may not notice the tide turning until it has.

Because the amplitude is low, the character — and everyone around them — often files the pattern under *personality*: "moody," "intense," "mercurial," "up and down," "you never know which one you'll get." It doesn't look like illness because nothing ever crests into crisis.

The cost is the chronic instability itself — the inability to count on their own next week, their own follow-through, their own read of how they'll feel about anything by tomorrow.

## Duration
**Chronic and persistent** — this is the defining timescale. The swings run **days to weeks**, never resolving into a clean baseline for long. Within a single scene the character is usually in one phase of the wave, and that phase is **stable** for the scene's duration. What can shift it mid-scene is intensity or irritability — a good-phase character running slightly hot, a flat-phase character snapping — but not a full polarity flip. Across a story arc, the *relentlessness* is the through-line: render the absence of a reliable middle, not dramatic peaks and troughs.

## Presentation & Tells
- **In an up-phase:** brighter, more energetic, more talkative and social, sleeping a little less, more confident or impulsive — but mildly, never the unmistakable lift of full hypomania.
- **In a down-phase:** flatter, more withdrawn, more irritable or tired, less motivated, slower to answer — but not the foreclosed weight of a full depressive episode.
- **The signature tell is the churn, not the height:** people describe the character as "moody," "mercurial," "you never know which version you'll get"; the instability reads as temperament, not symptom.
- Unpredictable reliability: plans made warmly in an up-phase, dreaded or quietly dropped in a down-phase — and the apologies that follow.
- Commitments and opinions that don't hold their shape week to week, so that others learn not to bank on them.
- Often *no* dramatic crisis to point to — no single bad night to anchor concern — which is part of why it goes unnamed for years.

## Interiority / POV Voice
From the inside, the most honest word is *unsteady*. The character has never quite known what they'll wake up as — and they've stopped expecting to. There's no clear illness to point at, no episode with a beginning and an end, just a weather that won't settle. In an **up-phase**, the voice is warmer and quicker and briefly hopeful — *this* is who I really am, things are clicking — but with a worn-in, half-suppressed knowledge that it won't hold. In a **down-phase**, the voice goes flat and irritable and tired of itself, and the recent up-phase is reread as naïveté.

What's distinct and load-bearing is the **fatigue of self-distrust**: the character can't fully commit to any feeling, any plan, any self-assessment, because experience has taught them it's provisional — the floor will move again. They may chronically over-promise in the highs and quietly fail to deliver in the lows, then carry the shame of that pattern. The thing they refuse to look at is that this *is* a condition with a name and not just their unfixable temperament — because naming it would mean it isn't simply who they are, and that's both a relief and a grief. The quiet truth lives in the longing for a stable middle they've never been given.

## Dramatic Affordances
- The **"is this just who I am?" question**: a character who has never separated their illness from their personality, and the slow, ambivalent arc of learning there's a difference.
- **The unreliable promiser**: enthusiasm in the up-phase, retreat in the down-phase, and the strain it puts on every relationship that learned to count on the wrong version.
- **Low-amplitude, high-frequency drama**: no single crisis to anchor a scene — instead the cumulative attrition of never being steady, which rewards quiet, patient storytelling.
- **Precursor tension**: in some arcs, the looming question of whether the swings will deepen into something with a sharper name.
- A character whose **inconstancy is the wound** — who would trade every good phase for one reliable, ordinary, level week.

## Fences — Responsible Use
Real people live with cyclothymia, often for years before it's named, and lead full ordinary lives. This card renders a *character*, not a verdict. Refuse the cheap portrayals: do not flatten cyclothymia into "just moody," a punchline, or a charming quirk — the chronic instability causes real distress and real damage to work and relationships. Equally, do not inflate it into the spectacle of full bipolar mania or major depression; its defining trait is that the swings **stay under the line**. Respect that the character (and their world) may genuinely not know it's a condition — render the ambiguity, don't caricature it. On violence: people with cyclothymia are **far more often harmed — by stigma, self-doubt, and strained relationships — than harmful to others**. NO operational detail about self-harm, suicide, or substance use — depict the lived experience and outward presentation, never a method or manual.

## Interaction Notes
- With **psyche-bipolar-i** / **psyche-bipolar-ii**: same family, lower amplitude; the line is whether any swing has ever reached a *full* episode. If a swing crosses that line in-story, the diagnosis has effectively changed — a usable arc beat, but render it deliberately, not by accident.
- With **psyche-persistent-depressive-disorder**: both are chronic; the distinguishing feature is cyclothymia's *up-swings*. If stacking or contrasting, keep cyclothymia's alternation visible against PDD's steady low.
- With **psyche-borderline-pd**: easily conflated as "mood swings"; keep them distinct — cyclothymia's waves run slower (days/weeks), are less reactive to interpersonal triggers, and don't center on an unstable self-image.
- With anxiety and substance/addictive conditions: commonly comorbid; substances may be used to flatten the churn, worsening it.
- With environmental condition cards: unstructured, high-stress, or sleep-disrupting settings amplify the churn; routine, rest, and stability dampen it — a useful lever for showing a character's instability rise or settle with their circumstances.
