# Specific Phobia — Psyche Profile

## Description
A psyche-profile condition card. It colors a *character's* behavior, perception, and interiority — not a location. Specific Phobia is a marked, out-of-proportion fear of a particular object or situation — heights, spiders, blood, flying, enclosed spaces, needles — that triggers near-immediate fear or panic and drives avoidance the character usually knows is irrational and cannot reason their way out of.

## Clinical Profile
- **Taxonomy:** DSM-5-TR — Anxiety Disorders. ICD-11 — Anxiety or fear-related disorders (6B03, Specific phobia).
- **Core features:**
  - Marked fear or anxiety about a specific object or situation, near-immediate on exposure.
  - The phobic stimulus is actively avoided or endured with intense distress.
  - The fear is out of proportion to actual danger and is recognized as excessive (insight is usually intact).
  - Common subtypes: animal (spiders, dogs, snakes), natural environment (heights, storms, water), blood-injection-injury (needles, blood, medical procedures), situational (flying, elevators, enclosed spaces), and other.
  - Avoidance, anticipatory anxiety, and distress meaningfully interfere with life.
  - The blood-injection-injury subtype is distinctive — it can trigger a vasovagal drop (fainting) rather than the usual fight-or-flight surge.
- **Onset & course:** Often childhood onset (animal/natural-environment types especially); situational types can begin later. Tends to be chronic if untreated, though severity varies; highly treatable with graded exposure.
- **Prevalence:** Approximately 7–12% over a 12-month period; among the most common of all anxiety disorders.
- **Common comorbidities:** Other specific phobias (often more than one), panic disorder, generalized anxiety; blood-injection-injury phobia can complicate necessary medical care.
- **Differential:** Distinguished from agoraphobia (fear is of the *specific* object, not of situations where escape/help is hard), from panic disorder (attacks here are cued, not unexpected), and from ordinary dislike by the intensity, the avoidance, and the functional cost.

## Sensory / Behavioral Impact
Away from the feared thing, the character may be entirely unbothered — this is the most situation-bound of the anxiety disorders. But the *cue* changes everything in an instant: at the sight, sound, or even the prospect of the phobic stimulus, the body fires before thought can intervene. Perception narrows to the threat — the spider becomes the only object in the room, the edge becomes the whole world. Reasoning goes offline; the rational knowledge that it's "just a needle" is fully intact and completely useless. For blood/injection/injury, the curve inverts: a sudden drop in pressure, gray vision, clamminess, and the floor coming up.

## Duration
Episodic and cue-bound, laid over anticipatory dread when the cue is forecastable (a flight next week, a blood draw scheduled). The fear response is acute and short — it peaks fast on exposure and subsides once the stimulus is gone or escaped, often within minutes. Within a scene it can go from zero to peak the instant the cue appears, and reliably resolves once distance or escape is achieved. The anticipation, however, can poison hours or days ahead of a known encounter.

## Presentation & Tells
- Scans a new space for the feared thing first — the corners (spiders), the windows (heights), the exits.
- Freezes, recoils, or bolts at the cue; a sharp involuntary sound; hands up, body turned away.
- Will go far out of the way to avoid it — stairs not the elevator, the long route not the bridge, no to the flight.
- Manages the *prospect*: changes plans, makes excuses, gets someone else to handle the thing.
- For needles/blood: looks away, goes pale and sweaty, lightheaded, has to lie down, may faint.
- Disproportionate vigilance to a cue others don't even register — startles at a shape on the wall.
- Insight on display — "I know this is stupid, I can't help it" — embarrassment alongside the fear.

## Interiority / POV Voice
There's a clean split in the mind that makes it almost unbearable: the rational self stands right there, calm and certain that the elevator is safe and the spider is the size of a thumbnail and harmless — and it is *ignored.* The fear doesn't argue; it acts. The body has already decided before the thought finishes, and the narrating voice watches its own recoil from a half-step behind, mortified and helpless. When the cue is present the world collapses to it: peripheral vision goes, sound dims, the feared thing swells until it is the only fact in the room, and every cell wants one thing only — *away, now.* When the cue is merely coming — the appointment on the calendar, the flight in three days — the dread metastasizes backward through ordinary time, souring meals and sleep over a thing that hasn't happened yet. And running under all of it, the small private shame of being undone by something everyone else walks past without a glance.

## Dramatic Affordances
The phobia is a clean, ready-made obstacle: the one path forward runs straight through the one thing this character cannot do. Drama lives in the moment they must — climb the ladder, cross the bridge, hold still for the needle, reach into the dark where the thing is — and what it costs them to override a body that won't be reasoned with. It exposes character economically: who they trust to talk them through it, what they're willing to face it *for,* the gap between competence everywhere else and helplessness here. Comedy and tenderness both live in the witnessing. Arcs bend toward graded courage — not the absence of fear but the act in spite of it — and the specific, earned triumph of doing the impossible small thing.

## Fences — Responsible Use
Real people live with specific phobias; this card renders a character, not a punchline. Avoid using the phobia purely as a joke or as proof the character is silly or weak — the fear is genuine and the body's response is involuntary, and "it's not even dangerous" is the cruelest and least helpful thing to say. Don't write the cure as one forced exposure that magically resolves it; flooding someone against their will is harmful, not heroic. Honor the intact insight — these characters usually *know* it's irrational, which is its own particular misery. No operational detail on triggering or worsening a real person's phobia. The blood-injection-injury faint is a physiological reflex, not cowardice.

## Interaction Notes
Often appears in clusters — one character may carry several specific phobias. With **psyche-panic-disorder**, a phobic cue can tip into a full attack, and the two share the body's alarm machinery. The boundary with **psyche-agoraphobia** matters: if the fear is of the elevator-as-object it's specific phobia; if it's of being trapped somewhere escape or help is unavailable across many settings, it's agoraphobia. **psyche-generalized-anxiety-disorder** can coat the phobia in broader anticipatory worry. Against environmental condition cards, the phobia is inert until the specific cue enters the scene — then that single condition card (a height, a confined space, an animal, a medical setting) becomes the dominant force, eclipsing everything else in the character's perception until escape.
