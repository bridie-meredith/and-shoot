# Disinhibited Social Engagement Disorder — Psyche Profile

## Description
A psyche-profile condition card. It colors a *character's* behavior, perception, and interiority — not a location. Disinhibited Social Engagement Disorder also grows from early deprivation, but it opens the opposite door from RAD: the child has no working sense of stranger-caution and reaches for *anyone* — wandering off with unfamiliar adults, overly familiar, the wariness that should protect them simply not there.

## Clinical Profile
- **Taxonomy:** DSM-5-TR — Trauma- and Stressor-Related Disorders (code 313.89 / F94.2). ICD-11 — Disorders specifically associated with stress; Disinhibited social engagement disorder (6B45).
- **Core features:**
  - A pattern of actively approaching and interacting with **unfamiliar adults** with at least two of:
    - Reduced or absent reticence toward strangers.
    - Overly familiar verbal or physical behavior (beyond cultural norms and developmental age).
    - Diminished or absent checking-back with a caregiver after venturing out, even in unfamiliar settings.
    - Willingness to go off with an unfamiliar adult with little or no hesitation.
  - The behaviors are not limited to impulsivity (as in ADHD) but include the socially disinhibited approach.
  - A history of **insufficient care** — neglect, repeated caregiver changes, institutional rearing.
  - Developmental age of at least 9 months.
- **Onset & course:** roots in early deprivation. Distinctively, the indiscriminate sociability can **persist even after the child is placed in a good, stable home** — it does not always remit with improved care the way RAD often does, and can echo into adolescence and adulthood as boundary and relationship difficulty.
- **Prevalence:** rare overall; notably elevated among children with histories of institutional care and multiple placements.
- **Common comorbidities:** ADHD (psyche-adhd) — the two overlap on impulsive approach but are distinct; cognitive/developmental delay; later relational and boundary difficulties feeding complex trauma (psyche-complex-ptsd).
- **Differential:** from RAD (psyche-reactive-attachment-disorder) — shared deprivation root, opposite presentation (over-approach vs. withdrawal). From ADHD (psyche-adhd) — DSED is specifically about *social disinhibition toward strangers and weak attachment selectivity*, not general impulsivity; the two can co-occur.

## Sensory / Behavioral Impact
The character moves toward people without the brake everyone else has. A stranger is not a question mark — they are an open door. The character will start a conversation, climb into a lap, share something too intimate, follow a friendly adult out of a room, all without the reflexive checking-back that anchors a securely attached child to a safe base. Affect is often warm and outgoing on the surface — they *seem* unusually friendly — which is exactly what makes the missing caution hard for others to see as a problem rather than a charm.

## Duration
Early-laid and notably *persistent* — more durable than RAD, capable of outlasting the deprivation that caused it and surviving years of good care. Within a scene the over-approach is the default and fires fast: a new face draws the character in before any caregiver can intervene. It does not toggle off easily; a familiar adult's redirection may slow a specific approach without altering the underlying pattern. Novelty and the presence of any responsive-seeming stranger pull the behavior out reliably.

## Presentation & Tells
- Walks up to strangers, starts talking, no warm-up and no wariness.
- Over-familiar fast — hugs, sits too close, shares private things with someone just met.
- Will go off with an unfamiliar adult readily, without hesitation or checking back.
- Doesn't orient back to the safe caregiver in a new place the way peers do.
- Reads as "everybody's friend," disarmingly open — which masks the missing brake.
- Indiscriminate: the warmth isn't *selective*, so it doesn't deepen into preference the way ordinary friendliness does.
- Caregivers describe constant low-grade fear of the child wandering off with anyone.

## Interiority / POV Voice
There is no inner gatekeeper at the door. Other people aren't sorted into safe and unsafe, known and unknown — the sorting machine was never built, so everyone arrives as a potential source of the attention the character is always, quietly, short of. A new face is simply *available*, and the character moves toward it the way water finds the low place, without the half-second of caution that would tell someone else to wait. What's missing isn't visible from inside — the character doesn't feel reckless; they feel friendly, hungry for connection, untroubled by a danger they were never wired to register. Underneath the easy warmth runs a thinness: the bonds don't catch and hold, because the part that would make *this* person matter more than the next stranger never formed. They give the same open face to everyone and wonder, sometimes, why no one stays.

## Dramatic Affordances
This character is in danger precisely *because* they are friendly — the missing caution is a vulnerability dressed as charm, and everyone around them feels it before they can name it. They reach for any new person in the room, no warm-up, no fear, which no one else in the scene would do. Drama lives in the protective adult's helpless watching — the child who will leave with anyone; in the contrast between the disarming surface warmth and the bonds that never deepen; in the predator the missing brake exposes them to, and the guardian racing the gap. The quiet truth lives in the realization that the open door swings *both* ways — everyone comes in, no one stays put — and in the loneliness underneath the indiscriminate friendliness.

## Fences — Responsible Use
DSED comes from early deprivation — the over-friendliness is a wound, not a personality, and emphatically not the child "being manipulative" or "attention-seeking" in a blameworthy sense. Be especially careful: the missing stranger-caution is a real safety vulnerability, so render the *risk to the child* without writing any operational detail about how that risk could be exploited. Avoid both the "creepy too-friendly kid" trope and the dismissal of the behavior as mere precocious charm. Note that, unlike RAD, it can persist despite good care — so don't write a tidy cure as the reward for a loving home. The card renders a child (or adult) whose door has no lock, not a verdict on their worth or their future.

## Interaction Notes
The mirror-twin of reactive attachment disorder (psyche-reactive-attachment-disorder) — same deprivation root, opposite door; staging both ideas in the same family or institution illuminates each. Overlaps and can co-occur with ADHD (psyche-adhd) on the impulsive-approach axis, though the social-indiscriminateness is distinct. The persistent boundary and relationship difficulty can feed forward into complex PTSD (psyche-complex-ptsd) and adult attachment problems. Environmental condition cards drive it sharply: any setting dense with unfamiliar adults (a crowd, a new institution, a public place) pulls the over-approach out immediately, and — unlike RAD — a stable home does not reliably switch it off, which is dramatically and safety-relevantly important to honor.
