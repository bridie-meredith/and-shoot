# Alcohol Use Disorder — Psyche Profile

## Description
A psyche-profile condition card that colors a *character's* behavior, perception, and interiority (not a location). Alcohol Use Disorder (AUD) is a chronic, relapsing pattern of drinking the character can no longer reliably govern — the drinking continues, and often escalates, in the face of mounting harm to health, work, and the people they love.

## Clinical Profile
- **Taxonomy:** DSM-5-TR — Substance-Related and Addictive Disorders (Alcohol Use Disorder), graded mild / moderate / severe by criterion count. ICD-11 — Disorders due to substance use or addictive behaviours (Disorders due to use of alcohol).
- **Core features:**
  - Loss of control over amount and duration — drinking more, or longer, than intended.
  - Persistent wish or failed attempts to cut down; large parts of life given over to drinking or recovering from it.
  - Craving — an intrusive pull toward a drink that crowds out other thought.
  - Continued use despite knowing it is damaging relationships, work, or health.
  - Tolerance (needing more for the same effect) and withdrawal (shakes, sweats, anxiety, insomnia when the level drops) — the body has reorganized around the substance.
  - Activities narrowed: the social, occupational, and recreational world shrinks to what accommodates drinking.
- **Onset & course:** Often emerges in late teens to mid-twenties, but can begin at any age. Typically chronic and relapsing, with periods of control, slips, and remission; many people recover fully and stay well.
- **Prevalence:** Approximately 5–8% point prevalence and higher lifetime prevalence in adults in many populations; one of the most common substance use disorders.
- **Common comorbidities:** depression (psyche-major-depressive-disorder), anxiety (psyche-generalized-anxiety-disorder), trauma disorders (psyche-ptsd), other substance use disorders (psyche-opioid-use-disorder, psyche-stimulant-use-disorder), and antisocial personality patterns (psyche-antisocial-pd) in a subset.
- **Differential:** Distinguished from non-disordered heavy or social drinking by the loss of control and continued use despite harm — the line is impaired governance and consequence, not quantity alone.

## Sensory / Behavioral Impact
The character moves through the scene on a hidden internal clock — the felt time since the last drink and the felt time until the next. Affect regulation runs through the bottle: edges softened when topped up, jagged when not. Early in a craving or in withdrawal, fine motor control frays (a tremor in the hands at rest, steadied by the first drink), sleep is broken, attention is hard to hold. When drinking, judgment and inhibition loosen, warmth or belligerence surfaces, speech slows or thickens, the body grows clumsy. The world is sorted, half-consciously, into places where a drink is available and places where it is not.

## Duration
Chronic and relapsing across a life; within a single scene, highly dynamic. State swings with blood level — anticipation, relief, intoxication, the dimming, then the rising pressure of withdrawal. A scene can turn on whether the character has had access, how long it has been, and whether a drink is in reach. Stress, a trigger, a familiar place, or an offered glass can shift the interior state mid-scene.

## Presentation & Tells
- A drink located, monitored, and managed — refills tracked, glasses kept full, the bar's distance from the chair quietly noted.
- Tremor in the hands at rest, eased after the first drink; sweating, restlessness, or irritability when it has been too long.
- Smell of alcohol carried through into morning; over-bright mints, gum, or mouthwash deployed to cover it.
- Plausible, rehearsed reasons to drink — a toast, a long day, "just one" — and quiet management of others' attention to how much.
- Reliability that erodes: lateness, missed mornings, a slow narrowing of obligations to fit the drinking.
- Defensiveness or breezy deflection when intake is named; the subject steered away.
- Flushed face, broken capillaries, bloodshot eyes, weight changes over a longer arc.

## Interiority / POV Voice
From inside, the drink is not the problem — it is the solution, the one reliable thing, the place the noise finally stops. The first one is relief made physical: the shoulders drop, the world loses its hard edges, the self the character can finally stand to be arrives. The interior voice is fluent in bargaining and time. *One more, then I'll stop. After tonight. I had it under control last month, I can do it again.* The accounting is constant and dishonest with itself — counting drinks while not-counting, knowing the number and refusing the number. There is a watched object in every room and a clock that no one else can see. Mornings carry a specific dread: the gap to be reconstructed, the texture of the night that won't come back, the resolve made and already doubted. Shame and the drink that quiets shame run in a closed loop — the thing that hurts is the thing that helps, and the character knows it and reaches anyway. What the inner voice refuses to look at directly is the size of the thing, the way the world has narrowed, the faces of the people quietly leaving. Underneath it, often, is a true and weary wish to be free of it — surfacing in the clear hours, drowned by evening.

## Dramatic Affordances
This character reaches for the drink the moment a scene applies pressure — it is their tool for grief, celebration, boredom, and fear alike, where another character would reach for words or solitude. The drama lives in the gap between the self they are sober and the self they become, in the loyalty and exhaustion of the people around them, in the near-misses and the relapses, in the morning resolutions. Rich vein: the first honest admission; the relapse that follows a long stretch of control; the recovery scene where craving has to be sat with and not answered; the relationship that loves the person and cannot live with the drinking. The quiet truth lives in the clear-eyed hours when the character sees exactly what is happening and grieves it — and in the ordinary, undramatic courage of one more day not-drinking.

## Fences — Responsible Use
Real people live with AUD; the card renders a character, not a verdict, and recovery is real and common. Refuse the lazy-drunk caricature — the comic stumbling fool, the one-note degenerate. Refuse moralizing: this is a disorder of brain and body, not a failure of willpower, and shame is part of the disease, not a cure for it. Do not make the drinking the character's whole identity — render the person the drinking is happening to. Avoid the redemption-by-rock-bottom cliché as the only available arc; recovery rarely runs in a clean line, and relapse is a feature of the illness, not a moral collapse. Do not depict withdrawal as trivial or as merely a hangover — severe alcohol withdrawal is a genuine medical emergency — but render it through the lived experience, not as a how-to. NO operational detail: do not specify quantities, sourcing, or methods that could function as instruction.

## Interaction Notes
Stacks heavily with depression and anxiety (psyche-major-depressive-disorder, psyche-generalized-anxiety-disorder) — alcohol often self-medicates both and worsens both over time, creating a downward spiral. With trauma (psyche-ptsd, psyche-complex-ptsd) the drink numbs intrusion and dysregulation; sobriety can surface the trauma it was holding down. Combines with other substance use (psyche-opioid-use-disorder, psyche-stimulant-use-disorder) into dangerous polysubstance patterns. With environmental condition cards: a celebration, a wake, a bar, an isolated long night, or social pressure to toast all raise the interior pull and should be played as live stressors, not set dressing.
