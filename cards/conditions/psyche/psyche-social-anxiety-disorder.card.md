# Social Anxiety Disorder — Psyche Profile

## Description
A psyche-profile condition card. It colors a *character's* behavior, perception, and interiority — not a location. Social Anxiety Disorder (social phobia) is an intense, persistent fear of being scrutinized and found wanting in social or performance situations — the conviction that one will be judged, embarrassed, or exposed, and that everyone is watching for the failure.

## Clinical Profile
- **Taxonomy:** DSM-5-TR — Anxiety Disorders. ICD-11 — Anxiety or fear-related disorders (6B04, Social anxiety disorder).
- **Core features:**
  - Marked fear of social situations involving possible scrutiny — meeting people, eating/drinking or speaking in front of others, performing.
  - Fear of acting in a way (or showing anxiety symptoms — blushing, sweating, voice shaking) that will be humiliating or negatively judged.
  - The situations almost always provoke fear and are endured with distress or avoided.
  - The fear is out of proportion to actual social threat, and the person typically knows this and still can't override it.
  - Anticipatory dread beforehand, hyper-self-monitoring during, and a brutal "post-mortem" replay afterward.
  - May be performance-only (e.g. public speaking) or generalized across most social settings.
- **Onset & course:** Typically early — childhood to mid-teens, often traceable to a shy temperament. Tends to be chronic if untreated; may ease somewhat with age and structure but rarely vanishes on its own.
- **Prevalence:** Approximately 7% over a 12-month period; one of the most common anxiety disorders.
- **Common comorbidities:** Depression, other anxiety disorders, avoidant personality disorder (on a continuum), alcohol use as social lubricant; in childhood, selective mutism sits on the same spectrum.
- **Differential:** Distinguished from ordinary shyness by intensity, persistence, and functional cost. Unlike panic disorder, attacks are cued by social scrutiny, not unexpected; unlike autism, the social difficulty is driven by fear of judgment rather than difference in social cognition; unlike avoidant PD, it is an anxiety disorder, though the two heavily overlap.

## Sensory / Behavioral Impact
The character enters social space already on stage and already failing the performance in their own mind. Attention turns inward and adversarial: instead of tracking the conversation, they monitor themselves as if through a hostile camera — *how do I look, was that weird, are they bored.* The body betrays them in exactly the visible ways they fear: heat in the face, sweat, a dry mouth, a voice that thins or shakes, hands that don't know where to go. Speech contracts — fewer words, softer, hedged. Energy is spent not on connection but on damage control, and a short conversation can leave them as wrung out as a day of labor.

## Duration
Chronic and situation-cued. Outside the feared contexts the character may be entirely at ease — warm, funny, themselves with a trusted few. The anxiety spikes with the *scrutiny gradient*: one-on-one with someone safe is fine; a group of acquaintances is hard; speaking up in a meeting, being introduced to strangers, or performing is brutal. Within a scene it surges on arrival, on being addressed, on any moment that turns the room's attention toward them, and it eases as a setting becomes familiar or an ally is present.

## Presentation & Tells
- Avoids eye contact, or holds it too carefully and too long, having decided the natural amount.
- Blushing, visible sweating, a voice that drops in volume or wavers; clears the throat before speaking.
- Rehearses lines silently before saying them; the timing comes out a half-beat late.
- Defers, agrees, laughs along; rarely the one to start a topic or disagree.
- Glued to a phone, a drink, a doorway, a task — anything that excuses not circulating.
- Arrives late and leaves early; gravitates to the edge of the room, the corner, the friend.
- Over-apologizes; takes any small awkwardness as a major failure and carries it.

## Interiority / POV Voice
The terror is being *seen* — not hurt, seen. From inside, every social moment is a verdict in progress, and the verdict is always nearly in, always nearly bad. The inner voice runs two tracks: one tries to participate, the other narrates the participation back as evidence against the self — *that was too much, that was nothing, why did I say it like that, they're being nice but they can tell.* They are certain their nervousness is broadcast in neon, that the blush and the shaking hand are the whole story everyone is reading. So they manage: pre-scripting, hedging, deferring, making themselves small enough to escape notice — and then they hate the smallness, because they know there is a warmer, funnier person in there who only comes out when no one is grading. The worst part is after. The replay. They will lie awake re-running a thirty-second exchange, prosecuting it, certain of an embarrassment that the other person forgot before they reached the door.

## Dramatic Affordances
This character wants connection as badly as anyone and is locked out of it by the wanting — the ache of standing at the edge of a warmth you can't make yourself walk into. Drama lives in the cost of small acts that are nothing to others: asking a question, sending a text, staying at the party, saying the true thing out loud. Great scenes pair them with someone who makes the room safe — and the quiet revelation, to the reader and sometimes to the other character, of who they are when the grading stops. Conflict comes from being misread as cold, aloof, or stuck-up when the truth is the opposite. Arcs bend toward staying in the discomfort long enough to learn the verdict was never being handed down — and toward the vulnerability of being known.

## Fences — Responsible Use
Real people live with social anxiety; this card renders a character, not a verdict. It is not "just shyness" to be teased out of, nor arrogance, nor disinterest — it is fear, and the person usually wishes more than anyone that they could just relax. Don't write the cure as a single bold moment that fixes everything; growth is incremental and hard-won. Avoid the trope where social anxiety is endearing quirk for a love interest to rescue. Don't conflate it with introversion (a preference) or with not caring (the opposite is true). No operational detail on alcohol or substances as social coping. Render the inner warmth, not just the outward freeze.

## Interaction Notes
Sits on a continuum with **psyche-avoidant-pd** — where social anxiety is pervasive, lifelong, and identity-shaping, the two blur, and the avoidant pattern adds a deeper conviction of inadequacy. With **psyche-panic-disorder**, the social fear can crest into full attacks, and the horror of attacking *in public* compounds both. **psyche-generalized-anxiety-disorder** adds diffuse worry on top of the social-specific fear. In children it overlaps with **psyche-selective-mutism**, which can be read as social anxiety so severe that speech itself shuts down in feared settings. Comorbid **psyche-major-depressive-disorder** is common as isolation deepens. Against environmental condition cards, performance settings, crowds of acquaintances, and any "all eyes on you" moment amplify; one-on-one with a trusted person, a clear role/task, and dim familiar rooms dampen.
