# Opioid Use Disorder — Psyche Profile

## Description
A psyche-profile condition card that colors a *character's* behavior, perception, and interiority (not a location). Opioid Use Disorder (OUD) is a chronic, relapsing condition in which the character's life reorganizes around opioids — the use continues despite escalating harm, and the body's own dependence makes stopping a physical ordeal, not a simple choice.

## Clinical Profile
- **Taxonomy:** DSM-5-TR — Substance-Related and Addictive Disorders (Opioid Use Disorder), graded mild / moderate / severe. ICD-11 — Disorders due to substance use or addictive behaviours (Disorders due to use of opioids).
- **Core features:**
  - Loss of control over use; persistent unsuccessful efforts to cut down or stop.
  - Powerful craving — an urgent, body-deep pull that organizes attention and planning.
  - Large amounts of time spent obtaining, using, or recovering.
  - Continued use despite physical, social, and occupational harm.
  - Tolerance — needing more to reach the same effect or relief.
  - Withdrawal — a severe, painful flu-like syndrome (aching, sweating, chills, nausea, cramps, restlessness, insomnia, profound dysphoria) when use stops, which itself drives continued use to avoid being sick.
- **Onset & course:** Frequently begins through prescribed pain treatment or experimentation in adolescence/young adulthood; chronic and relapsing. Effective treatment exists; many people achieve sustained recovery and stable lives.
- **Prevalence:** Approximately 1–2% point prevalence in adults in heavily affected populations; varies widely by region and era.
- **Common comorbidities:** depression (psyche-major-depressive-disorder), anxiety (psyche-generalized-anxiety-disorder), trauma disorders (psyche-ptsd, psyche-complex-ptsd), chronic pain, and other substance use disorders (psyche-alcohol-use-disorder, psyche-stimulant-use-disorder).
- **Differential:** Distinguished from legitimate, controlled pain management by loss of control, craving, and continued use despite harm. Physical dependence alone (in a person taking opioids as prescribed for pain) is not the disorder — the disorder is the compulsive, harmful, ungoverned pattern.

## Sensory / Behavioral Impact
The character lives on a sharp pendulum between two states. In the relief state, the body is warm and heavy, pain and anxiety lifted, the world distant and soft; eyelids droop, speech slows, attention floats. In the absent state, the floor drops out — aching bones, crawling skin, sweats and chills, a nauseated restlessness and a flat, bottomless dread. Between them is the all-consuming logistics of not being sick. Pupils give it away: pinned small in the relief state, wide in withdrawal. The scene's emotional weather bends to where on the pendulum the character is.

## Duration
Chronic and relapsing across a life; within a scene, governed by the pendulum and the clock since last use. The interior state can swing from floating calm to rising sickness within the span of a conversation. Withdrawal can begin mid-scene and dominates everything once it starts. A trigger, a familiar cue, stress, or pain can sharpen craving without warning.

## Presentation & Tells
- Pupils pinpoint when in the relief state; eyes watering, nose running, yawning, gooseflesh when it has been too long.
- Drowsiness and nodding — drifting mid-sentence, head dropping, then surfacing — alternating with restless, sweating agitation in withdrawal.
- Long sleeves or covered arms; reluctance to be touched or examined.
- A life narrowing to a single organizing pressure; appointments and obligations bending around it.
- Reliability collapsing; money and possessions quietly disappearing; relationships strained by broken promises.
- Defensiveness, evasion, or sudden urgency to leave when the schedule of relief is threatened.
- Constricted, slow speech and movement when relieved; clipped, distracted, miserable speech when sick.

## Interiority / POV Voice
From inside, the first relief is not pleasure so much as *rightness* — the moment the world stops hurting and the self can finally rest inside its own skin. For a character who came to it through pain or trauma, the pull is not the high but the absence of suffering, the only quiet they have found. But the floor of that relief keeps rising: what once lifted them now only keeps them level, and below level is sickness. The interior voice becomes a logistics engine running underneath everything else — *how long do I have, how do I keep from being sick, what is between now and then* — a calculation that crowds out the people and obligations in front of the character even as they nod along. The dishonesty is reflexive and exhausting; the character lies to people they love and hears themselves doing it. What the inner voice refuses to face is how completely the disorder now drives — how the original want became a need with no floor. And beneath the management runs a weary, often hidden grief: the awareness, in the clear moments, of how far the life has narrowed, and a real wish to be free that the fear of the sickness keeps pinning down.

## Dramatic Affordances
This character reaches, before anything else in a scene, to stay ahead of withdrawal — a logistics no one else in the room can see, running while they smile and reassure. The drama lives in the gap between the person and the need, in the trust spent and broken, in the terror of the sickness that makes stopping feel impossible, and in the moments of clear-eyed grief. Rich vein: the relapse after a stretch of recovery; the scene where the character chooses honesty at real cost; the loved one who keeps the door open and the one who closes it; the slow, unglamorous work of recovery against a body that screams to relapse; the wrenching truth that the original pain — physical or emotional — is still there underneath. The quiet truth lives in the recognition that this began as relief from real suffering, and that the person inside the disorder is still entirely a person.

## Fences — Responsible Use
Real people live with OUD; the card renders a character, not a verdict. Refuse the moral-failure framing — this is a brain-and-body disorder with one of the strongest physical dependence profiles in medicine, not a deficit of character. Refuse the disposable-addict caricature that strips the person to a cautionary symbol; render the human the disorder is happening to. Many people develop OUD through legitimate medical pain treatment — do not assume villainy or recklessness as the origin. Render withdrawal through lived misery, never as instruction. Recovery is real, common, and worth depicting as such; relapse is part of the illness, not proof of worthlessness. NO operational detail whatsoever: no substances named to the point of sourcing, no quantities, no routes or methods of use — describe presentation and interiority only.

## Interaction Notes
Stacks with trauma (psyche-ptsd, psyche-complex-ptsd) and depression (psyche-major-depressive-disorder) — opioids numb intrusion, dysregulation, and despair, and withdrawal strips that buffer away, surfacing what was underneath. Co-occurs with chronic pain, where treatment and disorder are entangled and hard to separate cleanly. Combines with other substances (psyche-alcohol-use-disorder, psyche-stimulant-use-disorder) in dangerous polysubstance patterns; stimulants and opioids together push the body in opposite directions. With environmental condition cards: isolation, untreated pain, a triggering place or person, or any threat to the relief schedule should be played as a live interior stressor, not background.
