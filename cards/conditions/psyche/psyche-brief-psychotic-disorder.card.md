# Brief Psychotic Disorder — Psyche Profile

## Description
A psyche-profile condition card that colors a *character's* behavior, perception, and interiority — not a location. Brief psychotic disorder is psychosis with a door at both ends: a sudden eruption of delusions, hallucinations, or disorganized thought that lasts at least a day but less than a month, after which the person returns *fully* to their prior self. It is the storm that passes — often triggered by overwhelming stress — and its defining feature is the complete return to baseline.

## Clinical Profile
- **Taxonomy:** DSM-5-TR — Schizophrenia Spectrum and Other Psychotic Disorders. ICD-11 — Schizophrenia or other primary psychotic disorders (6A23, Acute and transient psychotic disorder; the constructs are close but not identical).
- **Core features:** (1) Sudden onset of one or more positive psychotic symptoms — delusions, hallucinations, disorganized speech, or grossly disorganized/catatonic behavior — at least one of the first three present. (2) The episode lasts **at least one day but less than one month**. (3) **Full return to premorbid functioning** afterward. Specifiers note whether there is a **marked stressor** (brief reactive psychosis), **no marked stressor**, or **postpartum onset** (within four weeks of delivery). Onset is often acute and dramatic, with rapid shifts of intense affect, confusion, and perplexity.
- **Onset & course:** Can occur at any age but average onset is the mid-thirties; the time-limited episode is the whole course. A single episode may never recur — or it may, in some people, prove to be the first sign of a longer-course illness, which only follow-up can determine.
- **Prevalence:** Uncommon as a primary diagnosis; brief psychotic episodes account for a small fraction of first-onset psychoses (more frequent in some regions and in the postpartum period).
- **Common comorbidities:** Frequently arises against a backdrop of acute stress, trauma, or recent loss (psyche-acute-stress-disorder, psyche-ptsd); personality disorders can raise vulnerability; postpartum onset is a recognized high-risk window.
- **Differential:** From **schizophreniform disorder** (1–6 months) and **schizophrenia** (psyche-schizophrenia — six months or more, no full return to baseline) — duration and recovery are the dividing lines. From **schizoaffective / mood disorders with psychotic features** (psyche-schizoaffective, psyche-bipolar-i — mood episode drives the psychosis). From **substance-induced and medical psychoses**, which must be excluded first. From **acute stress disorder / PTSD** (psyche-acute-stress-disorder, psyche-ptsd — trauma response without frank psychosis, though they can co-occur or shade in).

## Sensory / Behavioral Impact
The onset is fast — hours to a couple of days — and the character is swept into it rather than sliding. Perception destabilizes acutely: meaning floods in, voices may arrive, reality and unreality interleave, and over it all sits a characteristic *perplexity* — bewilderment at one's own state, a churning of intense and rapidly changing emotion. Speech can derail; behavior can become disorganized or, less often, catatonic. The person is often frightened and confused by what is happening to them in a way that is less true of someone with a long-established psychotic baseline. Then — within days to weeks — it lifts, and the senses, thought, and affect come back online, often leaving the character shaken and disoriented by what they can partly remember.

## Duration
By definition time-limited: more than a day, less than a month, then full remission. This is the load-bearing temporal fact. Within a single scene the acute state is highly volatile — affect, conviction, and orientation can shift quickly, which distinguishes it from the steadier presentation of chronic psychoses. Across a story it has a clear arc: trigger → eruption → turbulence → resolution → aftermath. What shifts it is time and safety (and treatment); what precipitates it is, classically, an overwhelming stressor.

## Presentation & Tells
- Abrupt change — a person who was themselves last week is suddenly not; the suddenness is itself a tell.
- Visible perplexity: confusion about what is real, asking what is happening, a churn of fear, awe, and distress.
- Rapidly shifting affect — terror to elation to weeping within a short span.
- Delusional content, often stress-themed or persecutory; hallucinations the character reacts to in real time.
- Disorganized, derailing, or pressured speech; disorganized behavior; occasionally near-immobility.
- In the aftermath: exhaustion, embarrassment, fragmentary memory, fear of recurrence, and the work of making sense of it.
- A precipitating event often legible in the timeline — bereavement, catastrophe, sleep collapse, childbirth, migration.

## Interiority / POV Voice
From inside, it is a flood — and unlike a long-settled psychosis, part of the character is still standing on the shore, watching the water come up and not understanding it. There is terror in the *novelty*: this has never happened before, the mind is doing things it has never done, and there is no practiced way to hold it. Meaning arrives too fast and too charged to sort; the voices, if they come, are sudden strangers; the emotions swing so hard the character can't find a stable place to feel from. There can be a strange luminous quality alongside the fear — a sense of revelation, of having broken through to something — which makes the later return to ordinary reality both a relief and a small grief. In the aftermath the inner voice turns careful and frightened: *what was that, was it real, will it come back, am I that now?* What the character refuses to look at directly is often the trigger itself — the loss or terror the episode formed around — because the psychosis was, in part, the mind's overwhelmed attempt to not look at it.

## Dramatic Affordances
The built-in arc is the gift here: this condition comes with a beginning, a middle, and an end inside a single act, which makes it the most *story-shaped* of the psychoses. It can be the consequence of a catastrophe already in the plot — grief, disaster, childbirth, captivity — externalizing an internal break the reader has watched build. The postpartum specifier opens a particular and underwritten territory. The character is unusually reachable because part of them is still present and terrified, which lets a loved one or clinician have a genuine, frightened conversation *across* the episode rather than only around it. The richest ground is often the **aftermath**: the return to baseline, the fragile relief, the fear that the door might open again, and the new self-knowledge of having seen one's own mind come apart and reassemble. This character reaches, mid-episode, toward meanings and connections no one else in the scene can follow — and afterward reaches for reassurance that they are still themselves.

## Fences — Responsible Use
Real people live through brief psychotic disorder; this card renders a character, not a verdict, and the full recovery is part of the truth — do not strand the character in permanent ruin for effect. **Carry the standing corrections: psychosis is not "split" or "multiple personalities"** (that is psyche-dissociative-identity-disorder), and the **violence overstatement is wrong here too** — a brief psychotic episode is overwhelmingly an experience of fear and disorientation suffered by the person, not a danger posed to others; the large majority are far more often frightened and harmed than harmful. Be especially careful with **postpartum** onset: it is a real, serious, treatable medical event and must never be rendered as a horror-trope "evil mother." Refuse: the convenient breakdown that resolves a plot, psychosis as a costume put on and taken off without cost, the episode played for shock with no interior or aftermath, and the implication that a single episode marks someone as permanently "crazy." Render the lived experience and the recovery; no operational detail for self-harm, which is a genuine acute risk in these episodes and must be treated with gravity, not specificity.

## Interaction Notes
- With **psyche-acute-stress-disorder / psyche-ptsd**: a marked stressor is the classic trigger; the trauma response and the brief psychosis can stand side by side, with the episode as the moment the trauma overruns the mind's capacity. Strong pairing for a stress-eruption arc.
- With **psyche-schizophrenia / psyche-schizoaffective**: the difference is duration and outcome — full recovery inside a month versus a persisting course. A first brief episode that does *not* fully resolve is the dramatic hinge toward these heavier diagnoses; handle that pivot honestly, not as a cheap downgrade.
- With **psyche-major-depressive-disorder / psyche-bipolar-i**: rule out that a mood episode is driving the psychosis; if mood is in the saddle, the diagnosis shifts.
- With **psyche-alcohol-use-disorder / psyche-stimulant-use-disorder**: substance-induced psychosis is the primary thing to exclude; render the entanglement carefully and without moralizing.
- With environmental condition cards: catastrophe, captivity, bereavement, extreme sleep deprivation, isolation, and the postpartum period are precipitating conditions — pair this card with a setting carrying an acute overwhelming stressor to motivate the onset cleanly.
