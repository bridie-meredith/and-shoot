# Reactive Attachment Disorder — Psyche Profile

## Description
A psyche-profile condition card. It colors a *character's* behavior, perception, and interiority — not a location. Reactive Attachment Disorder arises in a young child who never had a reliable caregiver to attach to: the part of a person that learns to reach for comfort never got the chance to form, so the child doesn't seek soothing, doesn't accept it, and meets warmth with a closed door.

## Clinical Profile
- **Taxonomy:** DSM-5-TR — Trauma- and Stressor-Related Disorders (code 313.89 / F94.1). ICD-11 — Disorders specifically associated with stress; Reactive attachment disorder (6B44).
- **Core features:**
  - A consistent pattern of inhibited, emotionally withdrawn behavior toward caregivers — rarely seeks or responds to comfort when distressed.
  - Persistent social and emotional disturbance: minimal social/emotional responsiveness, limited positive affect, unexplained irritability, sadness, or fearfulness even in non-threatening interactions.
  - A history of **insufficient care** — neglect, deprivation, repeated changes of caregiver, or rearing in settings that prevented selective attachments (institutions).
  - The disturbance is evident before age 5; the child has a developmental age of at least 9 months (capable of forming attachments).
  - Does NOT meet criteria for autism spectrum disorder.
- **Onset & course:** roots in infancy/early childhood under pathogenic care. With early intervention and a stable, responsive caregiver, attachment capacity can substantially recover; without it, the relational patterns can echo into later life as attachment difficulties and complex trauma.
- **Prevalence:** rare in the general population (well under 1%); elevated among children with histories of severe neglect, foster care, and institutional rearing.
- **Common comorbidities:** developmental and cognitive delays from deprivation; depression (psyche-major-depressive-disorder); later, the relational template can feed complex PTSD (psyche-complex-ptsd).
- **Differential:** from disinhibited social engagement disorder (psyche-disinhibited-social-engagement-disorder) — same deprivation root, opposite presentation: RAD *withholds* and withdraws; DSED is *indiscriminately* over-familiar with strangers. From autism (psyche-autism-spectrum) — RAD's social deficit traces to deprivation, not neurodevelopment, and the capacity for attachment can grow with care.

## Sensory / Behavioral Impact
The child (or the adult shaped by it) does not orient toward comfort. When hurt or frightened, they don't run to the safe adult; they go still, or self-soothe alone, or push the adult away. Warmth is met with watchfulness rather than relief. Positive affect is thin — they don't light up at a returning caregiver. There's a baseline wariness and a tendency toward unexplained sadness, fear, or irritability that flares in moments that should feel safe. Touch and tenderness can read as intrusion rather than care.

## Duration
Rooted and early-laid, but malleable with consistent responsive care — this is one of the more *changeable* conditions if a reliable attachment figure persists. Within a scene, the closed door is the default and it does not open easily; an attempt at comfort can make the character withdraw *further* before any softening. A trusted figure who has earned ground over time may get a flicker through; a stranger's warmth almost never will. Pressure, novelty, or perceived demand for closeness shuts it tighter.

## Presentation & Tells
- Doesn't seek comfort when hurt; goes quiet, manages alone, or turns away.
- Stiffens or pulls back from hugs and tenderness; tolerates rather than accepts.
- Watches caregivers warily; little of the easy delight a securely attached child shows.
- Flat or muted positive affect; doesn't "light up."
- Bouts of unexplained irritability, sadness, or fear in safe, gentle moments.
- Self-reliant past their years — solves their own distress because no one else reliably did.
- A guarded, evaluating quality to how they meet a new adult.

## Interiority / POV Voice
Comfort is not a place the character knows to go, because there was never a reliable address for it. When something hurts, the impulse isn't *find the safe person* — that wire was never laid; the impulse is *handle it, alone, the way it has always been handled.* A reaching hand is a question the character can't answer and doesn't trust: what does this person want, what will it cost, and what happens when they leave like the others did. Tenderness lands as pressure. The self that does the watching is older than the body — it learned early that the only dependable thing was itself, and it guards that conclusion like the last dry match. Underneath, sometimes, is a want with no name and no map: the ache for exactly the thing the character cannot let in.

## Dramatic Affordances
This character refuses the one thing the scene keeps offering — comfort, closeness, being held — and that refusal is itself the story. They reach for self-sufficiency past all reason, for distance, for the exits in any relationship. The drama lives in the would-be caregiver who keeps showing up and keeps being turned away, and the slow, asymmetric, hard-won inch of trust that is worth more than a hundred easy bonds. The quiet truth lives in the moment the character almost lets someone in and then, terrified, slams the door — and in the rare, earned moment they don't. They make the unreachable child, the self-raised loner, the partner who cannot be loved at the speed everyone else expects.

## Fences — Responsible Use
RAD comes from a caregiving environment that failed the child — it is a wound, never the child's fault and not "coldness" or a bad nature. Avoid the "evil unbondable child" horror trope; this is one of the most stigmatized and most misrepresented child conditions, and the demonization causes real harm to real children in foster and adoptive systems. The withdrawal is protective, not malicious. **It is also treatable** — a stable, patient, responsive caregiver can change the trajectory, so don't render it as a life sentence. Never depict the originating neglect in operational detail. The card renders a guarded character who learned not to reach, not a verdict on their capacity to love.

## Interaction Notes
The mirror-twin of disinhibited social engagement disorder (psyche-disinhibited-social-engagement-disorder) — same deprivation root, opposite door (withdraw vs. over-approach); the two are rarely staged in one character but illuminate each other. Feeds forward into complex PTSD (psyche-complex-ptsd) and adult attachment difficulty as the early template hardens. Pairs with developmental delay and depression (psyche-major-depressive-disorder) from the same deprived environment. Environmental condition cards matter enormously: a *stable, warm, consistent* setting is the lever that opens the door over time, while instability, new caregivers, or pressure for closeness drive the withdrawal harder within a scene.
