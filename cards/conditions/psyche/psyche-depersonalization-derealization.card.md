# Depersonalization/Derealization Disorder — Psyche Profile

## Description
This is a psyche-profile condition card: it colors a *character's* behavior, perception, and interiority, not a location. Depersonalization/Derealization Disorder (DPDR) is persistent or recurrent unreality — of the self (depersonalization: feeling detached, robotic, like a watcher of one's own life) and/or of the world (derealization: surroundings seeming dreamlike, foggy, flat, or unreal) — while reality-testing stays intact. At its essence it is being awake inside your life but standing one pane of glass away from it.

## Clinical Profile
- **Taxonomy:** DSM-5-TR — Dissociative Disorders (Depersonalization/Derealization Disorder, 300.6). ICD-11 — Dissociative Disorders, "Depersonalization-derealization disorder" (6B66).
- **Core features:** **depersonalization** — feeling detached from, or an outside observer of, one's thoughts, feelings, body, or actions; emotional or physical numbing; sense of being unreal or automated; **derealization** — surroundings experienced as unreal, dreamlike, foggy, lifeless, or visually distorted (flat, too-bright, walled-off); crucially, **reality-testing remains intact** — the person knows the unreality is a feeling, not a fact (this distinguishes it from psychosis); the experience is distressing and often frightening precisely because it is recognized as wrong.
- **Onset & course:** typically begins in adolescence or early adulthood (mean onset around 16); often triggered by severe stress, trauma, panic, exhaustion, or substance experiences. Course is frequently chronic and continuous, though it can be episodic and is often unrecognized for years.
- **Prevalence:** transient depersonalization/derealization is extremely common (roughly half of all people at least once); the persistent, impairing *disorder* affects approximately 1–2% of the population.
- **Common comorbidities:** anxiety disorders (especially panic and generalized anxiety); depression; PTSD; the other dissociative disorders. (See `psyche-panic-disorder`, `psyche-generalized-anxiety-disorder`, `psyche-ptsd`, `psyche-dissociative-identity-disorder`, `psyche-dissociative-amnesia`.)
- **Differential:** distinguished from psychosis (in DPDR reality-testing is preserved — the person knows things only *feel* unreal), from panic (where the feelings are usually brief and acute rather than persistent), from substance/medication effects and seizure activity (which must be ruled out), and from the normal, fleeting unreality everyone experiences under stress.

## Sensory / Behavioral Impact
The character experiences themselves and the world at a remove. Their own voice may sound like a recording; their hands may look like they belong to someone else; emotions register as facts they know rather than feelings they feel. The world can go foggy, two-dimensional, drained of color or oddly over-lit, and familiar places feel subtly counterfeit. This is exhausting and frightening — much of the character's energy goes into appearing normal while privately checking whether they still exist. Concentration suffers; time can feel rubbery. Outwardly they may look composed, even flat, while inside they are scanning for proof of reality.

## Duration
Often chronic and continuous — a steady background haze the character has learned to live behind — but it waxes and wanes with stress, fatigue, and anxiety. Episodes can also be discrete, spiking under panic or trauma cues and easing with grounding. Within a scene it is usually present as an atmosphere rather than an event; it deepens with threat, crowding, sleep-deprivation, or a triggering reminder, and lifts somewhat with sensory grounding, movement, warmth, or trusted contact.

## Presentation & Tells
- A half-second lag — touching a surface, looking at their hands, as if confirming things are real.
- Flat or muted affect that doesn't match the stakes; emotions reported rather than shown.
- Describing the world in unreal terms: "like a dream," "like behind glass," "like a set," "far away."
- Pinching, gripping, cold water, sharp sensations sought to "feel something" or re-anchor.
- Tired, glazed, or faraway expression; asking others to confirm what just happened was real.
- Articulate, intact, and oriented — which can make the distress invisible and easy to dismiss.

## Interiority / POV Voice
From inside, the defining note is doubled awareness: the narrator is fully oriented and fully estranged at once. They know the chair is real, the friend is real, the grief is real — and none of it lands; it arrives muffled, as information about a life rather than the life itself. The inner voice runs a constant, weary reality-check: *that's my hand, that's my voice, this is happening, I am here.* There is a horror specific to this condition — not of monsters but of the suspicion that the self has thinned to a watcher, that feeling has gone offline and may not come back. The narrator often cannot explain it and dreads sounding insane, so they translate the unreality into something sayable or say nothing. There is loneliness in being present at every moment of your life while attending it from the doorway, and there is a strange, dry clarity too — the watcher sees the scene very precisely, just from the wrong side of the glass.

## Dramatic Affordances
The condition affords a narrator who is acutely observant and affectively numbed — a cool, exact eye that registers everything and is moved by nothing, until grounding cracks the glass and feeling floods back. It opens scenes of quiet desperation hidden behind composure, the loneliness of an invisible symptom, and the search for one reliable anchor — a person, a ritual, a sensation — that makes the world solid again. It pairs naturally with panic and trauma plots, where unreality is the mind's circuit-breaker. The quiet truth lives in the longing to feel one's own life directly, and in the moment something finally gets through.

## Fences — Responsible Use
Real people live with DPDR; this card renders a character, not a verdict. Refuse these distortions:
- **It is not psychosis and not a "break from reality."** The person knows the unreality is a feeling; reality-testing is intact. Do not write it as delusion, hallucination, or "losing their mind."
- **It is not coldness, fakery, or not caring.** The numbness is a symptom, not a character flaw or a manipulation; the flat affect often hides acute distress.
- **It is not the same as DID.** The "detached observer" feeling is not a separate personality; avoid collapsing the dissociative disorders into one another.
- **Don't romanticize the haze** as edgy detachment — render the exhaustion and fear under it.
- NO operational detail (including grounding/coping rendered as instruction); show the lived experience and outward presentation, never a how-to manual.

## Interaction Notes
Stacks tightly with `psyche-panic-disorder` and `psyche-generalized-anxiety-disorder` — unreality is a common product of acute fear, and fear of the unreality feeds a self-reinforcing loop. With `psyche-ptsd` it functions as a dissociative shield around trauma material. Within the dissociative spectrum it sits alongside `psyche-dissociative-amnesia` (missing time) and is distinct from, but can co-occur with, `psyche-dissociative-identity-disorder` (where depersonalization/derealization commonly accompany switching). Environmentally, condition cards establishing sensory overload, fluorescent or sterile spaces, sleep-deprivation, crowding, or trauma cues will deepen the unreality; cards establishing strong, specific sensory grounding, warmth, rhythm, and trusted presence will thin it.
