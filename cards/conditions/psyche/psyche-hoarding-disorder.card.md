# Hoarding Disorder — Psyche Profile

## Description
This is a psyche-profile condition card. It colors a *character* — their behavior, perception, and interiority — not a location. Hoarding Disorder is a persistent difficulty discarding possessions regardless of value, driven by a perceived need to save them and by distress at the thought of letting them go, resulting in accumulation that congests and compromises living spaces.

## Clinical Profile
- **Taxonomy:** DSM-5-TR — Obsessive-Compulsive and Related Disorders. ICD-11 — Obsessive-compulsive or related disorders (Hoarding disorder, 6B24).
- **Core features:**
  - Persistent difficulty parting with possessions because of a perceived need to keep them.
  - The difficulty is driven by attachment, anticipated usefulness, fear of waste, or distress at discarding — not by mere disorganization.
  - Accumulation congests active living areas to the point their intended use is compromised (a bed unsleepable-in, a stove unusable, a path through stacks).
  - Significant distress or impairment, including for household members, and sometimes safety/sanitation/eviction risk.
  - Specifier for excessive *acquisition* (buying, free-collecting) is common but not required.
  - Insight ranges from good to poor/absent — some recognize the problem; some do not see the volume as excessive.
- **Onset & course:** Symptoms often emerge in adolescence and worsen across decades; clinically significant most often in midlife and later. Chronic and typically progressive if untreated.
- **Prevalence:** Approximately 2–6%, rising with age.
- **Common comorbidities:** Depression, anxiety disorders, ADHD (attention/organization), OCD (a minority — distinct condition), social isolation.
- **Differential:** Distinguish from OCD-driven saving (in OCD, saving is tied to intrusive fears/rituals and is *ego-dystonic*; in hoarding, attachment is comfort-seeking and often ego-syntonic); from normal collecting (collections are organized, displayed, bounded; hoarding congests and distresses); from accumulation due to depression's inertia or ADHD's disorganization (which can co-occur and compound).

## Sensory / Behavioral Impact
The character reads a room as a field of potential and obligation — objects call out their possible futures, their histories, the waste it would be to lose them. Discarding is felt as loss, even physical wrench. Energy drains in the face of the volume; organizing stalls because every item demands a decision and decisions are agonizing. Affect can be warm and defensive about the things, anxious when they're touched, ashamed when the space is seen. Movement is shaped by the terrain — sidling through gaps, surfaces unusable, a single cleared chair the only seat.

## Duration
Chronic and usually progressive as a condition; the *acute distress* spikes situationally — when someone proposes discarding, touches the possessions, or threatens a clear-out, and when an outsider sees the space. A scene can turn on a relative reaching for a trash bag. Between such pressures the character may be calm, even content among the things; the disorder is most visible at the threshold of loss or exposure.

## Presentation & Tells
- Living space congested with stacks, bags, boxes; furniture and rooms unusable for their purpose; narrow paths between piles.
- Acute distress, bargaining, or anger when others suggest discarding or move an item — "I need that," "I was going to use it," "you can't throw that away."
- Bringing things home — free items, others' discards, bulk purchases, papers "to read."
- "I'll sort it" promises that never resolve; decisions deferred indefinitely.
- Shame-driven secrecy: declining visitors, meeting people elsewhere, vague excuses about the house.
- Detailed stories attached to ordinary objects; their value defended on history, potential use, or principle against waste.

## Interiority / POV Voice
Everything here is *something*. That's the part they never get. The cracked mug could be repaired, the cord might fit something one day, the stack of papers holds a thing you'll need the moment you toss it — and you've been right before, you've reached into the pile and found exactly the receipt, the photo, the part, and that vindication is fuel. To discard is to throw away a future use, a memory, a possibility, and the waste of it sits in your chest like grief. People walk in and see mess; you see a map, a held life, and their flinch lands as judgment of *you*, not the stuff. So you stop letting them in. The volume is its own weight — you know it's too much, somewhere you know — but to start is to face item after item, each one a small decision that costs more than you have, and so the chair you cleared stays the only chair, and you sit in it among everything you couldn't bear to lose, and it's almost peaceful and almost unbearable at once.

## Dramatic Affordances
Hoarding opens scenes of love braided with grief — a child returning to a parent's congested home, a partner negotiating a single shared room back into use, the social worker at the door. It powers conflict over exactly one bag, one shelf, one decision, where the stakes are felt as enormous to one person and trivial to the other. It dramatizes shame, isolation, and the secret labor of keeping people out. The drama lives in the threshold moment — a hand on an object, the question *can I throw this away* — and in care that wants to help colliding with a self that experiences the help as theft. The quiet truth lives in what each object holds: not junk, but a person's attempt to keep hold of meaning, safety, and time.

## Fences — Responsible Use
Hoarding is not laziness, filth-for-its-own-sake, or a failure of character — it is a recognized disorder with real emotional logic, and the "gross-out spectacle" framing common in media is a core misrepresentation to refuse. Do not reduce the person to their mess or play the squalor for disgust; the suffering and the meaning are the point. The objects matter to the character — render the attachment, not just the volume. Forced clear-outs are typically traumatic and rarely curative; do not write a bin-bag montage as a fix. Real people live with this, often alongside depression and isolation; the card renders a character, not a verdict. NO operational framing of sanitation/safety hazards as instructive detail.

## Interaction Notes
Distinct from but adjacent to `psyche-ocd` (a minority of hoarding is OCD-driven; the saving logic differs — fear/ritual vs. attachment). Stacks with `psyche-major-depressive-disorder` (inertia and hopelessness deepen the accumulation and the inability to start) and `psyche-generalized-anxiety-disorder` (anticipatory "what if I need it / what if discarding causes harm"). With `psyche-adhd`, attention and organization deficits compound the volume and the stalled sorting. Environmental condition cards interact strongly: the set *is* the symptom — clutter, congestion, blocked exits, disused furniture, and the social pressure of an outside visitor are the condition made physical.
