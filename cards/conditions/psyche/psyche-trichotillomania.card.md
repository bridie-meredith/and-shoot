# Trichotillomania (Hair-Pulling Disorder) — Psyche Profile

## Description
This is a psyche-profile condition card. It colors a *character* — their behavior, perception, and interiority — not a location. Trichotillomania is a body-focused repetitive behavior in which a person recurrently pulls out their own hair, resulting in hair loss, despite repeated attempts to stop. It sits in tension between relief and distress, and is most often a private, lived struggle rather than anything dramatic from the outside.

## Clinical Profile
- **Taxonomy:** DSM-5-TR — Obsessive-Compulsive and Related Disorders (Trichotillomania / Hair-Pulling Disorder). ICD-11 — Obsessive-compulsive or related disorders (Body-focused repetitive behavior disorders, 6B25).
- **Core features:**
  - Recurrent pulling of one's own hair leading to noticeable hair loss.
  - Repeated attempts to decrease or stop the pulling.
  - Clinically significant distress or impairment (often social, from shame and concealment).
  - Not better explained by another medical or psychiatric condition.
  - Common sites: scalp, eyebrows, eyelashes; pulling can be "focused" (deliberate, tension-relieving) or "automatic" (out of awareness, e.g. while reading or watching).
  - Often preceded by tension or an urge and followed by relief, gratification, or release — but also by shame.
- **Onset & course:** Typically around the onset of puberty (early adolescence). Chronic with a waxing-waning course; can fluctuate with stress over years.
- **Prevalence:** Approximately 1–2% lifetime; more often reported in females in clinical samples.
- **Common comorbidities:** Skin-picking (excoriation), anxiety and depressive disorders, OCD, body dysmorphic disorder.
- **Differential:** Distinguish from OCD (pulling is not driven by an intrusive obsession or aimed at preventing a feared event; it relieves tension/urge); from a primary dermatological cause of hair loss; from a psychotic-driven behavior; from typical grooming.

## Sensory / Behavioral Impact
The character's hand drifts to the body almost on its own — fingers exploring the scalp, the brow, the lashes, hunting by feel for a particular hair (coarse, out of place, "wrong"). A background tension builds and the act discharges it; there's a brief, real relief that the character does not fully choose and cannot easily explain. Awareness varies — sometimes deliberate, sometimes a surfacing from a fog mid-task. Affect afterward is often shame and frustration at having done it again. Attention can be split: the hand busy, the mind elsewhere, then a snap back to what's been done.

## Duration
Chronic as a condition; the *urge* and the behavior are highly state-dependent within a scene. Boredom, concentration (reading, screens, the car), stress, and idle hands raise it; engagement, occupied hands, and awareness lower it. A character can pass an absorbing scene untouched, then drift into pulling in a quiet beat. Concealment effort (a hat, a hand kept down) is a continuous background load that the scene can pressure.

## Presentation & Tells
- Thinning patches, an uneven hairline, sparse or missing eyebrows or eyelashes — often partly concealed.
- A hand resting at the scalp, brow, or lash line; fingers searching or twirling a strand; an absent, rhythmic exploring.
- Concealment: hats, scarves, particular hairstyles, drawn-on brows, avoidance of wind, water, bright light, or anything that exposes the area.
- A flicker of being "caught" — the hand dropping fast, a change of subject, a hand tucked away.
- Pulling that surfaces in idle or absorbed moments (reading, watching, on the phone) without the character first noticing.
- Avoidance of haircuts, swimming, intimacy, or close-up attention to the head and face.

## Interiority / POV Voice
Your hand knows the way without asking you. You're reading, or you're listening to someone, and at some point — you couldn't say when — your fingers are already up there, walking the scalp for the one that feels wrong, coarser, off, and finding it is its own small satisfaction, a release you didn't decide to want. Then it's done, and there's the relief, brief and real, and right behind it the drop: *again*. You told yourself this morning, not today. You meant it. The promise dissolves in the quiet of an ordinary afternoon. The shame isn't the pulling exactly — it's the concealing, the geometry of hats and partings and which side of the room has the light, the whole hidden architecture of not being seen. People who don't have it think *just stop*, as if you hadn't tried, as if wanting to had ever been the missing piece. The hardest part is that it isn't dramatic. It's small and constant and yours, a private negotiation with your own hands that nobody in the room can see you losing.

## Dramatic Affordances
Trichotillomania opens scenes of quiet, hidden struggle and the labor of concealment — a character whose composure is real but maintained at a cost no one in the scene registers. It powers intimacy beats: the vulnerability of being seen without the hat, a partner who notices and how they respond, the terror and relief of being known. It dramatizes the gap between *just stop* and the lived impossibility of stopping by will alone. The drama lives in the small moments — the hand caught and dropped, the changed subject, the morning's broken promise — and in shame meeting (or failing to meet) compassion. The quiet truth lives in how ordinary and how persistent it is, and in a person managing it day after day without applause.

## Fences — Responsible Use
Trichotillomania is not attention-seeking, not "just a bad habit," and not a sign of weak will — it is a recognized disorder that resists effort to stop, and "why don't you just quit" is exactly the harmful response to avoid putting in a sympathetic character's mouth uncorrected. It is rarely dramatic from the outside; resist sensationalizing it. Render *presentation and lived experience* — the urge, the relief, the shame, the concealment — never operational technique or instructional detail; describe that pulling happens and how it feels and looks, not how to do it. The common comorbid shame and isolation deserve gravity, not spectacle. Real people live with this and manage full lives around it; the card renders a character, not a verdict.

## Interaction Notes
Closely paired with `psyche-excoriation` — the two body-focused repetitive behaviors frequently co-occur and share the tension-relief-shame architecture; a character may have both. Adjacent to `psyche-ocd` (same DSM family, different mechanism — urge-relief, not obsession-ritual) and to `psyche-body-dysmorphic-disorder` (when appearance distress about the affected area compounds the concealment). Stress and anxiety conditions like `psyche-generalized-anxiety-disorder` raise the urge load. Environmental condition cards interact through idle states: long quiet scenes, reading or screen time, the car, waiting — any low-stimulation, hands-free setting raises the likelihood the behavior surfaces; high-engagement or hands-occupied settings lower it.
