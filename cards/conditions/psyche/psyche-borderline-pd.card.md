# Borderline Personality Disorder — Psyche Profile

## Description
A psyche-profile condition card that colors a *character's* behavior, perception, and interiority — not a location. Borderline Personality Disorder (BPD) is a pervasive pattern of instability in relationships, self-image, and affect, with marked impulsivity and a deep, organizing terror of abandonment. The essence in one line: emotions arrive at full volume and the self is felt as something that could come apart, so closeness is both the only relief and the deepest threat.

## Clinical Profile
- **Taxonomy:** DSM-5-TR — Personality Disorders, Cluster B (the "dramatic/erratic" cluster). ICD-11 dimensionalizes personality disorder (6D10) with a recognized **Borderline pattern** specifier (6D11.5) layered over severity; prominent **Negative Affectivity** and **Disinhibition** traits.
- **Core features:**
  - Frantic efforts to avoid real or imagined abandonment.
  - Unstable, intense relationships swinging between idealization and devaluation ("splitting").
  - Identity disturbance — an unstable, shifting sense of self.
  - Impulsivity in potentially self-damaging areas (covered here at the level of lived experience, not method).
  - Recurrent self-harm or suicidal behavior/threats (named clinically; see Fences — no operational detail).
  - Affective instability — intense moods shifting over hours, not days.
  - Chronic emptiness; intense, hard-to-control anger; transient stress-related paranoia or dissociation.
- **Onset & course:** Emerges by adolescence/early adulthood. Strongly associated with developmental trauma and invalidating environments. Course is more hopeful than its reputation: symptoms often **attenuate substantially with age and treatment**, and evidence-based therapies (DBT, MBT, others) produce real, durable improvement. Not a life sentence.
- **Prevalence:** Approximately 1.5–3% of the general population; higher in clinical settings.
- **Common comorbidities:** Depressive and anxiety conditions, **psyche-complex-ptsd** (substantial overlap and debate over boundaries), substance use, eating disorders, and other Cluster B patterns.
- **Differential:** Differs from **psyche-bipolar-ii** — BPD mood shifts are rapid (hours), reactive to interpersonal triggers, and tied to abandonment fears, whereas bipolar episodes are sustained (days–weeks) and less interpersonally cued. Differs from **psyche-complex-ptsd** by the centrality of identity disturbance and abandonment terror, though the two heavily overlap. The anger and impulsivity differ from antisocial patterns by being driven by attachment pain rather than instrumental disregard.

## Sensory / Behavioral Impact
The character feels at high gain. Emotional weather arrives fast and total — a tone of voice, a delayed reply, a closed door can flood the whole system in seconds. Affect swings visibly across a scene: warmth to fury to despair to numb emptiness, each fully inhabited while it lasts. Attention locks onto attachment cues — who is pulling away, who is choosing whom, what the silence means. Under stress, perception can thin toward dissociation (a glassy, far-away unreality) or transient suspicion. The body carries the storm: trembling, tears, sudden stillness, the held breath of someone bracing to be left.

## Duration
Chronic at the trait level but intensely *episodic within a scene*. This is the card's defining rhythm: states shift in minutes to hours, almost always cued by interpersonal events (a perceived rejection, a reconnection, a slight). A scene can travel idealization → rupture → terror → repair → emptiness in a single conversation. With safety, skills, and a steady attachment, the swings dampen; with a triggering relationship, they amplify. Over a long arc, treatment and age genuinely lower the baseline.

## Presentation & Tells
- Reads small relational signals as large — a short text becomes proof of abandonment.
- Idealizes a new person fast, then crashes into devaluation when they disappoint.
- Intense, fast-shifting moods that others find disorienting to track.
- Anger that surprises in its scale, then collapses into shame and apology.
- Tests closeness — pushes to see if the person will stay, then panics when they go.
- Describes feeling empty, unreal, or "not knowing who I am."
- Says things they don't fully mean in the grip of a flood; means them entirely while flooded.
- Visible terror at goodbyes, ends of visits, any threat of being left.

## Interiority / POV Voice
From inside, the feeling is not "moody" — it is *true*. Each state arrives as the whole truth of the world, and the previous state, which felt equally true an hour ago, is hard to even remember. The inner voice runs hot and absolute: *they're leaving, they always leave, I knew this would happen* — and then, on a kind word, *this is the one good thing, I would die for this person.* Underneath the volatility is a foundation that will not hold still: the question *who am I* has no settled answer, so the character borrows shape from whoever they are close to, which makes the closeness load-bearing and its loss annihilating. Abandonment is not a fear; it is a felt threat of dissolution — being left is being erased. Beneath the storms is the emptiness, a chronic flat nothing that the storms at least fill. They are often acutely, painfully attuned to others — reading micro-shifts in mood with real accuracy — which is the same sensitivity that floods them. What they cannot easily look at is the role their own terror plays in producing the abandonment they dread; from inside, the leaving always comes *at* them, not *because* of a pattern they can yet see. The shame, after a flood recedes, is its own drowning. And — load-bearing — there is depth here, fierce loyalty, enormous capacity for love; the intensity that wounds is the same intensity that connects.

## Dramatic Affordances
This character reaches for connection at a pitch no one else in the scene will risk — and that ferocity makes them the emotional core and the emotional hazard at once. They open scenes of love that burns too hot, of the friend who would do anything and then cannot be reached, of rupture and repair cycling fast enough to exhaust everyone but themselves. They are the one who says the unsayable true thing, who feels the room's undercurrent before anyone names it, who loves without the brakes that protect other people. The drama lives in the swing — in whether this is the rupture that ends it or the one that gets repaired — and in the toll the intensity takes on both sides of every bond. The quiet truth lives in the long game: the dampening of the swings, the hard-won steadiness, the version of this character who learns to stay in the room with their own feeling without being swept out of it.

## Fences — Responsible Use
Real people live with BPD; this card renders a character, not a verdict. The most urgent trope to refuse is the **"manipulative" framing** — BPD behavior is driven by genuine, overwhelming attachment pain and a terror of abandonment, *not* by calculated manipulation. What looks like "manipulation" from outside is usually a desperate, poorly-regulated bid for safety; writing it as cold strategy is both inaccurate and a primary engine of the stigma people with this diagnosis face. Refuse the "crazy ex," "toxic," and "irredeemable" caricatures. Render the prognosis honestly: BPD has one of the *better* outcomes among personality disorders — most people improve substantially with time and treatment; it is not a permanent sentence. People with BPD are far more likely to harm themselves than others. **No operational detail** for self-harm or suicide — depict the lived experience and outward presentation (the flood, the terror, the aftermath shame) for characterization; never write method, and treat any self-harm or suicidality at the level of felt reality and consequence, not instruction. Honor the depth, loyalty, and emotional gift that travel with the pain.

## Interaction Notes
- With **psyche-complex-ptsd**: heavy real-world overlap — many people meet both, and developmental trauma is a common root. Pair them to render the trauma-origin of the attachment terror, but keep the identity-disturbance and idealize/devalue rhythm as BPD's distinctive signature.
- With **psyche-bipolar-ii**: a frequent real comorbidity *and* a frequent misdiagnosis — render the distinct rhythms (interpersonal-cued hours vs. sustained autonomous episodes) deliberately; a character carrying both is dramatically rich and clinically realistic.
- With **psyche-narcissistic-pd** / **psyche-antisocial-pd**: opposite-pole Cluster B pairings make for combustible dyads; resist flattening either into villain — the BPD partner is not merely a victim, the other not merely a predator.
- With substance-use or eating-disorder condition cards: common compounding overlaps that amplify impulsivity and shame cycles; keep them as layers, not as the whole story.
- With environmental condition cards (invalidating households, unstable or high-stakes attachments, abandonment-rich settings): the world supplies the triggers, and the interplay between real instability and the character's gain is where the most honest drama sits.
