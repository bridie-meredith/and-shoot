# Schizoaffective Disorder — Psyche Profile

## Description
A psyche-profile condition card that colors a *character's* behavior, perception, and interiority — not a location. Schizoaffective disorder is the bridge diagnosis between psychosis and mood: a person experiences the active symptoms of schizophrenia *and* sustained, prominent mood episodes (depressive or manic), but — and this is the defining hinge — there are also stretches of psychosis with no major mood symptoms present. It is neither pure schizophrenia nor a mood disorder that merely turns psychotic at its peaks.

## Clinical Profile
- **Taxonomy:** DSM-5-TR — Schizophrenia Spectrum and Other Psychotic Disorders. ICD-11 — Schizophrenia or other primary psychotic disorders (6A21, Schizoaffective disorder). Specified as **bipolar type** (manic episodes occur) or **depressive type** (only depressive episodes).
- **Core features:** (1) An uninterrupted period in which a major mood episode (depressive or manic) co-occurs with the active-phase symptoms of schizophrenia — delusions, hallucinations, disorganized speech, disorganized behavior, or negative symptoms. (2) The load-bearing criterion: **delusions or hallucinations for two or more weeks in the absence of a major mood episode**, across the lifetime of the illness — this is what separates it from a mood disorder with psychotic features. (3) Mood symptoms are present for the *majority* of the total illness duration — this is what separates it from schizophrenia with incidental mood symptoms.
- **Onset & course:** Typically early adulthood; often somewhat later than schizophrenia for the depressive type. Course is chronic and episodic, with the shape varying by type — the bipolar type cycles through mania and depression against a psychotic backdrop; the depressive type carries a heavier, more persistent low. Prognosis tends to fall between schizophrenia and mood disorders.
- **Prevalence:** Approximately 0.3% lifetime (less common than schizophrenia).
- **Common comorbidities:** Anxiety disorders, substance use disorders (psyche-alcohol-use-disorder, psyche-stimulant-use-disorder), and notably elevated suicide risk, especially in the depressive type and during depressive episodes.
- **Differential:** From **schizophrenia** (psyche-schizophrenia — mood symptoms are brief or incidental, not present for the majority of the illness). From **bipolar I / major depression with psychotic features** (psyche-bipolar-i, psyche-major-depressive-disorder — psychosis occurs *only* during mood episodes; the two-week-mood-free-psychosis window is what rules these out). From **delusional disorder** (psyche-delusional-disorder — no mood episodes, no other active-phase symptoms).

## Sensory / Behavioral Impact
Two weather systems share one sky. The psychotic register colors perception the way it does in schizophrenia — over-meaning, voices, referential pull, thought that slips or thins. Onto this the mood layer stacks its own physics: in mania, the world speeds and brightens, sleep evaporates, speech outruns the listener, confidence becomes grandiosity that the psychosis can amplify into conviction; in depression, everything weights and slows, color drains, the voices (if present) turn crueler and more accusatory, and motivation collapses on top of any negative symptoms. The hardest passages are the seams — the mood lifts or drops while the psychosis stays, and the character cannot tell which engine is driving the moment.

## Duration
Chronic, with a layered episodic structure. Within a scene the mood state is relatively stable but can shift faster than the psychotic baseline — a manic surge or a depressive plunge has its own momentum. The defining lived fact is the *recombination*: mood and psychosis can be present together, or psychosis can persist while mood is level, so the character's state in any given scene is a specific blend rather than a single setting. Stress, sleep loss, seasonal change, and medication adherence move it.

## Presentation & Tells
- Mood color laid over psychotic content — grandiose, accelerated, pressured speech (manic phase) or leaden, slowed, self-condemning speech (depressive phase), with delusional or hallucinatory threads running through either.
- In mania: little or no sleep, fast plans, expansive or irritable affect, spending or risk, talk that's hard to interrupt.
- In depression: withdrawal, anhedonia, hopelessness, slowed movement, neglected self-care — heavier than ordinary low mood and harder to lift.
- Stretches where the mood has settled but the wariness, the referential reading, or the voices persist — a character calmer in affect yet still tracking something off-channel.
- Disorganized or derailing speech independent of the mood weather.
- The person themselves struggling to name what kind of bad day it is — "is this the down or the other thing?"

## Interiority / POV Voice
From inside, the danger is never knowing which of two unreliable narrators is talking. In the up-phase, the meaning-machine and the mood-machine collude: the connections feel not just true but *destined*, the energy makes the grandiose conviction feel earned, and it is intoxicating in a way that makes the eventual descent worse. In the down-phase, the same perceptual apparatus turns prosecutorial — the voices, if they come, take the side of the depression; the referential reading finds proof of worthlessness in everything; and the negative-symptom flatness compounds the depressive weight until even the will to be helped is gone. Between episodes, in the mood-level psychotic stretches, there is an eerie clarity of affect with no clarity of world — calm hands, a quiet face, and still the certainty that the room means something. What the character refuses to look at: the pattern, because seeing the pattern would mean distrusting every strong feeling, and some of those feelings are the only vivid things they have.

## Dramatic Affordances
This character reaches for the high of the manic-psychotic conviction — the sense of being plugged into a larger design — that no one else in the scene can match or follow, and pays for it on the far side. The drama lives in the layering: a single episode can carry mood *and* delusion, so a scene can pivot from luminous certainty to derailment to a crash within one arc. The depressive-type character carries a heavier, sadder gravity and the highest stakes. Caregivers and clinicians face a genuine epistemic puzzle — which symptom is which — that mirrors the character's own confusion and makes for honest, frustrating, loving conflict. The quiet truth lives in the mood-level stretches: the person doing the work of holding a shape while still hearing the room hum, neither fully up nor fully down, just *managing*.

## Fences — Responsible Use
Real people live with schizoaffective disorder; this card renders a character, not a verdict. **Carry forward the schizophrenia corrections: this is not "split personality" or "multiple personalities"** — it is a disorder of perception, thought, and mood, not of identity-fragmentation (that is psyche-dissociative-identity-disorder). And **the violence overstatement is wrong here too:** most people with this diagnosis are not dangerous and are far more often harmed than harmful; do not use the mania-plus-psychosis combination as a license to write a menacing wildcard. Refuse: the bipolar-as-quirky-creativity gloss (this is a serious psychotic-spectrum illness, not a personality flavor), the manic episode as comic energy, the "twist" reveal, and the character collapsed into nothing but their cycling. Render the lived experience for characterization — never an operational manual for self-harm, which is a real and serious risk in this population and must be handled with care, not detail.

## Interaction Notes
- With **psyche-schizophrenia**: the cards share their psychotic core; the difference is the sustained mood layer and the majority-of-illness mood criterion. Use schizoaffective when the character's story needs both engines genuinely present.
- With **psyche-bipolar-i / psyche-bipolar-ii**: the bipolar-type character will read as bipolar-adjacent in the mood phases; the line is the mood-free psychosis. Useful contrast pairing for a story about two characters where one's psychosis is mood-bound and the other's isn't.
- With **psyche-major-depressive-disorder**: the depressive type overlaps heavily in the down-phase; the persistent or mood-independent psychosis is the tell.
- With **psyche-alcohol-use-disorder / psyche-stimulant-use-disorder**: substance use both self-medicates and destabilizes, and can mimic or trigger either layer; render the tangle.
- With environmental condition cards: sleep-disrupting, high-stimulus, or isolating settings can tip the mood layer (toward mania or depression) and amplify the psychotic layer simultaneously — a double destabilizer.
