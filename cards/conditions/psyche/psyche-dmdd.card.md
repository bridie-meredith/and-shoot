# Disruptive Mood Dysregulation Disorder — Psyche Profile

## Description
A psyche-profile condition card. It colors a *character's* behavior, perception, and interiority — not a location. Disruptive Mood Dysregulation Disorder (DMDD) is a childhood-onset mood disorder defined by chronic, severe irritability punctuated by frequent temper outbursts grossly out of proportion to their trigger. Its one-line essence: a child who is angry almost all the time, between explosions that look bigger than any situation should warrant.

## Clinical Profile
- **Taxonomy:** DSM-5-TR — Depressive Disorders (Disruptive Mood Dysregulation Disorder). ICD-11 — does not list DMDD as a separate entity; the presentation is captured under Oppositional defiant disorder with chronic irritability-anger (6C90.0). It is grouped with the depressive disorders in DSM precisely because the *between-outburst* mood is persistently negative.
- **Core features:**
  - Severe, recurrent temper outbursts (verbal and/or behavioral) grossly out of proportion in intensity or duration to the provocation.
  - Outbursts inconsistent with developmental level — bigger than a child that age should be having.
  - Occurring, on average, three or more times per week.
  - Between outbursts, mood is persistently irritable or angry most of the day, nearly every day, and observable by others.
  - Present for twelve or more months with no symptom-free stretch of three or more months, across at least two settings (e.g. home and school).
  - Diagnosed only between ages 6 and 18; onset before age 10. Not diagnosed for the first time before 6 or after 18.
- **Onset & course:** A childhood diagnosis by definition. Longitudinally, children with DMDD are at elevated risk of developing unipolar depressive and anxiety disorders in adolescence and adulthood — *not* primarily bipolar disorder, which is the misclassification DMDD was created to prevent.
- **Prevalence:** Approximately 2–5% in children, depending on sample and criteria; more common in boys and in younger children.
- **Common comorbidities:** Very high overlap with psyche-oppositional-defiant-disorder (by design, DMDD takes precedence when criteria for both are met) and psyche-adhd; frequently precedes later psyche-major-depressive-disorder and psyche-generalized-anxiety-disorder.
- **Differential:** Distinguished from pediatric bipolar disorder by the *absence of distinct manic/hypomanic episodes* — DMDD irritability is chronic and non-episodic, not a cycling elevated mood. Distinguished from ODD by the severity and persistence of the mood disturbance (the chronic anger, not just the defiance). Distinguished from a developmentally typical temper by frequency, proportion, and the persistent negative baseline between episodes.

## Sensory / Behavioral Impact
The character runs hot. Their threshold for frustration is chronically low, so ordinary friction — a denied request, a lost game, a changed plan — can trigger an outburst that looks wildly disproportionate from outside. Between explosions there is no relief into cheer; the resting state is grouchy, prickly, braced for the next slight. The world is experienced as unfair and provoking, and the character's body holds tension that discharges as anger because anger is the channel that's open. For a child POV especially, the affect outpaces the language — they *feel* enormous, unmanageable distress and have no words for it, so it comes out as rage, slammed doors, and "I hate you," which the surrounding adults read as bad behavior rather than as a mood disorder.

## Duration
Chronic with acute spikes. The irritable baseline is persistent — present most of the day, most days, for a year or more. Within a scene the baseline is a simmering, easily-provoked grouchiness; the outbursts are the acute spikes, fast to ignite and disproportionate in size, often followed by exhaustion, tears, and remorse once the storm passes. What shifts it mid-scene is the trigger event (a "no," a transition, a frustration) and, on the downslope, co-regulation by a calm adult — though without it the child tends to ride the spike all the way to depletion.

## Presentation & Tells
- A child who seems angry or irritable almost all the time — rarely settled, rarely content, easily set off.
- Frequent, intense meltdowns far out of proportion to the trigger — screaming, throwing, hitting, slammed doors over what looks like a small thing.
- Outbursts in multiple settings (home, school) — not just acting out in one place.
- A short fuse around transitions, denied requests, losing, and unexpected changes.
- Post-outburst depletion: tears, exhaustion, sometimes genuine remorse or "I don't know why I did that."
- Strained relationships with parents, teachers, and peers; the child is often labelled "difficult" or "bad" rather than understood as struggling.
- A persistent grouchiness between episodes that adults find harder to name than the dramatic explosions.

## Interiority / POV Voice
From inside — and DMDD is most often written from a child's lens — the dominant feeling is that *everything is unfair and against me*, held with the absolute moral certainty children bring to injustice. The inner voice is loud, hot, and globalizing: a small "no" becomes proof that no one is on their side, that the rules are rigged, that they are always the one who gets in trouble. Crucially, the feeling vastly outsizes the available vocabulary — the character feels something enormous and has no name for it, so it presents to themselves and everyone else as anger, which is the one big feeling that has a clear shape. During the spike there is often a frightening loss of control the child experiences from a small remove, as if watching themselves come apart and unable to stop. After, the register collapses into a flat, tired, guilty grief — confusion at the size of what just happened, fear that they are simply bad, shame that lands harder than any punishment. What the voice refuses to look at directly is the soft hurt underneath the rage — the loneliness, the longing to be liked, the exhaustion of being "the difficult one." That tender underlayer, mostly unspoken, is the load-bearing center of the POV: the anger is the surface, and the surface is loud, but the truth is a kid who is suffering and out of words.

## Dramatic Affordances
This character reaches for the explosion — where a regulated child would reach for words, a sulk, or a negotiation. The drama lives in the gap between how the child is *read* (bad, defiant, a problem) and what is actually happening (a mood disorder with no vocabulary), and in the toll the chronic conflict takes on a family worn down by it. Powerful scenes: the adult who finally responds to the hurt under the rage instead of to the rage; the sibling caught in the fallout; the meltdown that frightens the child as much as everyone else; the rare quiet moment where the soft, lonely truth surfaces. The quiet truth lives in the aftermath — the depleted, remorseful, wordless child who does not understand themselves. The honest arc is slow skill-building and being correctly seen, not a single tearful breakthrough that "cures" the temper.

## Fences — Responsible Use
- Real children live with this. The card renders a character, not a verdict — DMDD is a recognized mood disorder, not "a bad kid," not "bad parenting," and not a moral failing in the child or the family.
- Refuse the "just needs more discipline" frame as a cure; a harsh-authority *character* might voice it, but the narration must not endorse punishment as the answer to a dysregulation disorder.
- DMDD was specifically created to stop over-diagnosis of pediatric bipolar disorder — do not write the chronic irritability as "manic episodes" or a cycling high. There is no euphoric pole here.
- Do not aestheticize or villainize the outbursts. The explosions are real and exhausting for everyone, *and* the child is suffering; hold both, and keep the tender underlayer in view.
- Most of the harm is borne by the child themselves (relationships, self-concept, later depression/anxiety risk) — render them as struggling, not as a threat.
- NO operational or method detail regarding self-harm or harming others. Render the felt experience — the unfairness, the wordless flood, the after-shame — never instruction; route toward co-regulation, being understood, and care.

## Interaction Notes
- With psyche-oppositional-defiant-disorder, the two overlap heavily and DMDD takes diagnostic precedence — render the chronic angry *mood* (DMDD's signature) underneath the rule-breaking and defiance (ODD's), not just oppositional behavior in isolation.
- With psyche-adhd, low frustration tolerance and impulsivity stack with the irritable baseline — the child is both quick to ignite and slow to inhibit, and transitions/denied requests become flashpoints; render the comorbidity as compounding, not as ADHD "explaining away" the mood disorder.
- As a developmental precursor to psyche-major-depressive-disorder and psyche-generalized-anxiety-disorder, a DMDD child written into an older-self arc may carry the irritability forward into adolescent depression/anxiety rather than into bipolar disorder — render continuity along the unipolar/anxious line.
- With an environmental condition card (chaotic household, school pressure, instability, an unpredictable caregiver), the ambient stress lowers the frustration threshold further and multiplies triggers — render the environment as fuel on an already-low ignition point, not as the sole cause of the outbursts.
