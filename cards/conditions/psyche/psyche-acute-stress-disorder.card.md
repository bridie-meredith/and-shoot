# Acute Stress Disorder — Psyche Profile

## Description
A psyche-profile condition card. It colors a *character's* behavior, perception, and interiority — not a location. Acute Stress Disorder is the immediate, near-term aftermath of trauma: the same storm as PTSD, caught in its first month, when the survivor is still inside the wave and doesn't yet know whether it will recede.

## Clinical Profile
- **Taxonomy:** DSM-5-TR — Trauma- and Stressor-Related Disorders (code 308.3 / F43.0). ICD-11 frames the related picture as **Acute stress reaction (QE84)**, classified as a *normal* transient response rather than a mental disorder; the DSM disorder and the ICD reaction differ in framing.
- **Core features:**
  - Exposure to actual or threatened death, serious injury, or sexual violence.
  - Nine or more symptoms across intrusion, negative mood, dissociation, avoidance, and arousal — beginning or worsening after the event.
  - Prominent **dissociation** is common acutely: feeling dazed, unreal, detached; time distortion; an inability to recall a key part of the event.
  - Intrusive memories and distressing dreams; vivid sense of re-living.
  - Hypervigilance, exaggerated startle, sleep disturbance, irritability, difficulty concentrating.
- **Onset & course:** symptoms appear within days of the trauma and last **3 days to 1 month**. By definition the diagnosis dissolves at the one-month mark — into recovery, or into PTSD (psyche-ptsd).
- **Prevalence:** highly event-dependent; roughly 5–20% of trauma survivors, higher after interpersonal violence. A meaningful fraction of those affected go on to develop PTSD, though many recover without it.
- **Common comorbidities:** acute depression, panic (psyche-panic-disorder), and — given the time window — physical injury, grief, and shock from the same event.
- **Differential:** from PTSD (psyche-ptsd) purely by timeline — identical symptoms past one month are PTSD. From adjustment disorder (psyche-adjustment-disorder), which lacks the traumatic-stressor criterion and the re-experiencing core. From a brief psychotic reaction, which the dissociation can superficially resemble but which involves loss of reality testing the survivor here largely retains.

## Sensory / Behavioral Impact
The world can feel unreal — flat, filmed, behind glass. Time stretches and skips. The character may move through necessary tasks with eerie competence and no felt connection to them, or freeze and be unable to act at all. Sensation is either muffled (the dissociative blunting) or unbearably sharp (the arousal). Sleep, if it comes, brings the event back. The body holds an electric readiness with nowhere to put it.

## Duration
Acute and time-bounded by definition — this is the *first month*. Within a scene it is volatile: a survivor can be numb and orderly one moment, sobbing or shaking the next, then numb again. Re-exposure to any reminder, or simply the adrenaline ebbing, can crack the dissociative shell and let the feeling through. This is the rawest window; nothing has scarred over yet.

## Presentation & Tells
- Glassy, dazed, "not all there"; slow to respond; asks the same question twice.
- Strikingly calm and functional in a way that unsettles onlookers — then breaks.
- Gaps in the account of what happened; "I don't remember that part."
- Hyperstartle, scanning, trembling hands; can't sit still or can't move at all.
- Reports the world feeling unreal, or themselves feeling unreal.
- Nightmares, won't sleep, or jolts awake.
- Flat affect that doesn't match the magnitude of what occurred.

## Interiority / POV Voice
It hasn't landed. Part of the character knows exactly what happened and another part keeps not-believing it, the two running side by side without touching. The world has a thin, staged quality — they reach to feel the table and it is there but it is *reported* there, the connection between hand and confirmation gone slack. Memory of the event comes in fragments and in floods, out of order, and when a fragment hits, the body answers before the mind can decide whether now is safe. There is a strange efficiency available in the numbness — they can do the necessary things, call the necessary people, in a voice that sounds almost normal — and underneath it a held breath, the sense of standing at the lip of something that hasn't fallen yet. They don't know who they'll be on the other side of this month.

## Dramatic Affordances
This is the scene *right after* — the survivor still wet from it, before the story has decided what the event will mean. The character reaches for ordinary tasks as handholds, for an unnerving competence that lets them function while the rest of the room falls apart, or for a stillness no one can pull them out of. The drama lives in the people around them misreading the calm as strength or the numbness as coldness; in the moment the shell cracks; in the open question of which way this resolves. The quiet truth lives in the survivor doing the necessary thing — making the call, identifying the body — in a flat clear voice while plainly not present behind their own eyes.

## Fences — Responsible Use
Acute stress responses are normal reactions to abnormal events — most people who have them recover. Do not pathologize a survivor for being numb, dazed, or "too calm"; dissociation is the mind's circuit breaker, not weakness or guilt. Avoid the trope of the survivor who is instantly, articulately traumatized on cue for the audience's benefit; in the first days the experience is more often fragmented, unreal, and inexpressible. Do not stage the originating event in graphic detail for impact. Real people pass through this window and out the other side; the card renders a character in their rawest hour, not a verdict on how they'll heal.

## Interaction Notes
By design it precedes PTSD (psyche-ptsd) — a character can be staged in ASD early in a story and shown crossing (or not crossing) into PTSD later. The dissociative features connect to depersonalization/derealization (psyche-depersonalization-derealization); acute derealization here is state, not the standalone disorder. Pairs with panic (psyche-panic-disorder) when the arousal spikes into full attacks. Stacks with grief and physical injury from the shared event. Environmental condition cards that re-supply the trauma's sensory signature — a return to the site, a similar sound or smell — can crack the dissociative shell mid-scene.
