# Narcolepsy — Psyche Profile

## Description
A psyche-profile condition card. It colors a *character's* behavior, perception, and interiority — not a location. Narcolepsy is a chronic neurological disorder of sleep-wake regulation: the boundary between sleeping and waking is porous, so sleep — and fragments of sleep, like dreams and paralysis — intrude into waking life without permission, and waking intrudes into the night.

## Clinical Profile
- **Taxonomy:** DSM-5-TR — Sleep-Wake Disorders (Narcolepsy). ICD-11 — Sleep-Wake Disorders, "Narcolepsy and other hypersomnolence disorders" grouping (code 7A20). Clinically split into Type 1 (with cataplexy / low hypocretin-orexin) and Type 2 (without cataplexy).
- **Core features:**
  - Overwhelming, recurrent daytime sleepiness — irrepressible need to sleep or lapses into sleep, multiple times a day, including at inappropriate moments.
  - **Cataplexy** (Type 1) — sudden, brief loss of muscle tone triggered by strong emotion (laughter, surprise, anger); the character stays conscious while the body buckles, the knees give, the jaw or eyelids droop.
  - **Sleep paralysis** — on falling asleep or waking, a span of being awake-aware but unable to move or speak.
  - **Hypnagogic / hypnopompic hallucinations** — vivid, often frightening dream-imagery at the edges of sleep, sometimes layered over the real room.
  - **Disrupted nighttime sleep** — paradoxically, fragmented, broken night sleep despite the daytime sleepiness.
  - Sleep arrives *fast* and goes straight toward REM — naps are short and dream-rich, and the character often wakes refreshed but briefly.
- **Onset & course:** Typically begins in adolescence or young adulthood (commonly 10–25), often with a years-long diagnostic delay. Lifelong and chronic; symptoms are managed, not cured. Type 1 is associated with loss of hypocretin (orexin)-producing neurons.
- **Prevalence:** Rare — approximately 0.02–0.05% of the population (roughly 1 in 2,000–3,000); often underdiagnosed.
- **Common comorbidities:** Depression and anxiety (the isolation and unpredictability take a toll), obesity, and other sleep disorders. See psyche-major-depressive-disorder.
- **Differential:** Distinguish from ordinary sleep deprivation and insomnia (in narcolepsy sleep comes too easily, not too hard — see psyche-insomnia-disorder), from sleep apnea, from seizure disorders (cataplexy preserves consciousness; seizures usually do not), and from fainting (cataplexy is emotion-triggered and the character stays aware). Cataplexy is sometimes misread as drunkenness, drama, or fainting by onlookers.

## Sensory / Behavioral Impact
The character carries a sleep pressure most people only feel at the worst end of a sleepless night — except it can land at 10 a.m. in a meeting. Attention is fine, then suddenly underwater; the world goes soft at the edges and the head wants to drop. They've learned to fight it, and the fight is exhausting in its own right — pinching, standing, moving. Strong emotion is physically dangerous in Type 1: a real laugh can take the legs out, so the character may armor against feeling too much. The membrane between dream and room is thin — a face at the foot of the bed that isn't there, a voice in the hypnagogic dark — and waking can begin trapped, awake inside a paralyzed body for a span that feels much longer than it is. Naps are quick and vivid; they surface from them dream-soaked, momentarily unsure which layer is real.

## Duration
Chronic and lifelong as a condition; *episodic and unpredictable* within a scene. Sleep attacks and cataplexy are events, not a baseline — they can hit suddenly and pass in seconds to minutes. What shifts them mid-scene: a wave of sleepiness after a meal, in warmth, in boredom or monotony; an emotional spike triggering cataplexy; a strict nap schedule (a managed character builds the day around planned naps). Between events the character may present as unremarkable, which is part of why the condition is so easily missed and so easily disbelieved.

## Presentation & Tells
- Sudden microsleeps — eyes glazing, head nodding, a lost beat in a conversation, then a snap back.
- Automatic behaviors — continuing to write, walk, or talk while half-asleep, then no memory of the gap.
- A cataplectic slump on a hard laugh or a jolt of anger: knees buckle, head drops, speech slurs, the character grabbing a chair or wall — fully aware the whole time.
- A guarded relationship to strong emotion; deflecting a joke before it lands too hard.
- Scheduled, almost ritual naps; a known quiet place they retreat to.
- Talking about being awake at night and exhausted by day — the paradox that confuses people.
- Practical adaptations: doesn't drive certain hours, sits near exits, warns new people in advance, keeps caffeine close.
- On waking, a few seconds of stillness — sleep paralysis passing — sometimes followed by a flinch at something that was never in the room.

## Interiority / POV Voice
From inside, the boundary you trust between asleep and awake is a rumor. Sleepiness isn't gentle drowsiness — it's a tide that rises and crests and wants to pull you under right where you stand, and the whole day can become a negotiation with it: *not now, not here, give me ten more minutes of upright.* There's a constant low vigilance about the body's reliability. A good laugh is a hazard you feel coming; you learn to clamp down on joy a half-second early, and you grieve that, quietly — the small reflex of holding your own delight at arm's length so your knees don't betray you.

The edges of sleep are populated. Falling asleep, waking up, the room can fill with things — a shape, a pressure, a voice — that you know aren't real and that terrify you anyway, because terror doesn't take instruction from knowledge. Sleep paralysis is its own specific dread: awake, aware, screaming inside a body that won't answer, counting the seconds until the wiring reconnects. And under all of it, the loneliness of not being believed — the eye-rolls, the "must be nice to nap all day," the bosses and teachers who read a sleep attack as laziness. The character knows their own tiredness is real and physiological, and spends a lot of energy proving it to people who'd rather call it a character flaw. There's grief in the unpredictability — plans you can't fully make, a body you can't fully trust — and a hard-won, dry humor about it that's often the only place the loneliness can go.

## Dramatic Affordances
Cataplexy is a gift for a scene where the body betrays the character at the exact wrong moment — the laugh that drops them mid-confrontation, the joy they can't afford. The thin membrane between dream and waking opens onto unsettling, near-uncanny imagery rendered honestly from the character's POV, where the audience shares the genuine question of what's real. Sleep paralysis is a contained horror beat that's true to lived experience rather than invented. The disbelief the character faces drives interpersonal conflict — being told their disability is an attitude problem — and the slow labor of proving themselves is an arc. The guarded relationship to strong emotion gives a character a poignant restraint: someone who has to ration their own laughter. And the scheduled-nap discipline shows a character managing a body that won't be argued with — competence under a real constraint.

## Fences — Responsible Use
Real people live with narcolepsy, and the popular picture is mostly wrong. It is not a comedic device — the person who hilariously face-plants into soup is a slander, not a portrayal; render the cost, the fear, and the disbelief, not just the gag. Cataplexy is **not** fainting and **not** loss of consciousness — the character stays fully aware while the body fails; get that right, because the awareness is the heart of it. Narcolepsy is not laziness, not a willpower problem, and not "just being tired" — characters who treat it that way should be shown as wrong. It is a neurological disorder, not a personality. Do not write operational detail on medication misuse or stimulant misuse — render the management and the lived experience, never a how-to. Avoid the trope of the narcoleptic as unreliable comic relief whose condition exists only to inconvenience others; the character is the one for whom this is real.

## Interaction Notes
- **With psyche-insomnia-disorder:** physiological opposites often confused at the surface (both characters are exhausted), but inverse — the insomniac can't reach sleep; the narcoleptic can't hold it off, and is paradoxically also broken-sleeped at night. The pairing plays well for irony or for two tired characters who can't understand each other's tiredness.
- **With psyche-major-depressive-disorder / anxiety:** the unpredictability, social disbelief, and emotion-rationing wear a real groove; depression and anxiety commonly ride alongside and can deepen the isolation. A depressive flatness can also mask or be masked by the daytime sleepiness.
- **With psyche-ptsd:** hypnagogic hallucinations and sleep paralysis can entangle with trauma imagery, making the edges of sleep an actively feared space; the two together make "going to sleep" itself a charged act.
- **With environmental condition cards:** stacks with warmth, monotony, dim light, and post-meal heaviness — anything that raises sleep pressure makes a sleep attack likelier mid-scene. A high-emotion environment raises cataplexy risk for a Type 1 character; a setting that demands sustained alertness (driving, machinery, a vigil) turns the condition into live danger.
