# Specific Learning Disorder (Dyslexia / Dyscalculia / Dysgraphia) — Psyche Profile

## Description
This is a psyche-profile condition card: it colors a *character's* behavior, perception, and interiority (not a location). Specific learning disorder is a neurodevelopmental difficulty in a particular academic skill — reading (dyslexia), arithmetic (dyscalculia), or written expression (dysgraphia) — in a person of otherwise typical intelligence, so that one channel runs slow and effortful while the rest of the mind is fully bright.

## Clinical Profile
- **Taxonomy:** DSM-5-TR — Neurodevelopmental Disorders → Specific Learning Disorder, specified by domain: with impairment in reading, in mathematics, and/or in written expression. ICD-11 — 6A03 Developmental learning disorder.
- **Core features:**
  - Reading impairment (dyslexia): difficulty with accurate/fluent word reading, decoding, spelling — a phonological-processing difference, not a vision problem.
  - Mathematics impairment (dyscalculia): difficulty with number sense, math facts, calculation, math reasoning.
  - Written expression impairment (dysgraphia): difficulty with spelling, grammar, punctuation, and organizing thoughts on the page; handwriting may be effortful.
  - Skills substantially below age expectation and interfering with school, work, or daily life.
  - General intelligence intact — the deficit is specific and unexpected given overall ability.
  - Persistent despite adequate instruction and effort; not explained by intellectual disability, sensory impairment, or lack of schooling.
- **Onset & course:** Emerges in the school-age years when the skill is first demanded; lifelong, though strategies, accommodation, and technology substantially reduce impairment. Adults often retain slow reading or shaky spelling/arithmetic and route around it.
- **Prevalence:** Approximately 5–15% of school-age children across domains; reading is the most common.
- **Common comorbidities:** ADHD (psyche-adhd), other learning-disorder domains co-occurring, anxiety, developmental coordination and language disorders, tic disorders (psyche-tourettes).
- **Differential:** Distinguished from intellectual disability (psyche-intellectual-disability, where the difficulty is general, not specific), from inadequate instruction or missed schooling, and from sensory impairment; the hallmark is the *gap* between strong general ability and the one weak channel.

## Sensory / Behavioral Impact
One specific channel runs slow, effortful, and unreliable while everything around it is sharp. For the reader with dyslexia, text is work — letters that won't hold still in order, words decoded one labored piece at a time, a page that costs ten times what it costs a peer, leaving little capacity left for the *meaning* underneath. For dyscalculia, numbers don't carry intuitive quantity — a price, a time, a tip is a small panic. For dysgraphia, the thought is clear and the hand or the page betrays it, ideas arriving faster than they can be captured and coming out scrambled. The mind is fully present; the bottleneck is narrow and exhausting, and the character spends real energy hiding it.

## Duration
Lifelong and stable in nature, domain-specific. Within a scene, demand on the weak channel spikes load instantly — a menu, a form, a chalkboard, a read-aloud-around-the-room — while everything else stays easy. Fatigue, stress, time pressure, and being watched worsen performance; accommodation (extra time, audio, a calculator, a scribe, assistive tech) restores it. The same character is articulate and quick in conversation and stalled in front of a paragraph.

## Presentation & Tells
- Avoids reading aloud; volunteers to do anything but; loses their place, substitutes or skips words.
- Reads slowly, traces lines with a finger, sounds words out under breath, re-reads.
- Spelling errors that surprise given how articulate they are; phonetic or reversed letters.
- Dyscalculia: counts on fingers, trusts a calculator for simple sums, freezes on prices/times/change.
- Dysgraphia: cramped or laborious handwriting, written work far below their spoken sophistication, avoids the page.
- Quick, fluent, often funny *out loud* — the gap between verbal ability and written/numeric output is the tell.
- Practiced cover stories: "I left my glasses," "you read it, you're faster," changing the subject.
- Visible flush of shame or deflection when the weak channel is exposed in front of others.

## Interiority / POV Voice
From inside, the mind is fast and clear and the doorway is jammed. The narrator knows exactly what they think and means to say — the ideas are *there*, bright and ordered — and then the page asks for them and the channel narrows to a straw. For the dyslexic reader, there's the specific dread of the page handed around the table, the silent math of *how many lines until mine*, the word that decodes one painful letter at a time while comprehension drains away into the labor of it. There is a deep, old confusion — *I'm not stupid, I know I'm not stupid, so why is this the one thing I can't do* — and the answer no one gave them for years was that nothing was wrong with their mind, only with how the world tested it. They become inventive: memorizers, listeners, talkers, charmers, people who get others to read the thing aloud. The shame is specific and channel-shaped — not a global feeling of incapacity but a sharp local one that fires the instant the weak skill is demanded in public. And there is relief, almost disproportionate, when a tool or a person makes the channel unnecessary and the rest of the bright mind is finally allowed through.

## Dramatic Affordances
The character is visibly smart and secretly stuck on the one thing the scene happens to require — a hidden disability, with all the tension of concealment, the dropped guard, the moment of exposure. They route around it with cunning and charm, which is its own character note. Drama lives in the gap between their evident ability and the institution that measures only the weak channel: the bright kid called lazy, the capable adult who can't get past the written exam, the cover story that finally fails. The quiet truth lives in the years of being misjudged and the late, vindicating discovery — *it had a name, it was never my fault* — and in the dignity of the workaround. Strong material: the read-aloud moment dreaded and survived; the mentor or tool that unlocks the channel; the reveal of the hidden struggle to someone who responds with help, not pity.

## Fences — Responsible Use
Real people live with learning disorders; this card renders a character, not a verdict on intelligence. The non-negotiable myth to correct: this is NOT stupidity, laziness, or low intelligence — general ability is intact; the deficit is narrow and specific. Dyslexia is not "seeing letters backwards" as a vision problem; it is a language/phonological-processing difference. Do not write the character as globally incapable — render the bright mind and the narrow bottleneck together; that contrast is the whole truth of the condition. Avoid the inspirational-overcoming arc as the only register, and avoid the "grew out of it" myth — it is lifelong and routed-around, not cured. Many succeed brilliantly; competence and disability coexist.

## Interaction Notes
Stacks very commonly with psyche-adhd (the executive load of both compounds the school/work shame; together they account for much misattributed "underachievement"). Multiple learning-disorder domains often co-occur. With anxiety and depression — frequent downstream, especially after years of being misjudged — the avoidance hardens and the shame globalizes. The distinction from psyche-intellectual-disability is load-bearing and worth rendering clearly: that is a general difference, this is a specific one in an otherwise typical mind. Environmental condition cards decide visibility: high-literacy, timed, public-performance, low-tech settings expose and disable; oral, untimed, accommodated, tech-supported settings make the disability nearly invisible and the character's full ability plain.
