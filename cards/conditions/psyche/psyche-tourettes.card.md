# Tourette's Disorder / Persistent Tic Disorder — Psyche Profile

## Description
This is a psyche-profile condition card: it colors a *character's* behavior, perception, and interiority (not a location). Tourette's is a neurodevelopmental tic disorder — sudden, recurrent, involuntary movements and sounds (tics) that the body produces against the will, preceded by a building "premonitory urge" and only briefly suppressible at real cost — a condition of being interrupted from inside by one's own nervous system.

## Clinical Profile
- **Taxonomy:** DSM-5-TR — Neurodevelopmental Disorders → Tic Disorders → Tourette's Disorder (both motor and vocal tics, >1 year) and Persistent (Chronic) Motor or Vocal Tic Disorder. ICD-11 — 8A05.00 Tourette syndrome (Diseases of the nervous system, cross-listed with neurodevelopmental).
- **Core features:**
  - Motor tics: blinking, grimacing, head/shoulder jerks, jaw movements, and more complex movements; range from subtle to large.
  - Vocal/phonic tics: sniffing, throat-clearing, grunting, squeaking, repeated words or sounds.
  - Premonitory urge: a rising physical sensation (pressure, itch, "have-to") that the tic discharges, then briefly relieves.
  - Suppressibility: tics can be held back for a time with effort, but the urge builds and suppression is tiring; release often follows in a flurry.
  - Waxing and waning: tics shift in type and intensity over weeks; new tics replace old.
  - Coprolalia (involuntary obscene/socially-taboo utterance) is the popular image but occurs in only a minority (roughly 1 in 10).
- **Onset & course:** Onset in childhood, typically ages 4–8, peaking around 10–12. Often improves substantially in adolescence and adulthood; a chronic-fluctuating course for some. Stress, excitement, and fatigue worsen it.
- **Prevalence:** Approximately 0.3–0.9% for Tourette's; transient and milder tic disorders are more common in childhood. More frequent in boys.
- **Common comorbidities:** ADHD (psyche-adhd) and OCD (psyche-ocd) very frequently — the "Tourette's triad"; also anxiety, learning disorders (psyche-specific-learning-disorder), and impulse-control difficulties.
- **Differential:** Distinguished from other movement disorders, stereotypies (rhythmic, soothing, suppressible differently), seizures, and from voluntary habit; the premonitory urge, suppressibility-at-cost, and waxing/waning pattern are the markers.

## Sensory / Behavioral Impact
The character's body acts before consent. A pressure builds — in the throat, the eyes, the neck, the shoulder, a spot under the skin — an itch that only the tic scratches, and the relief is real but temporary; the urge always refills. They can hold it for a while, in the important meeting, the quiet room, the first date — but suppression is active labor that drains attention and concentration, and the held tics tend to break loose afterward in a rush. Stress, excitement, and exhaustion all crank the volume up; deep absorption in a task can quiet it. The tic punctuates speech and movement at the worst and most ordinary times alike, and the character lives with never quite knowing when.

## Duration
Chronic and fluctuating — waxing and waning over weeks, often easing across adolescence into adulthood. Within a single scene, tic frequency tracks state: it climbs with stress, anticipation, and fatigue, drops during focused calm. A character may hold tics through a tense exchange and let go the moment they're alone or safe. Suppression has a ceiling and a cost; the longer held, the bigger the eventual release.

## Presentation & Tells
- Recurrent blinks, eye-rolls, facial grimaces, head/neck/shoulder jerks — sudden, not flowing.
- Throat-clearing, sniffing, grunts, squeaks, clicks, or repeated sounds/words punctuating speech.
- A quick discharge-then-settle rhythm; a flurry of tics after a stretch of held stillness.
- Visible effort to suppress — tension, a swallowed movement, restlessness — then breakthrough.
- Tics rise in tense or exciting moments; ebb when the character is calm or deeply absorbed.
- Self-aware management: positioning to hide a tic, an explanatory aside, a practiced deflection or joke.
- In the minority with complex tics: touching, echoing others' words/movements, or taboo utterance — followed often by distress, not glee.
- Fatigue and embarrassment after a high-tic stretch; the exhaustion of having been "on" against the body.

## Interiority / POV Voice
From inside, there is a self that wants stillness and a body that won't grant it — the narrator is the rider, not entirely the driver. The premonitory urge is the truest part to render: a rising, specific, physical *have-to*, like a sneeze that won't come or an itch deep under the skin, and the tic is the only thing that scratches it, and the relief lasts a few seconds before the pressure starts to refill. Suppression is possible but it's *work* — holding it through the funeral, the exam, the quiet library costs real attention, builds a backlog, and the held tics wait, and they always come due. There is a particular social arithmetic running constantly: *will it happen here, will they stare, do I explain first or just let it land, do I make the joke before they do.* For those with taboo tics, the cruelty is precise — the brain offers up exactly the word you'd never choose, in exactly the room where it's worst, and the horror afterward is real; it is not what they mean, and they know everyone will think it is. Underneath runs the fatigue of a body that interrupts you, the wish to be met as a person who has tics rather than as the tics, and — frequently — a sharp, funny, resilient humor forged by having no choice but to live out loud.

## Dramatic Affordances
The character carries a visible, involuntary difference into every scene — instant tension, instant question of how the room responds, instant test of who's kind and who isn't. They reach for control of a body that won't fully obey, and for the social management no one else has to do. Drama lives in suppression and breakthrough: the high-stakes quiet moment held by white-knuckle effort until it breaks; the taboo tic at the worst time; the stranger's stare and the choice to explain or endure. The quiet truth lives in the gap between intention and the body's interruption — and in the humor, courage, and matter-of-fact dignity many build to live with it. Strong material: the person who doesn't flinch; the safe space where the character finally stops suppressing and just *is*; the reveal that the tics were never the measure of them.

## Fences — Responsible Use
Real people live with Tourette's; this card renders a character, not a joke. The single most important correction: Tourette's is NOT primarily swearing — coprolalia occurs in only about 1 in 10, and reducing the condition to comedic obscenity is the central harmful cliché. Refuse it. Tics are involuntary, not attention-seeking, not "a habit," and not something the person can simply stop being asked to stop — telling them to "just relax" worsens it. Suppression is possible but costly and not a cure. When a taboo tic appears, render the character's distress and the gap between the utterance and their intent — never as their true feeling surfacing. Avoid the spectacle/freak framing; center the person who has tics, not the tics that have a person. Tourette's is not a sign of low intelligence or instability. NO content that treats involuntary utterances as a vehicle for slurs played for laughs.

## Interaction Notes
Stacks very commonly with psyche-adhd and psyche-ocd — the classic triad; the OCD overlap is worth careful rendering because a complex tic and a compulsion can look alike but differ in drive (premonitory urge discharge vs. anxiety neutralization), and the "just-right" tic blurs the line. With anxiety, the suppression load and social fear compound and tics climb. With psyche-specific-learning-disorder, school difficulty and visible tics together sharpen the misjudgment. Environmental condition cards strongly modulate the tells: quiet, high-pressure, eyes-on settings (exams, ceremonies, performances) both worsen the urge and demand suppression — maximum tension; relaxed, accepting, absorbing settings quiet the tics and let the character forget them for a while.
