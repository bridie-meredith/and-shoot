# Bipolar II Disorder — Psyche Profile

## Description
This is a psyche-profile condition card: it colors a *character's* behavior, perception, and interiority — not a location. Bipolar II Disorder is a mood disorder defined by at least one **hypomanic episode** (a distinct, milder high that does not rupture functioning or require hospitalization) together with at least one **major depressive episode** — and, critically, **no full mania, ever**. The essence in plain language: the lows are the deep ones; the highs are real but smaller, and the depression is usually what brings the suffering.

## Clinical Profile
- **Taxonomy:** DSM-5-TR — Bipolar and Related Disorders, Bipolar II (requires a hypomanic episode + a major depressive episode, never a manic one). ICD-11 — Mood disorders, Bipolar type II (6A61).
- **Core features:**
  - At least one **hypomanic episode**: ≥4 consecutive days of elevated/expansive/irritable mood plus increased energy — an unmistakable change from baseline, *observable by others*, but **not** severe enough to markedly impair functioning, require hospitalization, or include psychosis.
  - At least one **major depressive episode**; these are typically the longer, more frequent, and more disabling pole.
  - Hypomania may feel *good* — productive, social, creative — and is often not reported as a problem; the depression is what drives people to care.
  - No history of full mania (that crossing would reclassify to bipolar I).
  - Specifiers parallel bipolar I: with mixed features, rapid cycling, anxious distress, peripartum, seasonal.
  - Often underdiagnosed or mistaken for unipolar depression because patients describe the lows and not the highs.
- **Onset & course:** Typical onset in the early-to-mid twenties (sometimes later than bipolar I). Course is **episodic and recurrent**, often weighted heavily toward depression, with euthymic intervals between.
- **Prevalence:** Approximately 0.5–1% lifetime.
- **Common comorbidities:** Anxiety disorders, substance use disorders, and personality features overlapping psyche-borderline-pd (affective instability can resemble it). Depressive episodes mirror psyche-major-depressive-disorder.
- **Differential:** Bipolar I (psyche-bipolar-i) is distinguished by the presence of *full* mania — the disabling, sometimes-psychotic high; bipolar II tops out at hypomania. Unipolar depression (psyche-major-depressive-disorder) lacks any hypomanic history — the missed hypomania is the classic diagnostic trap. Cyclothymia (psyche-cyclothymia) never reaches a *full* major depressive episode or even a full hypomanic one — its swings stay sub-threshold and chronic. Borderline PD (psyche-borderline-pd) shifts mood in hours and reactively to interpersonal triggers, whereas bipolar II episodes last days-to-weeks and run on their own clock.

## Sensory / Behavioral Impact
The character's center of gravity tends to sit in the **depressive** range, with episodic lifts that feel less like a separate state than like the weight briefly coming off.

In **hypomania** the world brightens and quickens without tipping into chaos: more energy, less need for sleep, faster and easier thought, social confidence that comes without effort, output that flows. It is heightened but still *recognizably themselves and still functional* — nothing breaks, nothing alarms, which is precisely why it slips past unnoticed.

In **depression** the familiar weight returns: slowed body, flattened color, a future that shortens to a wall, effort priced out of reach. This is where the character spends most of their time and most of their suffering.

Because the highs are mild and often genuinely pleasant, the character frequently doesn't register them as illness at all — they read as good weeks, not symptoms — which makes the depressive crash feel inexplicable and unearned, a punishment with no visible cause.

## Duration
Episodic and recurrent, with the **depressive pole dominant** in time and burden. Hypomania runs at least four days; depressive episodes weeks to months. Within a single scene the mood state is usually **stable** — render the character inside one phase. Mid-scene movement is intensity, not polarity: a hypomanic character getting sharper and faster, a depressed character briefly lit and then sinking. A clean flip from low to high inside one short scene is clinically unrealistic; the timescale is days, not minutes.

## Presentation & Tells
- **In hypomania:** noticeably *up* — funnier, more talkative, more productive; sleeping less but not depleted.
- Ideas come quick and plans multiply; social magnetism rises; small risks and impulses feel easy and harmless.
- To others it reads as a great week, a creative streak, or someone running slightly hot — not as a symptom.
- **In depression:** slowed speech and movement, withdrawal, flattened or tearful affect, neglected basics, decisions that feel impossible, a body that weighs more.
- **The asymmetry tell:** the character readily narrates the lows and barely mentions — or actively values — the highs; they'll call the hypomania "just a good stretch," which is exactly why the disorder gets missed for years.
- Changed sleep as an early barometer in either direction, often noticed by others before the character names a mood at all.

## Interiority / POV Voice
From inside **hypomania**, the voice is bright, fast, and capable — but not unmoored. The character feels *finally well*, which is the quiet danger: the high reads as health, not symptom, so there's no alarm in it, only relief and momentum. Work flows, words land, the social world opens. They may not even file it as a "state" — it's just the version of themselves that shows up when the weight lifts. Underneath, sometimes, a faint awareness that this never lasts.

From inside **depression** — where this character spends most of their narrating time — the voice goes heavy, slow, and self-foreclosing. The flatness isn't drama; it's *subtraction*, color and want and future drained out, effort priced beyond reach. What makes bipolar II distinctly cruel from the inside is the **whiplash of contrast**: the character remembers, recently and vividly, being competent and light, and cannot reach it now, which the depressive logic reframes as proof that the good version was the lie. The thing they refuse to look at is the pattern itself — that the high they don't count *is* part of the same illness as the low that's drowning them — which is precisely why so many go years describing only the depression.

## Dramatic Affordances
- The **invisible high**: a character whose hypomania reads to everyone (including themselves) as simply a good stretch — and the dramatic irony of a watcher who can see it's the front edge of a cycle.
- **The crash after the good week**: the cruelest, most renderable beat — competence and lightness recently real, now unreachable, and the depressive voice reading the lift as a betrayal.
- **Misdiagnosis as plot**: a character treated for plain depression who never quite fits, the missing hypomania hiding in plain sight.
- **The depression-dominant arc**: most of the lived burden, and most of the quiet truth, lives in the low — the long labor of functioning while the floor is missing.
- A character whose **best self and worst self share a face**, separated only by which way the tide is running.

## Fences — Responsible Use
Real people live with bipolar II and lead full, capable, ordinary lives, especially with treatment. This card renders a *character*, not a verdict. Refuse the cheap portrayals: do not write bipolar II as "bipolar lite" or as mere moodiness — the depressive burden is often **heavier and more lethal** than the public image of "bipolar" suggests, and the suicide risk is serious. Do not erase the hypomania (the way the world often does) and then call it ordinary depression; and do not inflate the hypomania into spectacle — its defining trait is that it *doesn't* rupture functioning. On violence: people with bipolar II are **far more often harmed — by depression, exploitation, and stigma — than harmful to others**. NO operational detail about self-harm, suicide, or substance use — depict the lived experience and outward presentation, never a method or manual.

## Interaction Notes
- With **psyche-bipolar-i**: same family; the dividing line is whether the high ever became full mania. Don't render bipolar II's hypomania with bipolar I's grandiosity, psychosis, or functional collapse.
- With **psyche-major-depressive-disorder**: the depressive episodes render nearly identically in-scene — the difference is the hidden hypomanic history. Useful for a "what's actually wrong" reveal.
- With **psyche-borderline-pd**: easily conflated; if stacking, keep the timescales distinct — bipolar II episodes run days-to-weeks on their own clock, borderline shifts run hours and key to interpersonal triggers.
- With anxiety and substance/addictive conditions: commonly comorbid; anxious-distress can make a low more agitated and a high more irritable.
- With environmental condition cards: sleep loss, seasonal change, and high-stress settings can be written as destabilizers; structure, rest, and routine as stabilizers — especially relevant given how easily this character mistakes a lift for lasting wellness.
