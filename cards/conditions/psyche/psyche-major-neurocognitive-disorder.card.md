# Major Neurocognitive Disorder (Dementia, incl. Alzheimer's type) — Psyche Profile

## Description
This is a psyche-profile condition card. It colors a *character's* behavior, perception, and interiority — not a location. Major neurocognitive disorder (the term now used for what is commonly called dementia) is an acquired, usually progressive decline in one or more cognitive domains — memory, language, executive function, attention, perception, or social cognition — severe enough to interfere with everyday independence. Alzheimer's disease is the most common cause; vascular, Lewy body, and frontotemporal forms each color the picture differently.

## Clinical Profile
- **Taxonomy:** DSM-5-TR — Neurocognitive Disorders, "Major Neurocognitive Disorder" (with etiological subtypes: Alzheimer's, vascular, Lewy body, frontotemporal, etc.). ICD-11 — chapter 06, Neurocognitive disorders grouping; "Dementia" (6D8–) with etiological qualifiers.
- **Core features:**
  - Significant *decline from a previous level* in one or more cognitive domains, established by history and testing.
  - The deficits **interfere with independence** in everyday activities (managing money, medications, navigation, self-care) — this is the line between *major* and *mild* (milder forms preserve independence with effort).
  - Not occurring exclusively during delirium and not better explained by another mental disorder.
  - Subtype colors the leading edge: **Alzheimer's** — insidious memory-first decline; **vascular** — stepwise drops tied to cerebrovascular events; **Lewy body** — fluctuating cognition, visual hallucinations, parkinsonism, REM-sleep behavior; **frontotemporal** — early personality/behavior change or language loss with memory relatively spared at first.
  - Behavioral and psychological symptoms are common: apathy, depression, anxiety, agitation, suspicion/paranoia, disinhibition, wandering, sleep disturbance.
- **Onset & course:** Typically late-life onset (most after ~65; early-onset forms exist). Course is usually **chronic and progressive** over years — Alzheimer's gradually, vascular in steps. Generally not reversible (treatable contributors should always be sought).
- **Prevalence:** Strongly age-dependent — approximately 5–8% of people over 65, rising to roughly a third or more past 85. Alzheimer's accounts for the majority of cases.
- **Common comorbidities:** Depression (psyche-major-depressive-disorder) and apathy are frequent and often under-treated; delirium (psyche-delirium) superimposes easily and dangerously; cerebrovascular disease; in some forms, parkinsonism and sleep disorder.
- **Differential:** Distinguished from **delirium** by gradual onset and day-to-day stability (delirium is acute and fluctuating — and can ride on top). Distinguished from **depression** ("pseudodementia") — depression can blunt cognition but the person complains of memory loss and effortfully says "I don't know," where the person with dementia often minimizes or confabulates. Distinguished from **normal aging** by the *interference with independence*.

## Sensory / Behavioral Impact
The recent past stops sticking. New information slides off; the question just answered is asked again, intact and sincere. Words go missing mid-reach — the thing, you know, the *thing* — and the sentence detours around the hole. Familiar places stop resolving into a map; the route home dissolves. Sequencing breaks: the steps of making tea, dressing in order, come apart. Time loosens — decades-old memory is vivid and present while this morning is gone, and the past can become the present (a grown child becomes the small one; a long-dead spouse is expected at the door). Faces lose their names, then their familiarity. Affect can flatten into apathy or spike into frightened agitation, often late in the day.

## Duration
Chronic and progressive — the trajectory is the condition. Within a single scene the character is relatively *stable* at their current baseline (this is the key contrast with delirium, which swings hour to hour). But within that baseline there is real variability: better in the morning and the familiar, worse when tired, rushed, in unfamiliar surroundings, after dark ("sundowning"), or when an acute illness tips them into superimposed delirium. What shifts a scene: a familiar song or photograph or smell can briefly unlock a person who seemed gone; a strange room, a contradiction, or a demand to "remember" can collapse them into distress.

## Presentation & Tells
- Repeats a question or story within minutes, each time as if new.
- Word-finding pauses; substitutes "thingy," circles a word, loses the sentence.
- Searches for everyday objects; accuses others of moving or stealing what they misplaced (often the most painful tell for family).
- Gets lost somewhere once-familiar; can't work the now-foreign remote, phone, or stove.
- Wears the season's wrong clothes; mismatched, repeated, or neglected dress and hygiene.
- Mistakes the past for the present — calls an adult child by a sibling's name, waits for someone long dead.
- Apathy that reads as "not caring"; or sudden agitation, pacing, wandering, escalating toward evening.
- In Lewy-body presentations: vivid, often non-threatening visual hallucinations (people, animals) and stiff, shuffling movement; in frontotemporal: early loss of tact, empathy, or restraint that strangers read as rudeness.

## Interiority / POV Voice
From inside, the deep past is more real than the present, so the voice should live there — rich, specific, decades-detailed — while the last hour is a smooth blank the character doesn't experience as missing. They do not feel themselves forgetting; they feel *contradicted*, *moved-against*, *somewhere wrong*. The narrating lens fills its own gaps without seam — confabulation is not lying, it is the brain offering its best honest guess and believing it. There is dignity and there is fear, and they alternate: the clear stretches where they know exactly what is being lost and grieve it, and the stretches where the loss is invisible and the world has simply become unreliable and strangers keep insisting on things. Reach for the felt logic: *this is my house and these people won't let me leave it; that woman knows my name and I cannot find hers and I will not show it; my mother is coming, I should tidy up.* Write the held competencies too — the lifelong skill, the hands that still know the piano or the prayer when the words for everything else are gone. The terror is specific: not knowing whether the next thing to go will be the people. And underneath, often, an enormous effort of camouflage — the social cover-story, the deflecting joke, the changed subject — to keep the loss from being seen.

## Dramatic Affordances
This is the long goodbye, and the drama is in the asymmetry: the people around the character grieve a person who is still, sometimes, *right there*. It opens the role-reversal arc — child parenting parent, spouse becoming caregiver, the slow renegotiation of a marriage's whole balance. It opens the unbearable repetition (the same news of a death delivered again and again, or mercifully withheld) and the question of when a comforting lie is a kindness. It surfaces who a person *was* under the editing — the gentle parent who turns suspicious, or the hard one who turns soft. It opens the moment of unexpected return — a song, a grandchild, a clear hour — that is more devastating for being temporary. And it asks the legacy questions: inheritance, capacity, the will, the secret a person carries past their ability to tell it. The quiet truth lives in the small preserved things: the hand that still knows a task, the affection that outlasts the names.

## Fences — Responsible Use
Render a *person*, not a vacancy — the cardinal failure is writing the character as already-gone furniture, an empty body for others to react to. Selfhood, preference, humor, and feeling persist far longer than memory; the inner life does not switch off. Avoid the infantilizing register (the cutesy, the "second childhood" as adorable decline) and the horror-prop register (dementia as creepy, as a monster, as a jump-scare). People with this condition are far more often *frightened, frustrated, and harmed* — by neglect, by being talked over, by restraint — than dangerous; agitation is almost always fear, pain, or unmet need, not malice. Do not treat memory loss as comic. Confabulation is not lying and "correcting" the person into the present is usually cruelty, not care. This is a real condition that millions live with and millions care for; the card renders a character on a hard road, not a verdict on their worth. NO operational detail about exploitation, abuse, or any harm — render lived experience and presentation only.

## Interaction Notes
- **+ psyche-delirium:** the highest-yield and most dangerous pairing. Delirium superimposes on dementia constantly; a sudden steep worsening of a previously stable baseline is delirium until proven otherwise (treatable cause beneath), but it is routinely dismissed as "the dementia progressing." Render the missed-cause stakes.
- **+ psyche-major-depressive-disorder:** depression and apathy are common, under-recognized, and treatable; "pseudodementia" can mimic or mask the picture. A character's flatness may be depression layered on top — a thread worth pulling.
- **+ psyche-tbi-neurocognitive-disorder:** shares the cognitive-decline surface but differs in origin and course (a TBI deficit is typically static or improving from a fixed event, not relentlessly progressive) — useful contrast, occasionally co-occurring in an older injured character.
- **+ environmental condition cards:** unfamiliar rooms, clutter, poor lighting, noise, hurry, and broken sleep all *worsen* the presentation and provoke sundowning; calm, familiarity, routine, and good light steady it. Setting is an active variable here, not backdrop — the same character reads very differently at home in the morning versus a strange ward at night.
