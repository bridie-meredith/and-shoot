# Body Dysmorphic Disorder — Psyche Profile

## Description
This is a psyche-profile condition card. It colors a *character* — their behavior, perception, and interiority — not a location. Body Dysmorphic Disorder (BDD) is a preoccupation with one or more perceived defects in physical appearance that are unobservable or slight to others, experienced as glaring and shameful, and driving repetitive checking, grooming, or concealing behaviors.

## Clinical Profile
- **Taxonomy:** DSM-5-TR — Obsessive-Compulsive and Related Disorders. ICD-11 — Obsessive-compulsive or related disorders (Body dysmorphic disorder, 6B21).
- **Core features:**
  - Preoccupation with one or more perceived flaws in appearance that others see as minimal or not at all.
  - Repetitive behaviors (mirror-checking, excessive grooming, skin-picking, reassurance-seeking) or mental acts (comparing one's appearance with others') in response.
  - Common foci: skin, hair, nose, and other facial features; symmetry; perceived disproportion.
  - Marked distress or impairment — avoidance, social withdrawal, time lost to rituals.
  - Insight is often poor; a meaningful minority hold delusional conviction that the flaw is real and obvious.
  - A "muscle dysmorphia" subtype centers on a belief the body is insufficiently lean or muscular.
- **Onset & course:** Typically adolescence (mid-teens). Chronic if untreated; tends to wax and wane and can worsen with avoidance.
- **Prevalence:** Approximately 1.5–2.5% point prevalence.
- **Common comorbidities:** Major depression (high; elevated suicidality risk), social anxiety, OCD, eating disorders, skin-picking disorder, substance use.
- **Differential:** Distinguish from eating disorders (BDD concern is *appearance/shape of a feature*, not weight-and-eating control per se, though they overlap); from normal appearance concern (BDD is time-consuming, distressing, impairing); from OCD (BDD obsessions are appearance-specific and often less ego-dystonic — the person frequently believes the flaw is *true*).

## Sensory / Behavioral Impact
Perception narrows to a single feature that the character cannot stop tracking — they catch their reflection in every surface, read every face in the room for confirmation of being seen as ugly. Attention splits between the scene and the running comparison. Affect runs ashamed, vigilant, sometimes despairing. Reflective surfaces become magnets *and* threats — approached compulsively, then avoided entirely after a bad look. The body is managed constantly: angled away from light, hair arranged to curtain the feature, a hand drifting up to cover it.

## Duration
Chronic as a condition; intensity swings with mirrors, photographs, lighting, social exposure, and others' attention to the body. A camera, a bright unflattering light, a casual remark, or a public setting can spike it for the rest of a scene. A character may pass through a scene functionally and then collapse inward after a single glimpse of their reflection.

## Presentation & Tells
- Checking reflections — mirrors, windows, phone screens, the back of a spoon — then sometimes covering them or refusing to look.
- Grooming or adjusting one area repeatedly; touching or measuring the feature.
- Camouflage: hair, makeup, hats, high collars, posture and angle managed to hide the perceived flaw.
- Reassurance-seeking phrased obliquely ("Does this look weird?") — and disbelief when reassured.
- Comparing — eyes flicking to others' faces or bodies, sizing up.
- Avoidance of photos, bright rooms, being looked at directly; refusal of social plans framed vaguely.
- Long delays before leaving home; lateness tied to grooming that wouldn't come right.

## Interiority / POV Voice
You don't see a face in the mirror — you see *the thing*, and once you've seen it you can't unsee it, it's the whole room, it's what everyone is looking at because how could they not. They're being kind. They're not saying it. That's worse. You move through the day curating angles, calculating light, because there's a version of you that's almost bearable from exactly the right three-quarter turn and you spend energy nobody can see keeping yourself in it. The mirror is a trap you keep walking into — *just check, just make sure it's not as bad as you fear* — and it's always as bad, and you check again. Other people's faces are a referendum; a glance held a half-second too long is a verdict. You know, distantly, that they say it's slight, that they swear they don't see it. They're wrong, or they're polite, and either way the proof is right there in the glass. The cruelty is the specificity: not "I'm ugly" in some vague adolescent way, but this exact feature, mapped in detail, returned to a thousand times a day, a private deformity only you are honest enough to acknowledge.

## Dramatic Affordances
BDD opens scenes of hidden suffering under a composed surface — a character who declines the photo, sits with their bad side to the wall, leaves the party early for a reason no one's given. It strains intimacy: a partner's reassurance that can never land, a lover who can't understand why being told they're beautiful brings tears of frustration. It powers avoidance arcs and the high cost of being seen. The drama lives in the gap between how the character is perceived (often unremarkably, sometimes attractively) and the disfigurement they alone witness — and in the danger that the despair runs deep. The quiet truth lives in how much labor goes into appearing fine.

## Fences — Responsible Use
BDD is not vanity and not fishing for compliments — it is a painful, often debilitating illness, and treating it as shallow is a core misrepresentation. Do not write the perceived flaw as real and objectively visible to the audience; the point is the *perception*, and rendering the feature as genuinely grotesque collapses the condition into mere ugliness. Reassurance does not cure it and should not be written as a fix. BDD carries a serious depression and suicidality burden; handle that gravity responsibly and without sensationalism — describe lived experience and outward presentation, never operational self-harm detail. Real people live with this; the card renders a character, not a verdict.

## Interaction Notes
Stacks closely with `psyche-ocd` (shared ritual/checking architecture, appearance-themed), `psyche-excoriation` (skin-picking is a frequent overlapping behavior, especially with skin-focused BDD), `psyche-anorexia-nervosa` and other eating disorders (shared body-image distortion; the line is feature-vs-weight focus, often blurred), and `psyche-social-anxiety-disorder` (the fear of being negatively evaluated for appearance fuses the two). Depressive conditions deepen the risk. Environmental condition cards amplify it: harsh or clinical lighting, mirrored or reflective sets, a photographed or surveilled setting, and any scene built around being looked at.
