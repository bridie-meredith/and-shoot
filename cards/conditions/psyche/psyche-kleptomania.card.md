# Kleptomania — Psyche Profile

## Description
This is a psyche-profile condition card. It colors a *character's* behavior, perception, and interiority — not a location. Kleptomania is recurrent failure to resist impulses to steal objects that are not needed for use or for their monetary value, preceded by mounting tension and followed by relief or pleasure — and, almost always, by shame. Its one-line essence: a person who steals things they don't want, can't stop, and despises themselves for it.

## Clinical Profile
- **Taxonomy:** DSM-5-TR, Disruptive, Impulse-Control, and Conduct Disorders. ICD-11: Impulse control disorders (Kleptomania, 6C70).
- **Core features:**
  - Recurrent failure to resist impulses to steal objects not needed for personal use or monetary value.
  - Rising tension immediately before the theft.
  - Pleasure, gratification, or relief at the moment of the act.
  - The stealing is not done out of anger or vengeance and is not a response to a delusion or hallucination.
  - The behavior is ego-dystonic — the person experiences it as wrong and distressing, and typically conceals it.
  - Taken items are frequently hoarded, given away, secretly returned, or discarded — the object is beside the point.
- **Onset & course:** Often begins in adolescence or young adulthood; the course is typically chronic and fluctuating, waxing and waning with stress over years, often with long secrecy before any help is sought.
- **Prevalence:** Rare — approximately 0.3–0.6% of the general population; markedly more common in women than men in clinical samples.
- **Common comorbidities:** Mood disorders (especially depression), anxiety disorders, OCD and obsessive-compulsive-spectrum conditions, other impulse-control and addictive disorders (including gambling), and eating disorders (notably bulimia nervosa).
- **Differential:** Distinguish from ordinary shoplifting/theft (which is motivated by gain, need, or defiance and is not preceded by the tension-relief cycle); from theft in conduct disorder or antisocial PD (instrumental, unashamed); and from stealing during a manic episode or in response to psychosis.

## Sensory / Behavioral Impact
The character moves through retail and domestic space with a
doubled awareness — the ordinary surface, and beneath it a
constant low static of urge and resistance. Attention is split:
half on the conversation, half on the unattended thing within
reach, the small object that no one is watching.

The pre-theft tension is bodily — a tightening, a quickening, a
held breath, a heat — and the act discharges it like a struck
match, a hot wash of relief that lasts seconds before the cold
arrives. Vigilance about being seen runs high at all times, so
they read sightlines and exits reflexively even when they have no
intention to take.

Energy spikes around the act and crashes after into a flat, sick
aftermath. Outside the urge they may be entirely present and
warm — it is opportunity, and stress, that brings the second
awareness forward.

## Duration
Chronic and fluctuating across a life; episodic in any given scene. Within a scene the urge builds along a curve — opportunity, tension, the narrowing fixation, the act or the white-knuckled resistance, the aftermath. What raises the pressure: stress, loss, loneliness, boredom, the simple presence of an easy, low-stakes opportunity. What lowers it: distraction, company that keeps them present, removing themselves from the trigger environment, or the cost of the last episode still being fresh. Periods of remission can run long; a single bad week can undo them.

## Presentation & Tells
- Reflexive scanning of sightlines and staff positions, even when
  not intending to take — a learned wariness that never fully
  switches off.
- A held, distracted quality near unattended small objects; the
  conversation thins as the focus narrows to the one thing.
- Hands that don't quite settle; a pocket touched, a bag adjusted,
  an object turned over and set down and picked up again.
- After the act: a flush, an over-bright normalcy, a too-quick
  exit, a sudden change of subject.
- Drawers, boxes, or bags at home full of unused, unexplained
  things — never sold, often never even unwrapped, tags still on.
- Secret returns, anonymous gifts, items quietly left behind
  elsewhere — the disposal that betrays the lack of motive.
- Disproportionate dread of stores, security, or being searched; a
  flinch at the word *theft*; an over-paid honesty about small
  things, as if to balance the ledger.
- Profound, hidden shame — the behavior is concealed even from
  intimates, sometimes for years, sometimes for a lifetime.

## Interiority / POV Voice
It is not about wanting the thing — that's the part no one
believes, including, often, themselves. The voice runs on a
different circuit: the pressure builds with no name and no off
switch, and the object becomes the place all of it concentrates,
the one move that will make the static stop. *Just this, just once
more, and then never.*

In the seconds of the act there is relief so clean it feels like
sanity returning — and that's the cruelty of it, that the worst
thing they do is the only thing that quiets them. Then the cold
comes: the held thing in the pocket suddenly absurd, unwanted,
evidence; the contempt, vast and familiar; the calculus of how to
get rid of it, how to never be this again.

They are usually law-abiding, often scrupulous in every other
corner of their lives, which makes the gap unbearable — *I am not
a thief, and I keep stealing.* The two facts will not reconcile,
and the not-reconciling is its own daily weight.

What they refuse to look at: that the urge isn't about objects at
all, and that the empty place it's trying to fill won't be filled
by anything they can put in a pocket.

## Dramatic Affordances
- The respectable, conscientious character with a secret that contradicts everything visible about them — the slow reveal, and the question of who finds out.
- The intimacy of being known: the partner or friend who discovers the drawer of unused things and has to understand before they can judge.
- The agony of the near-miss, the held breath, the urge resisted at cost — quiet, high-tension scenes built entirely on stillness.
- A mystery engine: unexplained objects, anonymous returns, a thief whose loot makes no sense.
- The quiet truth: the stealing is a flare sent up by some deeper ache — grief, emptiness, a need for control over *something* — and the real scene is the one that finally asks what's underneath.

## Fences — Responsible Use
Kleptomania is rare and is NOT a quirky-thief trope, a euphemism for shoplifting, or a charming eccentricity — refuse the comic-pickpocket and the glamorous-cat-burglar framings, which describe motivated theft, the opposite of this condition. The defining truth is that the person does not want the objects and is ashamed of the compulsion; render the distress, not a heist. Do not write it as simple criminality or moral failing; it is an impulse-control disorder the person experiences as alien and unwanted, and most people who shoplift do NOT have it. Honor the real shame and the real suffering without sensationalizing either. The card renders a character, not a verdict, and never an instruction in how to steal — no method, no evasion technique, no operational detail of any kind.

## Interaction Notes
- **With psyche-ocd:** The tension-relief loop and the ego-dystonic distress echo OCD's compulsions; a character can carry both, and the kleptomanic urge can feel, from inside, like a compulsion they're resisting. Keep them distinct — OCD compulsions reduce a feared outcome; the theft reduces undifferentiated pressure.
- **With psyche-major-depressive-disorder:** A very common pairing; depressive emptiness and stress raise the urge, and the shame deepens the depression — a feedback loop worth playing across an arc.
- **With psyche-gambling-disorder / other addictive disorders:** Shares the tension-relief-shame mechanism; a character may have a constellation of impulse-control problems with a common emotional engine.
- **With psyche-bulimia-nervosa:** A documented co-occurrence; both involve secret, compulsive, shame-laden cycles of buildup and discharge — staging them in one character makes the shared machinery visible.
- **With environmental condition cards:** Settings dense with small, accessible, unattended objects (a market, a shop, a crowded party) raise the ambient pressure; isolation and stress raise it from the other direction.
