# Binge-Eating Disorder — Psyche Profile

## Description
A psyche-profile condition card that colors a *character's* behavior, perception, and interiority — not a location. Binge-eating disorder is defined by recurrent episodes of eating with a felt loss of control, marked by distress, and — unlike bulimia — without regular compensatory behavior afterward; the wreckage stays in the body and the shame stays in the head.

## Clinical Profile
- **Taxonomy:** DSM-5-TR — Feeding and Eating Disorders. ICD-11 — Feeding or eating disorders, code grouping 6B82. The most common eating disorder.
- **Core features:**
  - Recurrent binge episodes: eating an unusually large amount in a discrete period with a sense of being out of control.
  - Episodes carry several distress markers — eating fast, eating past comfortable fullness, eating when not hungry, eating alone from embarrassment, feeling disgusted or guilty afterward.
  - Marked distress about the bingeing itself.
  - The binges recur over time with a defining regularity.
  - Crucially, NO regular inappropriate compensatory behavior — this is what separates it from `psyche-bulimia-nervosa`.
- **Onset & course:** Onset commonly in late adolescence through the twenties, though it can begin later. Course is often chronic and relapsing; frequently associated with weight gain and the medical and social consequences that follow, and with a long history of stigma.
- **Prevalence:** Approximately 1–3% lifetime; the most prevalent eating disorder and more evenly distributed across genders than anorexia or bulimia.
- **Common comorbidities:** `psyche-major-depressive-disorder`, anxiety disorders (`psyche-generalized-anxiety-disorder`), `psyche-adhd` (impulsivity and reward dysregulation), and trauma histories.
- **Differential:** Distinguished from `psyche-bulimia-nervosa` by the absence of compensatory behavior; from ordinary overeating by the loss of control, the distress, and the recurrent pattern; not the same as simply having a large appetite.

## Sensory / Behavioral Impact
The character carries a low, persistent hum of food-preoccupation that surges into episodes. During an episode, awareness narrows — the room recedes, time blurs, the act runs almost on autopilot, and the character is both inside it and watching from a distance. Afterward comes physical heaviness, sluggishness, self-disgust, and a deep wish to be unseen. Between episodes there is dread of the next one and exhausting effort spent on resolve. The body is a site of shame the character would rather not inhabit.

## Duration
Chronic and episodic. The baseline is a manageable preoccupation; under stress, loneliness, boredom, restriction, or strong emotion the pull intensifies and an episode can follow. Within a scene the character can be entirely present and composed; the vulnerability shows at transitions — alone, after conflict, late at night.

## Presentation & Tells
- Eating alone, secretly, or differently in private than in company — small public plate, hidden private one.
- Disappearing food; wrappers tucked away; a quiet relationship with the kitchen at off hours.
- Eating fast and past fullness when an episode is underway, with a glazed, elsewhere quality.
- Self-deprecating talk about food, the body, or willpower; flinching from body comment.
- A cycle of public resolve — diets announced, rules set — that the others never see broken.
- Withdrawal, low mood, and self-isolation around eating; avoidance of being seen eating.
- The weight of carried shame in how the character holds themselves in a room.

## Interiority / POV Voice
It starts as a feeling with no food-shaped reason — empty, frayed, lonely, too full of something — and food is the nearest, fastest comfort, and the character reaches for it the way someone reaches for a hand in the dark. For a few minutes the reaching works: the noise quiets, the want is met, there is a numb, blank peace where the distress was. And then the peace turns over and becomes its underside, and the character is sitting in the aftermath asking *why, again, why can't I just stop*, as if stopping were a thing willpower had ever managed. The disgust is not really about the food. It is older and more total — a verdict on the self that the episode seems to prove all over again. The character makes the resolution sincerely every time, and breaks it sincerely every time, and the breaking becomes more evidence for the prosecution. Most of all they want not to be seen — eating, or wanting, or being the kind of person who does this. So they do it alone, and the aloneness is both the shelter and the wound.

## Dramatic Affordances
This condition gives a character a private struggle that the world routinely misreads as mere indulgence, which makes the gap between how they're seen and what they're carrying a rich seam to mine. The drama lives in shame and concealment, in the comfort that betrays, in the resolve that keeps failing not for lack of trying but because the problem was never about food. It opens scenes of loneliness, of self-isolation, of a person managing an inner life no one suspects behind a competent or cheerful surface. The quiet truth lives in being met without judgment — the friend who finds the wrappers and stays, the moment the underlying ache gets named. Arcs run on separating the food from the feeling underneath it.

## Fences — Responsible Use
Real people live with this; the card renders a character, not a verdict, and never a method. Do NOT specify quantities, foods-as-instructions, or anything actionable — name that episodes of loss-of-control eating occur; never write them as a guide. Refuse the cruelest myth: this is not laziness, greed, or lack of willpower, and a character's body size is not a measure of their virtue or their illness. Reject fat-shaming framings entirely — the disorder is the loss of control and the distress, not a number, and people of any size can have it or not have it. Do not use the character's body as a punchline or a moral lesson. Recovery addresses the emotional driver, not just the eating; do not stage a diet as a cure.

## Interaction Notes
Stacks with `psyche-major-depressive-disorder` and `psyche-generalized-anxiety-disorder` (the affective fuel for episodes) and with `psyche-adhd` (impulsivity, reward dysregulation, and difficulty with the pause between urge and act). Shares the binge phenomenon with `psyche-bulimia-nervosa` but differs in the absence of compensation — a character drifting toward compensatory behavior is shifting diagnostic ground, which can itself be dramatic. In environmental conditions of scarcity, isolation, or chronic stress the pull intensifies; in conditions of constant company the secrecy strains and the struggle surfaces.
