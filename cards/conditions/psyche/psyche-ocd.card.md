# Obsessive-Compulsive Disorder — Psyche Profile

## Description
This is a psyche-profile condition card. It colors a *character* — their behavior, perception, and interiority — not a location. Obsessive-Compulsive Disorder (OCD) is a cycle in which unwanted, intrusive thoughts, images, or urges (obsessions) drive distress, and ritualized acts or mental routines (compulsions) are performed to neutralize that distress, in a loop that never quite resolves.

## Clinical Profile
- **Taxonomy:** DSM-5-TR — Obsessive-Compulsive and Related Disorders. ICD-11 — Obsessive-compulsive or related disorders (grouping 6B2; OCD at 6B20).
- **Core features:**
  - Recurrent, intrusive, ego-dystonic thoughts/images/urges the person experiences as unwanted and tries to suppress or neutralize.
  - Repetitive behaviors or mental acts (checking, washing, counting, ordering, silent reviewing, reassurance-seeking) performed to reduce anxiety or prevent a feared outcome.
  - The compulsions are excessive or not realistically connected to what they aim to prevent.
  - Time-consuming (often >1 hour/day) or causing marked distress and functional impairment.
  - Insight varies — most retain some awareness the fears are unreasonable; a minority hold them with near-delusional conviction.
  - Common themes: contamination, harm/responsibility, symmetry/"just-right," taboo intrusive thoughts (aggressive, sexual, blasphemous), relationship and existential doubt.
- **Onset & course:** Typically adolescence to early adulthood (often earlier in males). Chronic and waxing-waning if untreated; responsive to treatment (ERP, SSRIs).
- **Prevalence:** Approximately 1–2% lifetime.
- **Common comorbidities:** Depression, anxiety disorders, tic disorders, body dysmorphic disorder, hoarding, skin-picking and hair-pulling, eating disorders.
- **Differential:** Distinguish from generalized worry (OCD fears are intrusive, ego-dystonic, ritual-bound vs. realistic-themed worry); from OCPD (a personality style of rigidity/perfectionism the person finds *ego-syntonic* — no intrusive obsessions, no relieving rituals); from psychosis (OCD obsessions are recognized as one's own thoughts, not external).

## Sensory / Behavioral Impact
Attention is hijacked and split — part of the mind tracks the scene, part runs the loop. The character scans the environment for triggers (a doorknob, an off-center stack, a knife on the counter) and for the relief of a completed ritual. Affect runs anxious, braced, often quietly exhausted; a finished ritual brings a brief, incomplete exhale before the doubt creeps back. Speech can stall on a pending count or a half-finished phrase that "has to" be redone. The body carries the cost: raw hands, worn thresholds re-crossed, a hovering hand that won't quite commit.

## Duration
Chronic and persistent as a condition; symptom *intensity* is highly state-dependent within a scene. A trigger, time pressure, a feared-responsibility cue, or fatigue can spike it; a completed ritual, distraction, or a trusted person's presence can ease it. Resisting a compulsion raises anxiety sharply before it falls — so a scene can pivot on whether the character gives in or holds the line.

## Presentation & Tells
- Repeated checking — locks, stove, switches, a bag's contents — often in fixed counts or a fixed order.
- Hand-washing or surface-cleaning past the point of cleanliness; visible chapped, reddened skin.
- Touching, tapping, or aligning objects until they feel "right"; redoing an action that was interrupted.
- Lips moving on a silent count or phrase; a pause mid-sentence, then a restart from the top.
- Reassurance-seeking — the same question asked again in slightly different words.
- Avoidance of triggers framed as preference ("I'll let you get the door").
- Lateness or last-out-of-the-room because the exit ritual ran long.

## Interiority / POV Voice
The thought arrives uninvited and lands like a splinter — *what if the stove's on, what if you meant it, what if you let it happen* — and the horror is precisely that it's yours, surfacing from your own mind against everything you want. You know, in the daylit part of you, that it's nonsense. That knowing buys nothing. The doubt is a hunger that eats certainty as fast as you feed it; every reassurance is digested in seconds and the question reopens. So you pay the toll. One more check. One more wash. Just this once and then you're free — except the once was a lie the last hundred times too. There's a private arithmetic running under everything: even/odd, done/not-done, safe/not-safe, and the world won't release you until the sum comes out right. You become a connoisseur of the *almost* — almost-right, almost-clean, the ritual that didn't quite land and so has to be redone from the start. People think the fear is dirt or fire. The real fear is responsibility: that the disaster will be yours because you could have prevented it and didn't perform the act that would have. You'd rather be late, be raw-handed, be tired, than carry that.

## Dramatic Affordances
OCD opens scenes of private siege inside ordinary moments — a character frozen at a doorway while the plot waits, a goodbye stretched thin by an exit ritual, a relationship strained by reassurance the partner can't keep supplying. It puts an honorable instinct (don't let harm happen on my watch) into a disease that turns it against the person. Drama lives in the choice to resist — a held breath, a hand pulled back from the lock — and in the shame of being seen mid-ritual. The quiet truth lives in the gap between a sharp, capable mind and a loop that mind cannot out-argue: the character is not irrational about the world, only trapped in one corner of it.

## Fences — Responsible Use
OCD is not tidiness, not a fondness for neatness, not a quirk — "I'm so OCD about my desk" is a misuse that erases a genuinely distressing illness. The hallmark is *unwanted* intrusive thought and *distressing* ritual, not preference. Taboo intrusive thoughts (violent, sexual, blasphemous) are a recognized symptom and are the opposite of intent — the person is horrified by them; never write the intrusive thought as a hidden wish or as foreshadowing of real action. Insight is usually intact; do not portray the character as believing their fears are reasonable. Real people live with OCD and most function, work, and love around it; the card renders a character, not a verdict, and never a punchline. NO operational detail that frames rituals as a how-to.

## Interaction Notes
Stacks tightly with its OCD-spectrum siblings: `psyche-body-dysmorphic-disorder` (appearance-checking rituals), `psyche-hoarding-disorder` (when "what if I need it / what if discarding causes harm" drives saving), and `psyche-excoriation` (body-focused repetitive behavior). With `psyche-generalized-anxiety-disorder` the realistic-worry and intrusive-ritual loops compound and exhaust. With depressive conditions, ritual fatigue and hopelessness feed each other. Environmental condition cards amplify it: time-pressure cues, ambiguous-threat settings (a kitchen, a clinic, a road), and clutter or disorder in the set all raise the trigger load.
