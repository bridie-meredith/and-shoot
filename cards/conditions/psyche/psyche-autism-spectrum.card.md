# Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) — Psyche Profile

## Description
This is a psyche-profile condition card: it colors a *character's* behavior, perception, and interiority (not a location). Autism is a lifelong neurodevelopmental difference in social communication and in how sensory and information input is processed — a different operating system, not a damaged one — typically with focused interests, a need for predictability, and distinctive sensory experience.

## Clinical Profile
- **Taxonomy:** DSM-5-TR — Neurodevelopmental Disorders → Autism Spectrum Disorder (a single spectrum diagnosis with severity levels 1–3 across two domains, replacing the older Asperger's / PDD-NOS split). ICD-11 — 6A02 Autism spectrum disorder.
- **Core features:**
  - Differences in social communication and reciprocity: reading and producing nonverbal cues, back-and-forth conversation, relationships.
  - Restricted, repetitive patterns: routines and rituals, intense focused interests, repetitive movement or speech (stimming, echolalia).
  - Insistence on sameness; distress at unexpected change or transitions.
  - Sensory differences: hyper- or hypo-sensitivity to sound, light, texture, touch, taste, smell, pain, interoception.
  - Literal, precise, or direct communication style; difficulty with implied meaning, sarcasm, social scripts.
  - Wide range of language and cognitive ability — from non-speaking to highly verbal; co-occurring intellectual disability in a minority.
- **Onset & course:** Present from early development, often recognized in early childhood; some (especially those who mask) identified much later. Lifelong; presentation evolves but the core difference persists.
- **Prevalence:** Approximately 1–2.8% of the population (around 1 in 36 children in recent estimates); diagnosed more often in boys, with girls and women historically under-recognized.
- **Common comorbidities:** ADHD (psyche-adhd), anxiety, depression, OCD (psyche-ocd), intellectual disability (psyche-intellectual-disability), epilepsy, gastrointestinal issues, sleep problems.
- **Differential:** Distinguished from social anxiety (where social skill exists but fear blocks it), from ADHD (attention vs. social-communication core), and from intellectual disability (which can co-occur but is a separate construct of general cognitive ability).

## Sensory / Behavioral Impact
The world arrives at full volume and unfiltered — a flickering light, a tag in a collar, a refrigerator's hum can be as loud as a conversation, and the conversation itself runs without subtitles: tone, subtext, and shifting social rules are present to others but not handed over. The character expends real labor decoding what neurotypical people do automatically, which is tiring. Routine and predictability are not preference but regulation — the known path frees the mind for everything else. A focused interest is a place of deep, ordered pleasure and competence. Movement (rocking, hand motion, pacing) discharges overload or expresses joy. Change without warning lands as a small system shock.

## Duration
Lifelong and chronic, but moment-to-moment regulation is dynamic. Within a scene, accumulating sensory or social load builds toward overload or shutdown; an unexpected change can tip a calm character into distress. Predictability, a quiet environment, access to the special interest, a trusted person, or stim access restore regulation. The same character can be fluent and easy in a known setting and overwhelmed in a loud, novel one an hour later.

## Presentation & Tells
- Direct, precise, sometimes blunt speech; says what's true, misses the diplomatic detour.
- Limited or differently-timed eye contact; looking away to listen better.
- Steady, deep talk about a focused interest — detailed, organized, hard to stop.
- Stimming: rocking, hand-flapping, finger movements, repeating a phrase, fidget objects.
- Takes figures of speech literally; pauses on sarcasm; asks what someone "really meant."
- Visible distress at schedule changes, surprises, or interrupted routines.
- Sensory guarding: covering ears, squinting, avoiding certain textures or foods, recoiling from unexpected touch.
- Scripted social phrases that can sound formal or rehearsed; flat or differently-modulated prosody.
- After heavy social/sensory load: withdrawal, fewer words, shutdown — or a meltdown (overload, not tantrum).

## Interiority / POV Voice
From inside, the world is high-resolution and high-bandwidth — detail-first, where most people see gist. The narrator notices the precise, the patterned, the inconsistent; an asymmetry or a broken rule snags and won't release. Social interaction is a foreign language they have become fluent in by study, not by birth — running a constant background process of *what does that face mean, what's the right thing to say, did I say it right, are they upset* — and the lag is exhausting in a way invisible to everyone watching them pass. There is a clean, almost physical relief in the right routine, the right object, the right deep subject — the interest is not a hobby but a home, ordered and reliable and good. Honesty is the default setting; the social expectation to soften or imply truth can feel like being asked to lie. Sensory overload isn't metaphor — it stacks, a real rising pressure, until words drop out and the only move is to leave, to go quiet, to rock it down. Underneath, often, a long history of being told they're rude, cold, weird, too much, not enough eye contact — and the private exhaustion of masking, of performing a normal that costs more than anyone sees, and the deep relief of the rare person or place where the mask can come off.

## Dramatic Affordances
The character sees what the group has agreed not to see — the inconsistency in the story, the unfair rule, the elephant nobody's naming — and says it plainly, which is by turns clarifying, embarrassing, and brave. They bring deep expertise from the focused interest exactly when it's needed (and sometimes exactly when it isn't). Drama lives in the translation gap: the meant-well bluntness that wounds, the missed signal that costs a relationship, the meltdown read as drama by people who don't understand the load behind it. The quiet truth lives in loyalty and integrity — they tend to mean exactly what they say — and in the cost of masking, the relief of accommodation, the specific tenderness of being understood without translation. Strong material: a found person or community where they don't have to perform; the moment an accommodation is offered without being asked for.

## Fences — Responsible Use
Real people are autistic; this card renders a character, not a curiosity or a lesson. Refuse the savant cliché (most autistic people are not gifted prodigies), the cold-robot-without-feeling cliché (autistic people feel deeply; they express and read differently), and the puzzle-piece-to-be-solved framing. Do not equate autism with intellectual disability — they are distinct and only sometimes co-occur. Do not write autistic people as incapable of love, humor, or relationship. Honor the diversity of the spectrum: non-speaking does not mean non-thinking; high-masking does not mean low-need. Center autistic personhood and self-advocacy; avoid the cure narrative and the burden-on-the-family framing as the only register. Sensory and social difference is rendered as lived experience, not deficit-to-be-pitied.

## Interaction Notes
Stacks commonly with psyche-adhd ("AuDHD" — sameness-need and novelty-need pull against each other, a real internal tension worth dramatizing) and with psyche-ocd (rituals and ordering can blur but differ in felt drive — relief/regulation vs. anxiety-neutralization). With anxiety and depression (very common), social load and masking fatigue deepen. With psyche-intellectual-disability the support needs compound but the constructs stay distinct. Environmental condition cards are load-bearing: loud, bright, crowded, unpredictable settings push toward overload; quiet, structured, low-demand settings let the same character be regulated and capable.
