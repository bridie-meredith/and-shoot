# Intermittent Explosive Disorder — Psyche Profile

## Description
This is a psyche-profile condition card. It colors a *character's* behavior, perception, and interiority — not a location. Intermittent Explosive Disorder (IED) is recurrent failure to resist aggressive impulses, producing outbursts — verbal tirades or physical aggression — grossly out of proportion to the provocation. Its one-line essence: a person who is mostly fine, then briefly, suddenly, not — and who lives in the long shadow between the eruptions, ashamed.

## Clinical Profile
- **Taxonomy:** DSM-5-TR, Disruptive, Impulse-Control, and Conduct Disorders. ICD-11 does not list IED as a distinct entity; the picture is captured under impulse-control/disruptive-behaviour groupings.
- **Core features:**
  - Recurrent behavioral outbursts representing a failure to control aggressive impulses.
  - High-frequency, lower-intensity verbal/non-damaging aggression (e.g. twice weekly over months), and/or lower-frequency outbursts causing damage, injury, or assault.
  - The aggression is grossly out of proportion to the trigger and to any precipitating stressor.
  - Outbursts are impulsive and/or anger-based — not premeditated, not committed to achieve a tangible goal (money, power, intimidation).
  - They cause marked distress, impairment, or consequences (legal, financial, relational).
  - The outburst is typically brief (often under thirty minutes) with a rapid escalation and a crash afterward.
- **Onset & course:** Onset is most common in late childhood/adolescence; the course is typically chronic and persistent over years, with the outbursts themselves episodic.
- **Prevalence:** Approximately 2–3% lifetime, depending on threshold; among the more common impulse-control disorders.
- **Common comorbidities:** Mood disorders, anxiety disorders, substance use disorders, ADHD, ODD and conduct disorder; histories of trauma are frequent.
- **Differential:** Distinguish from aggression that is *purposeful* (antisocial PD, predatory violence — IED gets nothing it wanted); from outbursts better explained by intoxication, psychosis, mania, or a head injury; from ODD/conduct disorder (where the broader pattern, not the discrete loss of control, defines the picture); and from the ordinary, justified anger of a provoked person.

## Sensory / Behavioral Impact
Between outbursts the character may be unremarkable — even mild,
conscientious, well-liked. The condition lives in the threshold: a
fast, full-body activation that arrives almost before awareness, a
narrowing of vision, a roar of pressure, a sense of the world
going red-edged and far away.

Affect regulation has a cliff in it rather than a slope — there is
no gradual climb the people around them can ride out, only the
calm and then the drop. During an outburst, fine motor control and
language coarsen; thought goes loud and simple.

Afterward comes a physical crash — trembling, exhaustion,
sometimes a blank where the worst seconds should be. Attention in
the run-up snaps onto the trigger to the exclusion of everything
else, including consequences, including the faces of the people
they love.

## Duration
Chronic disorder, episodic eruption. Within a scene the baseline is usually calm; the outburst is a brief spike (seconds to a half-hour) followed by a long recovery and a longer remorse. What shifts it mid-scene: a sudden frustration, a perceived disrespect, a blocked goal, a touch when keyed up — any of which can flip the switch with little warning. What pulls it back: early exit, space, a calm voice that does not match the escalation, removal of the trigger before the cliff. Sleep loss, alcohol, heat, and accumulated stress lower the threshold across a whole sequence.

## Presentation & Tells
- A visible "tell" window before the break: jaw set, color
  rising, voice dropping or climbing, a stillness that is the
  opposite of calm.
- The escalation is disproportionate and fast — zero to maximum
  over a trivial trigger, baffling to onlookers who didn't see the
  pressure stacking.
- Verbal eruption: a sudden tirade, naming things that can't be
  unnamed, aimed with a precision that surprises even them.
- Physical discharge aimed at objects as often as people — a
  slammed door, a thrown phone, a punched wall.
- The crash: shaking, sudden quiet, sitting down, hands over the
  face, the body spent.
- Profuse, genuine remorse afterward — and the bewildered "I don't
  know what came over me," which is closer to true than it sounds.
- A pattern of broken things and apologized-to people; a wake of
  small repairs, replaced phones, patched walls.
- Between episodes: hypervigilance about their own triggers,
  sometimes avoidance of situations — bars, arguments, traffic —
  that might set them off.

## Interiority / POV Voice
The inside of an outburst is not deliberation — it is being
driven. There is a moment they can feel it coming, a pressure
stacking with nowhere to go, and a thinning window in which they
could still stop, and then the window closes and something else
has the wheel.

From inside it can feel *justified*, righteous even, the
provocation looming huge and the response proportional — that's
the distortion; only afterward does the scale snap back and they
see what they did to what. The aftermath is the truest part of the
voice: the cold flood of shame, the inventory of damage, the face
of the person they frightened, the private vow — *never again* —
that they have made and broken before.

They are not cruel between the breaks; many are unusually gentle,
*because* they know what they hold, *because* they have seen what
they're capable of and spend the calm hours making sure it stays
caged. The thing they refuse to look at squarely: the fear that
the calm self and the exploding self are the same self, and that
the people they love are right to flinch.

## Dramatic Affordances
- The slow-burn dread of a scene where everyone is managing the air around one person — and the audience can feel the threshold being approached.
- The relationship organized around the aftermath: the partner who measures distance, the friend who learned the tells, the child who got quiet.
- Redemption-and-relapse arcs; the gap between sincere remorse and changed behavior; the work of actually controlling it.
- The character whose gentleness is a discipline, not a nature — restraint as the daily, invisible heroism.
- The quiet truth: the loudest scene is not the outburst; it's the one after, in the wreckage, where they have to decide who they are.

## Fences — Responsible Use
People with IED are not "ticking time bombs" or monsters — most periods of their lives are non-violent, the remorse is real, and the condition is treatable. Refuse the lazy equation of IED with evil or with cold, calculated abuse: the defining feature is *loss of control over impulsive aggression*, not the pursuit of dominance — that distinction matters and is dramatically richer. At the same time, do not romanticize the harm or excuse it; the damage to others is real and the card should never frame an outburst as the partner's fault or as secretly admirable. Crucially: people with mental illness are far more often the victims of violence than its perpetrators, and the great majority are never violent at all. NO operational detail of any kind — no method, no how-to. Render the lived experience and the aftermath; never a manual.

## Interaction Notes
- **With psyche-oppositional-defiant-disorder / psyche-conduct-disorder:** Common developmental neighbors; a character can carry an oppositional baseline that primes the explosive break. Keep one register dominant so the audience can read which is driving a given beat.
- **With psyche-antisocial-pd:** A sharp contrast worth playing — antisocial aggression is *instrumental* and remorse-free; IED aggression is impulsive and drowning in remorse. Putting them in a scene together makes the line legible.
- **With psyche-ptsd:** Trauma histories are frequent; a startle or trauma-trigger can be the spark, and the hyperarousal lowers the threshold. The outburst then carries a second meaning — protection misfiring.
- **With environmental condition cards:** Heat, crowding, noise, intoxication, sleep deprivation, and high-stakes time pressure all lower the threshold; stage them as the slow tightening before a break.
