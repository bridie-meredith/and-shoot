# Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder — Psyche Profile

## Description
A psyche-profile condition card. It colors a *character's* behavior, perception, and interiority — not a location. PTSD is the persistence of a survival response past the danger that warranted it: the body keeps fighting a war the calendar says is over, and the past intrudes on the present without permission.

## Clinical Profile
- **Taxonomy:** DSM-5-TR — Trauma- and Stressor-Related Disorders (code 309.81 / F43.10). ICD-11 — Disorders specifically associated with stress (6B40).
- **Core features:**
  - Exposure to actual or threatened death, serious injury, or sexual violence — directly, witnessed, learned of in a loved one, or through repeated exposure to aversive detail.
  - **Intrusion:** unwanted memories, nightmares, and flashbacks in which the trauma is re-experienced as if happening now.
  - **Avoidance:** steering away from reminders — people, places, conversations, internal sensations that resemble the event.
  - **Negative alterations in cognition and mood:** persistent fear, horror, guilt, or shame; distorted blame; emotional numbing; loss of interest; detachment.
  - **Alterations in arousal and reactivity:** hypervigilance, exaggerated startle, irritability, sleep disturbance, difficulty concentrating, reckless behavior.
  - Symptoms persist more than one month and cause significant distress or impairment. A dissociative subtype (depersonalization/derealization) is specified.
- **Onset & course:** any age after a qualifying event; onset usually within three months, but delayed expression (months to years) occurs. Course is variable — many recover within a year; a substantial minority become chronic.
- **Prevalence:** approximately 6–7% lifetime in the general population; far higher among combat veterans, assault survivors, and first responders. Women are diagnosed roughly twice as often as men.
- **Common comorbidities:** depression (psyche-major-depressive-disorder), substance use (psyche-alcohol-use-disorder), panic and other anxiety disorders, complex PTSD (psyche-complex-ptsd) where trauma was prolonged.
- **Differential:** distinguished from acute stress disorder (psyche-acute-stress-disorder) by duration — the same symptom picture inside one month is acute stress disorder; from adjustment disorder (psyche-adjustment-disorder), which follows a non-traumatic stressor and lacks the intrusion/re-experiencing core.

## Sensory / Behavioral Impact
The character's threat-detection runs hot. A slammed door, a smell, a particular quality of light can pull the body into readiness before thought catches up. Attention narrows and scans — exits, hands, the space behind. Sleep is shallow and guarded. Affect can swing from numb flatness to sudden flood. A reminder can trigger a flashback in which the present briefly thins and the past overwrites it; the character may lose seconds, freeze, or react to something no one else can see.

## Duration
Chronic when untreated, but symptom *intensity* is highly state-dependent within a scene. A character can be regulated and competent one moment and ambushed the next by a trigger — a sound, a date, an anniversary, an unexpected touch. The shift can be near-instant. Grounding (a familiar voice, a physical anchor, naming the present) can pull them back; escalating threat cues drive them deeper.

## Presentation & Tells
- Scans a room on entry; sits with back to the wall, facing the door.
- Flinches or startles disproportionately; the startle outlasts the cause.
- Goes suddenly still or absent mid-conversation — the "thousand-yard stare."
- Avoids a topic, place, or person with a flatness that doesn't match the subject.
- Sleep-deprived: dark circles, short temper, mention of nightmares deflected.
- Controlled, clipped speech that frays under reminders; or numb, affectless delivery.
- Quick to anger over small things, then ashamed of the anger.

## Interiority / POV Voice
From inside, the present is never fully trusted. There is a second track always running underneath the surface one — checking, ranging, waiting for the floor to drop. The character notices what could go wrong before they notice what is going right: the angle of a stranger's approach, the weight of a hand near a pocket, the exit they'd take. Memory does not stay where it is filed. A smell arrives and the body believes it; for a breath, *then* is *now*, and they are back, and the room they are actually standing in goes paper-thin. They refuse to look directly at the event — the mind builds walls around it and patrols them. The cost of those walls is the numbness: feeling muffled, people at a distance, joy reported rather than felt. And underneath, often, the corrosive arithmetic of survival — *why me, why not them, what should I have done* — a verdict the character keeps re-litigating against themselves.

## Dramatic Affordances
This character carries a war no one else in the scene can see. They reach for control of the environment — the seat, the sightline, the exit — that reads as paranoia to others and is, to them, simple competence. Drama lives in the gap between their hyper-prepared exterior and the helplessness underneath; in the loved one who can't reach them; in the trigger no one saw coming detonating in a safe room. The quiet truth lives in the moment a flashback ends and they have to re-enter a present that watched them leave it. They make extraordinary protectors and unreliable partners; the same vigilance that keeps others alive keeps the character alone.

## Fences — Responsible Use
PTSD is not a personality flaw or a "weak" reaction — it is an injury, and people recover from it. Avoid the broken-veteran trope where trauma is only ever a ticking bomb. **Correct the violence overstatement directly:** people with PTSD are far more often harmed, withdrawn, or self-endangering than dangerous to others; the flashback-to-rampage cliché is rare and misleading. Do not render flashbacks as cinematic montage the character narrates calmly — they are disorienting and frightening. Do not stage trauma detail for shock. Real people live with this; the card renders a character, not a verdict, and not a warning label.

## Interaction Notes
Stacks heavily with depression (psyche-major-depressive-disorder) — numbing and hopelessness compound. Pairs with substance use (psyche-alcohol-use-disorder) as self-medication for sleep and arousal. With panic disorder, body-sensation triggers and panic feed each other. Where the trauma was prolonged and interpersonal, read alongside complex PTSD (psyche-complex-ptsd). Environmental condition cards that supply trigger cues — darkness, crowding, loud sudden noise, a specific weather or smell — sharply raise reactivity within a scene and can be staged as the thing that tips the character over.
