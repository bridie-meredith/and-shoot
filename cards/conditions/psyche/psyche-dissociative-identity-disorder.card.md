# Dissociative Identity Disorder — Psyche Profile

## Description
This is a psyche-profile condition card: it colors a *character's* behavior, perception, and interiority, not a location. Dissociative Identity Disorder (DID) is a trauma-rooted condition in which a person's sense of self is split across two or more distinct identity states ("parts" or "alters"), with gaps in memory that exceed ordinary forgetting. At its essence it is one mind that, under unbearable early stress, never fully integrated into a single continuous self.

## Clinical Profile
- **Taxonomy:** DSM-5-TR — Dissociative Disorders (DID, 300.14). ICD-11 — chapter on Dissociative Disorders, "Dissociative identity disorder" (6B64); the related "Partial dissociative identity disorder" (6B65) covers presentations where one identity is dominant and intrusions are subtler.
- **Core features:** disruption of identity marked by two or more distinct personality states, each with its own pattern of perceiving and relating to self and world; recurrent gaps in recall of everyday events, personal information, or trauma, beyond ordinary forgetting; the experience of being acted on (intrusive thoughts, emotions, impulses, or actions that feel "not mine"); "switching" of control between states, sometimes observable, often hidden; significant distress or functional impairment.
- **Onset & course:** roots are in early childhood (typically before age 6–9) repeated, inescapable trauma, but the disorder is usually identified far later, in the 20s–40s, often after a crisis or after years of being misdiagnosed. Chronic and fluctuating; can stabilize substantially with long-term, phase-oriented trauma therapy.
- **Prevalence:** approximately 1–1.5% of the general population in community samples — comparable to schizophrenia, and not "vanishingly rare."
- **Common comorbidities:** PTSD and complex PTSD almost universally; dissociative amnesia; depersonalization/derealization; major depression; borderline personality disorder; substance use; somatic symptom presentations. (See `psyche-ptsd`, `psyche-complex-ptsd`, `psyche-dissociative-amnesia`, `psyche-depersonalization-derealization`, `psyche-borderline-pd`.)
- **Differential:** distinguished from borderline PD (where identity instability is continuous and felt as one unstable self, without amnesic barriers between distinct states), from psychosis (DID "voices" are experienced as internal parts, not external hallucinations, and reality-testing is generally intact), from malingering, and from ordinary mood-driven changes in behavior.

## Sensory / Behavioral Impact
The character moves through a scene with a self that is not reliably continuous. Time can drop out — they arrive somewhere with no memory of the journey, find writing in their own hand they don't recall making, are told of conversations they never had. Affect can shift sharply and without external cause as one state recedes and another comes forward; energy, posture, voice, even handedness or apparent age can change. Attention may fracture under stress as internal parts press toward the front. Triggers (a smell, a tone of voice, a date) can pull the floor out from under them mid-scene.

## Duration
Chronic and lifelong as a structure, but highly variable moment to moment. Within a single scene the presenting state is often stable — until a trigger, exhaustion, threat, or a particular relationship cue prompts a switch, which can be sudden (seconds) or a slow blurring (a "co-conscious" drift where two parts share awareness). Safety, grounding, and trust tend to reduce switching; threat and shame increase it.

## Presentation & Tells
- A blink-and-stillness, a flutter of the eyes, or a brief "absence" — then a subtly or markedly different manner.
- Referring to self inconsistently — sometimes "I," sometimes "we," occasionally a different name.
- Lost time presented matter-of-factly or with embarrassment: "I don't remember Tuesday."
- Possessions, clothes, or handwriting that don't match the person in front of you, met with genuine confusion.
- Marked, context-mismatched shifts in voice register, vocabulary, posture, or apparent age.
- Headaches, fogging, or a faraway look at moments of stress (a switch building).
- Covering and apologizing — long practice at hiding the gaps to seem normal.

## Interiority / POV Voice
From inside, continuity is the thing that cannot be taken for granted. The narrating part keeps a running, quiet audit of evidence — *did I do this, did I say that, whose handwriting is this* — and learns to bluff the parts they can't reconstruct. There is an inner population: voices, pressures, presences that are unmistakably *them* and unmistakably *not me*, sometimes arguing, sometimes protecting, sometimes simply watching. The narrator refuses to look directly at certain memories; the refusal is itself a felt wall, a place the mind slides off of. Trust is expensive because being known means being found out — and being found out has historically meant danger. There is grief in it (the lost hours, the relationships managed across a gap) and there is competence in it (a self that learned, very young, to divide the unbearable into pieces small enough to survive). The texture is vigilant, apologetic, dryly resourceful, and threaded with a loneliness specific to never being sure you are only one person in the room.

## Dramatic Affordances
The condition opens scenes built on the unreliability of the self as witness: a character who cannot fully account for their own night, a confession they don't remember making, a kindness or a cruelty that came from a part they've never met. It affords protective alters who step forward in danger and the bittersweet relationship between parts learning, across a whole arc, to cooperate. It affords the slow drama of being believed — by a therapist, a partner, oneself. The quiet truth lives in the ordinary heroism of a life held together across gaps, and in the moment a character stops asking "what is wrong with me" and starts asking "what happened to me."

## Fences — Responsible Use
Real people live with DID; this card renders a character, not a verdict, and not a plot device. Refuse these myths directly:
- **DID is not a "violent split personality."** The "murderous alter" trope is fiction's invention, not the clinical reality. People with DID are far more likely to be *harmed* than to harm anyone — overwhelmingly the victims of abuse, not perpetrators of it. Do not write an alter as a monster reveal.
- **DID is not fake, faked-for-attention, or vanishingly rare.** It is a recognized, trauma-based disorder with real prevalence; treating it as a hoax or a quirky superpower is stigmatizing.
- **Ground it in trauma, not spectacle.** DID develops from repeated, inescapable childhood trauma; the parts are survival, not entertainment. Switching is not a costume change or a jump-scare.
- It is not the same as schizophrenia (a psychotic disorder) — conflating "split personality" with schizophrenia is a separate, common error to avoid.
- NO operational detail enabling harm. Render lived experience and outward presentation for characterization; never a manual.

## Interaction Notes
Stacks tightly with `psyche-ptsd` and `psyche-complex-ptsd` — the trauma layer is usually the engine beneath the dissociation, and flashbacks may belong to one part and not another. With `psyche-dissociative-amnesia` and `psyche-depersonalization-derealization` it forms a dissociative cluster; amnesic barriers and unreality can intensify around switches. With `psyche-borderline-pd` the identity disturbance compounds and the differential blurs in life as well as on the page — render both with care. Environmentally, condition cards that establish threat, confinement, sensory overload, or trauma-cued settings (specific smells, sounds, anniversaries) will raise switching pressure; cards establishing safety, predictability, and trusted presence will lower it.
