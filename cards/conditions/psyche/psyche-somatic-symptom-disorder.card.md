# Somatic Symptom Disorder — Psyche Profile

## Description
A psyche-profile condition card. It colors a *character's* behavior, perception, and interiority — not a location. Somatic Symptom Disorder (SSD) is the experience of one or more genuinely felt physical symptoms (pain, fatigue, breathlessness, gut trouble) that come bound to disproportionate thoughts, feelings, and time spent on them — the body hurts, and the hurt swallows the life around it.

## Clinical Profile
- **Taxonomy:** DSM-5-TR, *Somatic Symptom and Related Disorders* (the chapter that replaced the older "somatoform" cluster). ICD-11, *Bodily distress disorder* (chapter 6, mental/behavioural/neurodevelopmental), the closest WHO analogue.
- **Core features:**
  - One or more somatic symptoms that are distressing or disrupt daily life — the symptoms are **real and genuinely experienced**, not invented or feigned.
  - Disproportionate and persistent thoughts about the seriousness of the symptoms.
  - Persistently high anxiety about health or the body.
  - Excessive time and energy devoted to symptoms or health concerns.
  - The diagnosis turns on the *response* to symptoms, not on whether a medical explanation exists — a person with a confirmed medical illness can also have SSD if their reaction is disproportionate.
  - Typically persistent (symptoms present more often than not for 6+ months), though the specific symptom may change over time.
- **Onset & course:** Onset often in early-to-mid adulthood; symptoms can begin in adolescence. Course tends to be **chronic and fluctuating**, with stress-linked flares.
- **Prevalence:** Approximately 5–7% of the general adult population, with higher rates in primary-care and pain-clinic settings; more frequently diagnosed in women.
- **Common comorbidities:** Anxiety disorders (psyche-generalized-anxiety-disorder, psyche-panic-disorder), depression (psyche-major-depressive-disorder), and overlap with psyche-illness-anxiety-disorder and psyche-functional-neurological-disorder.
- **Differential:** Distinguished from psyche-illness-anxiety-disorder (there the *fear of having/getting* an illness dominates and somatic symptoms are minimal or absent; in SSD the symptoms themselves are front and center). Distinguished from a purely medical condition by the disproportion of the psychological response. Distinguished from factitious disorder and malingering by the **absence of any intent to deceive** — SSD symptoms are not produced for gain.

## Sensory / Behavioral Impact
The body is loud. A baseline ache that another person would file and forget arrives instead as a question the character cannot put down. Attention narrows onto interoception — heartbeat, gut, the catch in a breath — so that a portion of cognitive bandwidth is always assigned to the body's weather report. Energy runs low because vigilance is expensive. Affect skews anxious or flatly exhausted. Movement may be guarded, protective of the symptomatic region; the character braces a back, cradles an arm, sits down sooner than others would.

## Duration
Chronic and fluctuating across a life; within a single scene, usually a stable background hum that **spikes** under stress, dismissal, a new sensation, or the mention of illness. A reassuring touch or a trusted clinician's plain answer can lower it for a beat; a brusque "there's nothing wrong with you" reliably raises it.

## Presentation & Tells
- Detailed, fluent symptom narration — the character can date the onset, describe the texture, rank today against yesterday.
- A thick folder, a phone full of logged readings, a bag with remedies; the apparatus of self-monitoring travels with them.
- Repeated checking — pulse, temperature, the tender spot pressed again to confirm it still hurts.
- Reassurance that doesn't hold: relief after a clear test result lasts hours, not weeks, then the worry refills.
- Conversations that bend back toward the body; social plans shaped around how the symptom might behave.
- Visible frustration or hurt at being told it's "stress" or "all in your head" — a phrase that lands as an accusation of lying.

## Interiority / POV Voice
From the inside, the symptom is not a belief — it is a fact reporting itself, minute by minute. The chest tightens and the mind does not say *I am imagining this*; it says *this is happening, and I am the only one taking it seriously.* There is a private, exhausting arithmetic: is the ache worse on the left today, was that twinge new, did the last good day mean I'm fine or mean I'm overdue. The character is not enjoying the attention; they would trade it in a heartbeat for a body that stayed quiet. What they refuse to look at is the possibility that the looking is itself part of the engine — that vigilance feeds the very thing it's guarding against — because to believe that would mean the suffering is, in some sense, self-made, and the suffering is real, so that conclusion feels like a trap. Underneath runs a deep wish to be *believed*: to have someone, anyone, treat the body's report as true without first requiring proof.

## Dramatic Affordances
This character reaches for the doctor, the search bar, the second opinion, the person who will sit with them and not flinch. Drama lives in the gap between an unimpeachable symptom and an unimpeachable test result — the scene where every instrument says *fine* and the body says *no*. It opens conflict with partners worn thin by reassurance-giving, with clinicians who must choose between dismissal and collusion, with a self that suspects it is both the patient and the cause. The quiet truth lives in the moment someone finally says *I believe that it hurts* without adding *but* — and the character's whole posture changes, because being believed was the thing they could not give themselves.

## Fences — Responsible Use
- The symptoms are **genuinely experienced**. This is the load-bearing fence: SSD is not faking, not attention-seeking-as-lie, not "drama." Render a person in real distress, never a malingerer. The disorder is in the *disproportionate response*, not in fabricated pain.
- Refuse the lazy "it's all in their head" beat as authorial verdict. That phrase is the wound, not the diagnosis. A clinician character may say it; the narrative should not endorse it.
- Do not make the character a punchline — the hypochondriac played for laughs is a tired caricature that erases real suffering.
- Real people live with this and build full lives around it; the card renders a character, not a moral judgment. Believing-and-managing is a valid and common outcome, not a "cure" beat owed to the reader.
- No operational detail: do not write symptom-amplification techniques or health-system-gaming how-to.

## Interaction Notes
Stacks intuitively with psyche-illness-anxiety-disorder (somatic focus plus catastrophic interpretation) and psyche-generalized-anxiety-disorder (health worry as one channel of a broader worry stream). With psyche-major-depressive-disorder, somatic complaints can become the language depression speaks in. Overlaps at the border with psyche-functional-neurological-disorder when symptoms become neurological in form. With environmental condition cards — sterile clinic, crowded waiting room, a long night with no answer — the ambient state can pull symptom-vigilance up or let it briefly subside; pair a dismissive setting with this card and the flare is dramatically earned.
