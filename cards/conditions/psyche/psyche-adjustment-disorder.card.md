# Adjustment Disorder — Psyche Profile

## Description
A psyche-profile condition card. It colors a *character's* behavior, perception, and interiority — not a location. Adjustment Disorder is distress that runs ahead of its cause: a real but ordinary life stressor — a breakup, a layoff, a move, a diagnosis — knocks the character harder and longer than expected, and they can't quite get their feet back under them.

## Clinical Profile
- **Taxonomy:** DSM-5-TR — Trauma- and Stressor-Related Disorders (code 309.x / F43.2x, with subtype specifiers). ICD-11 — Disorders specifically associated with stress (6B43).
- **Core features:**
  - Emotional or behavioral symptoms within **3 months** of an identifiable stressor (which need not be traumatic).
  - Distress out of proportion to the stressor, and/or meaningful impairment in functioning.
  - Subtypes: with depressed mood; with anxiety; mixed anxiety and depressed mood; with disturbance of conduct; mixed disturbance of emotions and conduct.
  - Does NOT meet criteria for another disorder (it is, in part, a diagnosis of what something *isn't* yet).
  - Symptoms resolve within **6 months** after the stressor (or its consequences) end; persistent stressors can sustain it longer.
- **Onset & course:** any age; by definition tied to and time-bounded by the stressor. Self-limiting in most cases once the stressor resolves or the person adapts.
- **Prevalence:** common — among the most frequent diagnoses in outpatient and consultation settings; rough estimates of 5–20% in clinical populations, lower in the community.
- **Common comorbidities:** can shade toward major depression (psyche-major-depressive-disorder) or generalized anxiety (psyche-generalized-anxiety-disorder) if it deepens or persists; substance use as a coping attempt.
- **Differential:** from major depression / GAD — adjustment disorder is sub-threshold for those and explicitly stressor-tied. From acute stress disorder and PTSD (psyche-acute-stress-disorder, psyche-ptsd) — those require a *traumatic* stressor and a re-experiencing core absent here. From normal grief / expected reactions — distinguished by the disproportion and the impairment.

## Sensory / Behavioral Impact
The character is preoccupied — the stressor crowds the foreground and ordinary tasks take more effort than they should. Concentration slips; small decisions feel heavy. Mood is low or tense or both, often with a worried looping over the situation. Sleep and appetite wobble. Energy for the things they used to manage easily is gone. Nothing is catastrophically broken — they are still functional, mostly — but everything costs more, and they know they're not handling it the way they "should."

## Duration
Reactive and time-bounded — it tracks the stressor. Within a scene the distress fluctuates with proximity to the trigger: a reminder, a conversation about the situation, the place or person involved can sink the mood or spike the anxiety; distraction or good company can lift it. It is more weather than climate. When the stressor resolves or the character genuinely adapts, the disorder lifts — which is exactly why it can read, dramatically, as a character "getting through a hard time."

## Presentation & Tells
- Distracted, preoccupied; keeps circling back to the one subject.
- Tearful, irritable, or anxious in a way friends note is "not like them lately."
- Pulls back from usual activities and people; cancels, begs off.
- Concentration lapses; uncharacteristic mistakes at work or in tasks.
- Sleep and appetite changes mentioned in passing.
- Voices a sense of being overwhelmed, stuck, "unable to move on."
- Functioning enough to mask it in short bursts, then visibly flagging.

## Interiority / POV Voice
The stressor sits in the middle of the room of the mind and won't be moved around. The character keeps returning to it — replaying the conversation, the news, the loss — looking for the angle that makes it bearable and not finding one. There's a low background hum of *this shouldn't be this hard* that adds a second weight on top of the first: not just the difficulty, but shame at being undone by it. They watch other people manage similar things and can't understand why they can't. The future has gone foggy and provisional; plans they used to make easily now feel like reaching through water. It isn't despair — there are still good hours, the machinery still runs — but the character is using more fuel than the road should take, and they can feel the gauge dropping.

## Dramatic Affordances
This is the most *common* and most quietly relatable trauma-class condition — the ordinary person knocked sideways by an ordinary blow that happened to land wrong for them. The character reaches for normalcy, for "fine," for the shape of their old life while it no longer fits. Drama lives in the disproportion others can't see the reason for; in the friend who says "it's been months, you should be over this" and the character who can't explain why they're not; in the slow, undramatic work of adapting — or in the moment it deepens, the day the bottom drops and adjustment disorder quietly becomes depression. The quiet truth lives in the gap between functioning and coping.

## Fences — Responsible Use
Adjustment disorder is real distress, not "weakness" or "overreaction" — the disproportion is the diagnosis, not a moral failing. Don't write it as a character merely being dramatic, and don't let other characters' "just get over it" be the narrative's verdict. It is also genuinely *common* and usually self-limiting, so avoid both poles: neither trivializing it as nothing nor inflating it into a breakdown it isn't. People move through this constantly and come out the other side; the card renders a character having a hard, ordinary time that happens to be hitting them harder than expected — not a verdict on their resilience.

## Interaction Notes
The natural neighbor of major depression (psyche-major-depressive-disorder) and generalized anxiety (psyche-generalized-anxiety-disorder) — it can be staged as the milder, stressor-tied state that either *resolves into recovery* or *deepens into* one of them, which is dramatically useful as a turning point. Distinct from the trauma-stressor siblings (psyche-acute-stress-disorder, psyche-ptsd) by the non-traumatic stressor. Pairs with grief and life-transition circumstances. Environmental condition cards that represent or re-supply the stressor — the empty house after a loss, the new city, the workplace after the layoff — keep the disorder fed and can intensify it mid-scene; remove or resolve the stressor and the condition begins to lift.
