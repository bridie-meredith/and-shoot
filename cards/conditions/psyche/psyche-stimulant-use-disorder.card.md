# Stimulant Use Disorder — Psyche Profile

## Description
A psyche-profile condition card that colors a *character's* behavior, perception, and interiority (not a location). Stimulant Use Disorder is a chronic, relapsing pattern of ungoverned stimulant use — the character chases an accelerated, charged, often euphoric state, and the pursuit continues through escalating harm, crashes, and the wear of running the engine too hot.

## Clinical Profile
- **Taxonomy:** DSM-5-TR — Substance-Related and Addictive Disorders (Stimulant Use Disorder; covers cocaine-type and amphetamine-type stimulants), graded mild / moderate / severe. ICD-11 — Disorders due to substance use or addictive behaviours (Disorders due to use of cocaine; Disorders due to use of stimulants including amphetamines / methamphetamine).
- **Core features:**
  - Loss of control over use; persistent failed efforts to cut down.
  - Intense craving, especially cue-driven and sharp.
  - Use in binges — escalating runs followed by a depleted crash (exhaustion, flat mood, hunger, heavy sleep, low and irritable affect).
  - Continued use despite harm to health, finances, relationships, and judgment.
  - Tolerance and a diminishing return — chasing a peak that grows harder to reach.
  - At higher intensities: paranoia, agitation, and in some cases transient stimulant-induced psychosis.
- **Onset & course:** Often begins in adolescence or young adulthood; characteristically a binge-and-crash cycle rather than a steady daily level. Chronic and relapsing; recovery is achievable and common with support.
- **Prevalence:** Approximately 0.5–2% point prevalence in adults depending on substance and population; varies sharply by region and era.
- **Common comorbidities:** mood disorders (psyche-major-depressive-disorder, psyche-bipolar-i — note the manic overlap), anxiety (psyche-generalized-anxiety-disorder), other substance use (psyche-alcohol-use-disorder, psyche-opioid-use-disorder), and ADHD (psyche-adhd) in a subset.
- **Differential:** Stimulant intoxication can closely mimic a manic episode (psyche-bipolar-i) and stimulant-induced psychosis can mimic primary psychosis (psyche-schizophrenia); the substance history and time course distinguish them — symptoms tied to use and resolving with abstinence point to the substance rather than a primary mood or psychotic disorder.

## Sensory / Behavioral Impact
On the upswing the character runs fast and bright — accelerated thought and speech, confidence verging on grandiosity, appetite and need for sleep switched off, attention burning narrow and hot on whatever has it. The body is wired: pupils wide, jaw tight or grinding, restless hands, an electric forward lean. As the run extends, the brightness curdles into edginess, suspicion, and a jumpy, watchful paranoia; the world feels charged with threat or significance. The crash is the inverse: flattened, exhausted, leaden, hungry, low and irritable, the contrast itself a kind of pain that argues for going back up.

## Duration
Chronic and relapsing across a life; within a scene, defined by where the character sits on the binge-crash arc. A scene early in a run reads as charged, fast, expansive; deep in a run, as paranoid and frayed; in the crash, as depleted and bleak. The state can shift fast — the upswing fading into edginess and suspicion, or the crash deepening — across the span of a scene, with little stability to lean on.

## Presentation & Tells
- Rapid, pressured, tangential speech; jumping between threads; finishing others' sentences; talking past listeners.
- Wide pupils, tight or grinding jaw, restless hands, fidgeting, an inability to sit still; later, skin-picking or repetitive fussing.
- Hyperfocus on a task pursued for hours past sense — tearing something apart, cleaning, fixating — then abandoned.
- Going without sleep or food across a run, then crashing into long, heavy sleep and ravenous hunger.
- Suspicion sharpening into watchfulness or paranoia as a run extends — reading threat into ordinary things, glancing at exits.
- Boom-bust spending and reliability; grand plans on the upswing, collapse and absence on the crash.
- Visible depletion afterward — drawn, grey, low, irritable, contrite.

## Interiority / POV Voice
From inside the upswing, the world finally moves at the right speed — the character is fast and sharp and capable, the dull weight of ordinary life lifted, every idea luminous and connected, themselves at last who they were meant to be. The interior voice is quick, certain, expansive; it makes plans, sees patterns, trusts itself completely. That confidence is the trap: the character cannot, in the moment, tell the chemical certainty from real clarity, and decisions made on the upswing have the full force of conviction. As a run lengthens the voice frays — the brightness develops a paranoid edge, connections turn sinister, the watchfulness becomes exhausting, and a part of the character knows the wiring is hot and pushes on anyway. Then the crash, and the voice goes flat and grey and merciless: *this is what I actually am, the bright thing was borrowed, and the only way back to bearable is back up.* The cruelty of the cycle is that the low makes the high's argument for it — the contrast itself is the craving's strongest case. What the inner voice refuses to look at is the cost accruing while it runs, and that the self it loves on the upswing is the one it can't sustain.

## Dramatic Affordances
This character reaches for acceleration — to outrun a low, to feel capable, to make the world keep pace — where another would rest or sit with the feeling. The drama lives in the conviction of the upswing (and the wreckage of decisions made inside it), in the paranoid fray of a long run, in the bleakness of the crash, and in the seductive logic that pulls the character back up. Rich vein: the brilliant, frightening plan hatched at full burn; the relationship that can't trust the upswing's promises; the paranoid turn that costs the character an ally; the crash where they see clearly and despair; the recovery that has to make the flat ordinary world livable without the borrowed brightness. The quiet truth lives in why the character needs the speed — the dullness, depression, or smallness they are accelerating away from.

## Fences — Responsible Use
Real people live with this disorder; the card renders a character, not a verdict. Refuse the wild-eyed-monster caricature — the menacing "tweaker" or "crackhead" stock figure is dehumanizing and inaccurate; render the person. People with substance use disorders are far more often harmed — to their own health, safety, and dignity — than they are dangerous to others; do not lean on the violent-addict stereotype. Render stimulant-induced paranoia and psychosis with care and without spectacle, and keep it distinct from primary psychotic illness (psyche-schizophrenia). Recovery is real and common; relapse is part of the illness, not a moral verdict. Do not glamorize the upswing as pure productivity or genius, nor flatten the person to the crash. NO operational detail: no substances named to sourcing, no quantities, no methods of use — presentation and interiority only.

## Interaction Notes
Overlaps sharply with bipolar mania (psyche-bipolar-i) — the upswing can read identically, and the two can co-occur and amplify each other; the history distinguishes them. With depression (psyche-major-depressive-disorder) the stimulant becomes a way to outrun the low, and the crash deepens it, forming a punishing loop. Paired with ADHD (psyche-adhd), the line between self-medication and disorder needs careful, non-judgmental handling. Combines dangerously with depressants (psyche-alcohol-use-disorder, psyche-opioid-use-disorder), the body pulled in opposite directions at once. With environmental condition cards: high-pressure performance demands, sleeplessness, a crowd, or a paranoid-charged setting will all intensify the interior state — play them as live stressors on whichever point of the arc the character occupies.
