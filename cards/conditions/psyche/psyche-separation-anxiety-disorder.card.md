# Separation Anxiety Disorder — Psyche Profile

## Description
A psyche-profile condition card. It colors a *character's* behavior, perception, and interiority — not a location. Separation Anxiety Disorder is excessive, developmentally-inappropriate fear about being apart from the people one is most attached to — dread that something terrible will befall them or sever the bond, and intense distress at the separation itself. Long framed as a childhood condition, it is now recognized to occur in adults as well.

## Clinical Profile
- **Taxonomy:** DSM-5-TR — Anxiety Disorders (moved from the childhood-disorders chapter; now diagnosable across the lifespan). ICD-11 — Anxiety or fear-related disorders (6B05, Separation anxiety disorder).
- **Core features:**
  - Excessive distress when separation from attachment figures is anticipated or occurs.
  - Persistent worry about losing them, or about harm befalling them (illness, accident, death, "never coming back").
  - Worry that an event will cause separation (getting lost, being kidnapped, the figure leaving).
  - Reluctance or refusal to go out, be alone, or sleep away from the attachment figure.
  - Physical complaints (headaches, stomachaches, nausea) when separation looms or occurs.
  - Repeated nightmares about separation; "shadowing" the attachment figure; intolerance of being alone.
- **Onset & course:** Most common childhood anxiety disorder, often pre-adolescent. Can persist or recur in adulthood, where it may center on a partner, child, or parent. Course fluctuates with life transitions (starting school, moving, loss, a relationship change).
- **Prevalence:** Approximately 4% in children; roughly 1–2% in adults across a year, with a meaningful lifetime figure.
- **Common comorbidities:** Other anxiety disorders (GAD, panic), depression; in children, selective mutism and school refusal; in adults, panic disorder and agoraphobia.
- **Differential:** Distinguished from normal developmental attachment by severity, duration (≥4 weeks in children, typically ≥6 months in adults), and impairment. Unlike agoraphobia, the fear is tied to *who* one is apart from, not *where* one is; unlike GAD, the worry is specifically about separation/loss of the attachment figure.

## Sensory / Behavioral Impact
The character's nervous system is tuned to the location and safety of one or a few specific people. Their presence is regulating — proximity lowers the whole baseline; their absence raises it sharply. Attention tracks the attachment figure even mid-task: where are they, when will they be back, why haven't they answered. Anticipated separation produces a real-body response — a knot in the stomach, a headache, nausea, the throat going tight. Being alone is not neutral but actively distressing, a low alarm that doesn't switch off until reunion. Sleep is hard apart from the safe person, easy beside them.

## Duration
Chronic at the trait level, acutely modulated by proximity and forecast. In the safe person's presence the character can be calm and fully functional. Distress spikes at the *prospect* of parting (the goodbye at the door, the trip on the calendar) and during the separation itself, often easing only at reunion or a reassuring contact. Within a scene, a goodbye, a delayed return, an unanswered call, or a plan that requires going alone can each tip the baseline into open anxiety; reunion or contact reliably settles it.

## Presentation & Tells
- "Text me when you get there" / "call me when you land" — reassurance-seeking around the other's safety and whereabouts.
- Shadowing — staying in the same room, following the figure house to house, reluctant to let them out of sight.
- Distress, tears, stomachaches or headaches at goodbyes, drop-offs, departures.
- Reluctance to sleep alone or away from home; needing the figure present to settle.
- Catastrophic forecasting aloud — "what if something happens to you" — when the other goes.
- Frequent check-in calls/texts; rising agitation when they go unanswered.
- Resists trips, sleepovers, work travel, anything that separates; engineers ways to keep the figure close.

## Interiority / POV Voice
The fear is not of being alone in the abstract — it is of *this person* being gone, harmed, lost, not coming back, and the certainty (felt, not reasoned) that their absence and one's own safety are the same thing. When the attachment figure is near, the world is whole and quiet. When they leave, a thread pulls taut and the mind goes to work on the catastrophe: the car, the stairs, the call that doesn't come, the version of the day where they simply don't return. The narrating voice tracks them like a compass needle — half-listening to everything else, fully tuned to *where are they, are they safe, how long.* An unanswered message is not a delay; it is, for a few terrible minutes, evidence. There is a child's logic underneath even in an adult: that vigilance is what keeps them safe, that to stop watching is to let the worst in. And there is the ache of knowing the love has curdled into a leash — that wanting them close has become needing them close — and the fear that the need itself could drive them away.

## Dramatic Affordances
This is the anxiety of love at its most exposed, and it generates intense relationship drama: the goodbye that costs too much, the check-in that crosses into smothering, the partner or parent caught between reassuring and feeling held hostage. Drama lives in the necessary separation — the trip, the new school, the deployment, the grown child moving out — and what it surfaces in everyone. It can read as devotion or as suffocation depending on the witness, and a good scene plays the line. In a child, it powers school-refusal mornings and bedtime standoffs; in an adult, jealousy that isn't really jealousy, panic at a partner's silence. Arcs bend toward tolerating distance, toward trusting that the bond survives the goodbye — and, at the tender center, learning that love can let go and still be love.

## Fences — Responsible Use
Real people, children and adults, live with separation anxiety; this card renders a character, not a verdict, and not "clingy" or "needy" as a slur. The fear is genuine terror of loss, not manipulation or weakness — though it can produce controlling behavior, the driver is dread, not malice, and a scene that treats it as mere possessiveness misses the truth. Don't write the adult form as immaturity or the child form as a discipline problem to be punished out. Avoid the trope of the smothering partner played only for the other character's exasperation. Honor that the love is real even when the grip is too tight. No operational detail on harmful coping.

## Interaction Notes
With **psyche-generalized-anxiety-disorder**, the separation-specific worry sits inside broader free-floating dread. **psyche-panic-disorder** can be intertwined — separation or its prospect can trigger attacks, and the two co-occur notably in adults. The boundary with **psyche-agoraphobia** is the axis of fear: agoraphobia organizes around *places* where escape/help is hard, separation anxiety around *people* whose absence is the threat — but they can co-exist, and "won't leave home" can be driven by either. In children it overlaps with **psyche-selective-mutism** and school refusal. Against environmental condition cards, departures, travel, nightfall, distance, and unreachable communication amplify; the attachment figure's presence, a reliable line of contact, and a kept promise of return are the dampeners.
