# Delirium — Psyche Profile

## Description
This is a psyche-profile condition card. It colors a *character's* behavior, perception, and interiority — not a location. Delirium is an acute, fluctuating disturbance of attention and awareness, usually caused by an underlying medical insult (infection, withdrawal, metabolic crisis, medication, surgery), in which a person's grip on where, when, and who they are loosens over hours and slips back and forth across a single day.

## Clinical Profile
- **Taxonomy:** DSM-5-TR — Neurocognitive Disorders, "Delirium." ICD-11 — chapter 06 (Mental, behavioural and neurodevelopmental disorders), Neurocognitive disorders grouping; code 6D70 ("Delirium").
- **Core features:**
  - Acute onset (hours to days) of a disturbance in *attention* (hard to direct, focus, sustain, or shift) and *awareness* (reduced orientation to environment).
  - A characteristic **fluctuating course** — lucid one hour, disoriented the next, often worse at night ("sundowning").
  - Additional cognitive disturbance: disorientation, memory deficit, disorganized thinking, language, perceptual distortion.
  - Evidence the disturbance is a direct physiological consequence of a medical condition, substance intoxication or withdrawal, a toxin, or multiple causes.
  - Three motor presentations: **hyperactive** (agitated, restless, pulling at lines, combative), **hypoactive** (drowsy, withdrawn, slowed — most often missed), and **mixed**.
  - Frequently perceptual: illusions, misinterpretations, frank visual hallucinations (less commonly auditory).
- **Onset & course:** Acute (hours–days). Typically reversible once the underlying cause is treated, though recovery can lag the medical recovery by days to weeks. Not a trait condition — it is an *event*.
- **Prevalence:** Common in medical settings — approximately 15–25% of general medical inpatients at some point; up to ~50% post-operatively in older adults and as high as ~70–80% in the ICU. Uncommon in healthy people outside acute illness.
- **Common comorbidities:** Rides on top of vulnerability — pre-existing dementia (psyche-major-neurocognitive-disorder), brain injury (psyche-tbi-neurocognitive-disorder), alcohol or sedative withdrawal (psyche-alcohol-use-disorder, psyche-opioid-use-disorder), advanced age, frailty.
- **Differential:** Distinguished from **dementia** by its *acute onset and fluctuation* — dementia is gradual and stable day-to-day; delirium can be superimposed on it. Distinguished from a **primary psychotic disorder** by the clouding of consciousness and the medical trigger — a person in delirium is not clearly *awake*.

## Sensory / Behavioral Impact
The character's attention is a flashlight with a dying battery — it swings, dims, won't hold a beam. Time desynchronizes: minutes stretch, hours vanish, day and night invert. The room won't stay still or stay meaningful — a curtain becomes a figure, a beeping monitor becomes a voice. Speech wanders off its own track mid-sentence. Energy is either a thrashing surplus (hyperactive) or a fog they keep sinking under (hypoactive). They cannot reliably tell waking from dreaming, and they cannot lay down memory of any of it.

## Duration
Acute and **fluctuating within the scene** — this is the defining feature. A delirious character can be coherent enough to hold a hand and say a clear sentence, then twenty minutes later not know the speaker. The waveform tends to worsen toward evening and night. What shifts it mid-scene: a treated fever breaking, oxygen restored, a bladder relieved, a sedative wearing off — or the opposite, exhaustion and darkness pulling them back under. It resolves when the body's crisis resolves; it is not a permanent state.

## Presentation & Tells
- Looks "not there" — gaze unfocused, eyes drifting, slow or absent response to their name.
- Picks and plucks at the air, the bedsheet, an IV line; reaches for things that aren't there.
- Disjointed, rambling, or mumbled speech; loses the thread of a sentence; word-finding failures.
- Asks the same question minutes apart with no memory of asking it.
- Misidentifies people — calls a nurse by a dead relative's name, thinks the hospital is a hotel or their childhood home.
- Day/night reversal; restless and frightened after dark, dull and sleepy by day.
- Sudden swings between calm and agitation, or long flat withdrawal (the quiet ones get overlooked).
- Startles at shadows; tracks something in the corner of the room that no one else sees.

## Interiority / POV Voice
From inside, nothing holds still long enough to be checked. The character is sure of things in the moment and the moment keeps changing the things. The face leaning over the bed is their mother — no, it's a stranger wearing their mother — no, it's their mother again, and why won't she answer the question they're certain they just asked. They surface into a clear patch and feel briefly, almost shamefully, lucid — *I'm in a hospital, I've been confused, that was the fever* — and then a tube is a snake, the wall is breathing, and the lucid patch is gone with no seam where it tore. Cause and effect have come unstitched. They narrate with total conviction and no continuity: terror and calm trade places without a reason either could name. They cannot find the edge of the dream. Write this as a lens that *believes itself* moment to moment, that does not know it is unreliable, that keeps reaching for a coherence the brain can no longer assemble — and underneath, when a clear patch holds, a thin awful awareness of having been somewhere they can't account for.

## Dramatic Affordances
Delirium is a ticking clock with a person inside it — the underlying cause is killing or curing them while they cannot advocate for themselves. It opens the bedside vigil: a loved one watching a known person flicker in and out, granted a clear sentence and not knowing if it's the last. It is the unreliable witness at the worst possible time — a character who *saw something* but cannot be trusted to have seen it. It strips a proud or controlled character to raw need and exposes who they call for when the editor in their head goes offline (the name they say in the fog is the truth). It sets up the missed diagnosis (the quiet hypoactive patient no one noticed was crashing) and the terrible tenderness of orienting someone again and again — *you're safe, it's me, you're in the hospital* — and being forgotten between each telling.

## Fences — Responsible Use
Delirium is a **medical emergency**, not a character quirk or a convenient madness — render the underlying stakes, not "spooky confusion" for color. It is not the same as dementia or "going senile"; it is acute and usually *reversible*, and conflating the two erases that the person can come back. Do not write the delirious as comic or merely picturesque; the experience is frightening and the hypoactive form is a dangerous, easily-missed killer, not a peaceful drowse. The character is a person temporarily losing their footing inside their own brain, not a verdict on their mind or worth — the clear-patch lucidity is them, and it deserves respect. Real people survive this and remember the fear; some don't survive the cause. NO operational detail about inducing, worsening, or any harm — render the lived experience and outward presentation only.

## Interaction Notes
- **+ psyche-major-neurocognitive-disorder:** the highest-yield pairing. Delirium superimposed on dementia is extremely common and brutal — a baseline of stable confusion suddenly destabilizes, the fluctuation steepens, and family/staff may dismiss it as "just the dementia getting worse" while a treatable cause goes unfound.
- **+ psyche-tbi-neurocognitive-disorder:** a brain-injured baseline is more vulnerable to acute decompensation; post-traumatic delirium/agitation is a recognizable acute phase.
- **+ psyche-alcohol-use-disorder / withdrawal:** delirium tremens is a distinct, dangerous hyperactive delirium with autonomic storm and vivid hallucinations — render the withdrawal trigger, never any detail of inducing it.
- **+ environmental condition cards:** darkness, noise, sensory isolation, restraint, and disrupted sleep all *deepen* delirium — a windowless room, perpetual machine-light, and broken nights are not neutral set dressing here; they are accelerants the scene can pull on.
