# Bipolar I Disorder — Psyche Profile

## Description
This is a psyche-profile condition card: it colors a *character's* behavior, perception, and interiority — not a location. Bipolar I Disorder is a mood disorder defined by at least one full **manic episode** — a sustained stretch of abnormally elevated, expansive, or irritable mood with surging energy — usually alternating across a lifetime with depressive episodes. The essence in plain language: the floor and the ceiling both move, and at least once the ceiling went all the way up.

## Clinical Profile
- **Taxonomy:** DSM-5-TR — Bipolar and Related Disorders (the diagnosis turns on a manic episode; hypomanic and major depressive episodes commonly occur but are not required). ICD-11 — Mood disorders, Bipolar type I (6A60).
- **Core features:**
  - At least one **manic episode**: ≥1 week (or any duration if hospitalization is needed) of elevated/expansive/irritable mood plus persistently increased energy or goal-directed activity.
  - During mania: inflated self-esteem or grandiosity, decreased need for sleep without fatigue, pressured speech, racing thoughts/flight of ideas, distractibility, and high-risk goal-directed activity.
  - Mania impairs functioning markedly, may require hospitalization, and **can include psychotic features** (grandiose or paranoid delusions, sometimes hallucinations).
  - Major depressive episodes are typical between or after manic episodes, though not strictly required for the diagnosis.
  - Episodes can carry specifiers: with mixed features, rapid cycling, anxious distress, psychotic features, peripartum onset, seasonal pattern.
  - Inter-episode periods may be relatively well or carry residual symptoms; the disorder is lifelong but treatable.
- **Onset & course:** Typical onset in late adolescence to mid-twenties (often ~18–25). Course is **episodic and recurrent** across the lifespan, with euthymic intervals; untreated, episodes tend to recur and may quicken.
- **Prevalence:** Approximately 1% lifetime (roughly 0.6–1% across populations).
- **Common comorbidities:** Anxiety disorders, ADHD (psyche-adhd), substance use disorders, and depressive features overlapping psyche-major-depressive-disorder. Distinguished from psyche-schizoaffective when mood and psychosis intertwine.
- **Differential:** Bipolar II (psyche-bipolar-ii) never reaches *full* mania — it tops out at hypomania; the dividing line is the severity, duration, and functional rupture of the high. Cyclothymia (psyche-cyclothymia) is chronic sub-threshold swings that never meet full episode criteria. Schizoaffective (psyche-schizoaffective) involves psychosis persisting *outside* mood episodes. Unipolar depression (psyche-major-depressive-disorder) has no manic history. ADHD overlaps on distractibility and restlessness but lacks discrete, sustained mood episodes.

## Sensory / Behavioral Impact
The character's baseline is mobile, not fixed — the same person reads very differently across a manic high, a depressive low, and a stable middle.

In **mania**, time accelerates and the world arrives over-bright and over-meaningful. Sleep stops mattering — three or four hours feels generous and waking comes easy, wired rather than tired. Thought outruns speech, so words pile up and crowd each other, and the body runs hot: pacing, gesturing, starting things. Impulse collapses the gap between wanting and doing — the idea and the action are nearly the same instant.

In **depression**, everything inverts. The world goes flat and heavy, color and salience drained out. Movement costs effort that has to be budgeted; speech slows and thins; attention sinks and won't hold; the future shortens to a wall. The body feels weighted, slept-in, far away.

Between episodes the character may be steady and unremarkable, sometimes shadowed by the memory of the last high or the dread of the next swing. Affect regulation is the central fault line — not how they feel, but how far and how fast the feeling can travel.

## Duration
Episodic and recurrent. A manic episode runs at least a week; a depressive episode weeks to months. Within a single scene the mood state is usually **stable** — you render the character inside one weather system, not switching between them mid-conversation. Mid-scene shifts come from intensity, not polarity: a manic character escalating, an irritable high tipping toward confrontation, a depressed character briefly lit by something and then sinking back. Rapid cycling and mixed states blur the edges, but a clean polarity flip inside one short scene is clinically unrealistic and reads as caricature.

## Presentation & Tells
- **In mania:** rapid, pressured speech that's hard to interrupt or steer; jumping between topics on loose associations a listener can't always follow.
- Little or no sleep yet wired, not tired; up at strange hours, busy with something.
- Grand plans and unshakable certainty out of scale with reality; ideas pitched as obvious, urgent, already half-done.
- Spending sprees, risk-taking, sudden ventures, abrupt commitments; choices that look reckless from outside and self-evident from inside.
- Charm and irritability that flip fast — warm and expansive one moment, cutting and impatient the next.
- A room that feels *run* by them, the gravity bent their way whether or not they meant it.
- **In hypomania (milder highs):** sharper, funnier, more productive, more confident — magnetic, sometimes too much, but still tracking and still functional.
- **In depression:** slowed speech and movement, long pauses before answering, flattened or tearful affect, withdrawal, neglected basics — meals, hygiene, replies — a body that seems to weigh more.
- **Across states:** changed sleep as an early signal in either direction; people close to them learning to read the barometer before words confirm it.

## Interiority / POV Voice
From inside **mania**, the voice is fast, lit, and *sure*. Connections snap together everywhere — every coincidence is a sign, every plan obviously brilliant, every limit other people's small thinking. The self feels enormous and the night feels wasteful; sleep is for people who don't understand how good things are about to get. There is real joy here, and that's the trap: the high doesn't feel like illness, it feels like *finally being themselves at full size*, which is exactly why they won't stop and won't be talked down. Underneath, sometimes, a thin wire of irritability — the world is too slow, too dense, in the way.

From inside **depression**, the voice goes quiet, heavy, and certain in the opposite direction. The same mind that built empires now can't decide whether to stand up. The flatness isn't sadness so much as *absence* — color drained, future foreclosed, effort priced out of reach. They may narrate their own life from a distance, as though watching someone they used to be.

The load-bearing truth lives in the **gap between the states**, where the character has to hold both memories at once: the person who promised the impossible and the person who can't get out of bed are the same person, and they know it. The thing they refuse to look at, often, is the wreckage the high left behind — and the dread that the next one is already gathering.

## Dramatic Affordances
- The **seduction of the high**: a manic character is generative, fearless, intoxicating to be around — and the cost lands offstage or later. Drama lives in the people who love the person *and* fear the episode.
- **Insight versus the high:** the hardest, truest conflict is a character who can see the climb starting and must choose to fight a state that feels like wellness.
- **Aftermath and repair:** rebuilding trust, finances, relationships, and self-image after a manic episode — the long unglamorous arc that follows the spectacular one.
- **The depressive trough** opens the opposite scene: stillness, withdrawal, the quiet labor of staying alive and present.
- A character whose **range itself** is the engine — reaching for scale, risk, and meaning that no one else in the scene would dare, and paying for it.

## Fences — Responsible Use
Real people live with bipolar I and build full, ordinary, accomplished lives, especially with treatment. This card renders a *character*, not a verdict or a warning. Refuse the cheap portrayals: bipolar is **not** ordinary moodiness or someone "switching" within a sentence; it is **not** a synonym for "unpredictable" or "crazy"; mania is **not** glamour with no cost, nor is it merely "having a lot of energy." On violence: people with bipolar disorder are **far more often harmed — by suicide risk, exploitation, and stigma — than harmful to others**; do not write the condition as menace. Render the joy of the high honestly *and* its real consequences; render depression without romanticizing it. NO operational detail about self-harm, suicide, or substance use — depict the lived experience and outward presentation, never a method or manual.

## Interaction Notes
- With **psyche-major-depressive-disorder**: the depressive pole renders identically in-scene, but bipolar I's history of mania changes the stakes and the dread — distinguish them by arc, not by the low alone.
- With **psyche-adhd**: comorbid and easy to conflate; ADHD's restlessness is steady-state, mania's is a discrete escalating tide — stacking them, separate the chronic from the episodic.
- With substance/addictive conditions (e.g. psyche-alcohol-use-disorder): commonly comorbid, mutually worsening; substances can trigger or mask episodes.
- With anxiety conditions: mixed/anxious-distress states make a high feel agitated rather than euphoric, edging toward irritability and dread.
- With environmental condition cards: high-stimulation, sleep-disrupting, or high-stakes settings can be written as pressures that *destabilize* a euthymic character; calm, structured, well-slept environments as stabilizers.
