# Dissociative Amnesia — Psyche Profile

## Description
This is a psyche-profile condition card: it colors a *character's* behavior, perception, and interiority, not a location. Dissociative Amnesia is an inability to recall important autobiographical information — usually stressful or traumatic — that is too extensive to be explained by ordinary forgetting and is not caused by injury, substances, or a medical condition. At its essence it is the mind protecting itself by walling off what it cannot hold, leaving a person with a hole where part of their own life should be.

## Clinical Profile
- **Taxonomy:** DSM-5-TR — Dissociative Disorders (Dissociative Amnesia, 300.12), with an optional specifier for dissociative fugue (purposeful travel or bewildered wandering tied to the amnesia). ICD-11 — Dissociative Disorders, "Dissociative amnesia" (6B61), with a fugue qualifier.
- **Core features:** loss of autobiographical memory beyond normal forgetting; most often **localized** (a circumscribed period, e.g. the hours around a trauma) or **selective** (some details of an event but not others); less often **generalized** (loss of identity and whole life history); the gap is reversible in principle — the information is encoded, not destroyed; often accompanied by **fugue** (unexpected, sometimes purposeful travel away from home with amnesia for the journey or for identity); marked distress or impairment, or revealed only when the gap surfaces.
- **Onset & course:** can appear at any age, frequently after acute or chronic trauma, combat, abuse, disaster, or overwhelming loss. Onset is often sudden; duration ranges from minutes to years. Memory may return gradually, in fragments, suddenly (sometimes when safety is reached), or remain inaccessible.
- **Prevalence:** approximately 1–2% point prevalence in community samples, with higher rates in trauma-exposed populations.
- **Common comorbidities:** PTSD and acute stress disorder; depersonalization/derealization; DID (amnesia is a core feature there); depression; somatic presentations; conversion/functional symptoms. (See `psyche-ptsd`, `psyche-acute-stress-disorder`, `psyche-depersonalization-derealization`, `psyche-dissociative-identity-disorder`.)
- **Differential:** distinguished from neurological amnesia (head injury, seizure, dementia, intoxication — where new learning is typically impaired and the loss follows organic patterns), from ordinary forgetting, from age-related decline, and from malingering. In dissociative amnesia, procedural skills and the capacity to form new memories are generally intact; what's missing is autobiographical and emotionally charged.

## Sensory / Behavioral Impact
The character carries a blank where a stretch of life should be — and most of the time does not feel it as a wound, because the mind closes neatly over the gap. They function: they can drive, work, hold a conversation. The impact shows at the seams — when asked about the missing time, when a detail almost surfaces and slides away, when the absence becomes consequential (a face they should know, a promise they don't remember making). In fugue, behavior can look eerily ordinary from outside while the person operates with no access to who they are. Cues tied to the walled-off material can produce sudden dread, fogging, or flight without the person knowing why.

## Duration
Highly variable. A localized gap may be permanent and stable, sitting quietly in a life. A fugue may last hours or days and resolve abruptly. Within a scene the amnesia is usually stable — the character simply does not have the memory — but pressure on the gap (interrogation, a returning cue, a safe-enough moment) can crack it, and recovered fragments can arrive with overwhelming force.

## Presentation & Tells
- Vague, deflecting, or genuinely puzzled when a specific period comes up: "I just... don't remember that part."
- A flat or strangely calm relationship to a clearly significant gap (la belle indifférence — the absence noted without the expected alarm).
- Inconsistencies others catch before the character does — a misremembered timeline, an unaccounted day.
- Sudden anxiety, nausea, or a need to leave when something nears the walled-off material, with no stated reason.
- In fugue: turning up somewhere with no idea how, possibly under a different sense of self, distress on "coming to."
- Fragments breaking through — an image, a smell-memory, a phrase — that don't connect to anything the character can place.

## Interiority / POV Voice
From inside, the gap is most striking for how *un*-felt it usually is: the mind does not present a missing piece as a missing piece; it presents a smooth edge, a place where curiosity simply doesn't go. The narrator can describe the day before and the day after and feel no seam between them, and only the testimony of others — a receipt, a wound, a stranger who knows them — tells them something is gone. When the material does press, it presses as physical wrongness before it is ever a thought: a cold drop in the stomach, a swimming room, an urge to be elsewhere. There is a peculiar doubling of trust and dread toward one's own mind — gratitude for the wall and fear of what it's holding. The narrator manages the gap with quiet workarounds, learns not to push, and lives with the unsettling knowledge that the most important thing that ever happened to them might be the one thing they cannot reach.

## Dramatic Affordances
The condition is a mystery engine pointed inward: the protagonist is the case. It affords the slow, dangerous work of recovering what was hidden, and the question of whether recovery is mercy or harm. It affords fugue plots — a character found far from home as someone else — and the reunion drama of a returning identity. The richest material is the moral one: who has the right to give a memory back, and what is owed to the self who chose, however involuntarily, not to know. The quiet truth lives in a person walking carefully around a hole in their own history, and in the costliness of the protection that put it there.

## Fences — Responsible Use
Real people live with dissociative amnesia; this card renders a character, not a verdict or a gimmick. Refuse these distortions:
- **It is not convenient plot-amnesia.** The soap-opera "bonk on the head, forgets everything, kiss restores it" trope misrepresents a trauma response as a tidy device. Recovery is rarely instant, total, or painless, and is sometimes destabilizing.
- **It is not faking or lying.** The gap is real and involuntary; treating every amnesiac character as a manipulator is a stigmatizing default.
- **Distinguish it from neurological memory loss** — they are different mechanisms and conflating them muddies both.
- **Recovered-memory caution:** memory is reconstructive and suggestible; render the ambiguity honestly rather than as guaranteed buried-truth-unlocked. Avoid implying coercive "recovery" techniques are reliable or safe.
- NO operational detail; render the lived experience and outward presentation, never a how-to.

## Interaction Notes
Stacks closely with `psyche-ptsd` and `psyche-acute-stress-disorder` — the amnesia often walls off the very event driving the trauma response, so flashbacks and the gap can coexist around the same material. With `psyche-depersonalization-derealization` it forms part of the dissociative spectrum; unreality and missing time may arrive together under stress. It is a defining feature within `psyche-dissociative-identity-disorder`, where the amnesia falls between identity states rather than around a single event. Environmentally, condition cards establishing trauma cues, threat, or interrogation pressure will strain the gap and risk sudden recovery; cards establishing safety and stability make recovered material easier to bear when it comes.
