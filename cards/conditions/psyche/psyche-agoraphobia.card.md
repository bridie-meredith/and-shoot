# Agoraphobia — Psyche Profile

## Description
A psyche-profile condition card. It colors a *character's* behavior, perception, and interiority — not a location. Agoraphobia is intense fear of situations where escape might be difficult or help unavailable if something goes wrong — crowds, open spaces, public transit, lines, being far from home or alone — leading to avoidance that can shrink a character's world to a few safe places, or to the home itself.

## Clinical Profile
- **Taxonomy:** DSM-5-TR — Anxiety Disorders (a standalone diagnosis, no longer requiring panic disorder). ICD-11 — Anxiety or fear-related disorders (6B02, Agoraphobia).
- **Core features:**
  - Marked fear/anxiety about two or more of: public transit, open spaces, enclosed spaces, lines or crowds, being outside the home alone.
  - The fear is of panic-like or other incapacitating/embarrassing symptoms occurring where escape is hard or help unavailable.
  - The situations are avoided, require a companion, or are endured with intense distress.
  - "Safe person" and "safe place" become load-bearing — many can do feared things only with a trusted companion or within a known radius of home.
  - Avoidance can be severe and progressive — in the worst cases the person becomes housebound.
  - Anticipatory anxiety often exceeds the feared event itself.
- **Onset & course:** Typically late teens to mid-thirties; can follow a panic attack or a frightening public episode. Tends to be chronic and can worsen as avoidance reinforces itself; treatable with graded exposure.
- **Prevalence:** Approximately 1–2% over a 12-month period; more common in women.
- **Common comorbidities:** Panic disorder (the classic pairing), other anxiety disorders, depression (driven by isolation), specific phobia.
- **Differential:** Distinguished from specific phobia (the fear is of being *trapped/helpless*, not of a particular object), from social anxiety (the fear isn't of judgment but of incapacitation/escape), and from a realistic safety concern by its disproportion and the avoidance pattern.

## Sensory / Behavioral Impact
The character experiences the world as a map graded by escapability and proximity to safety. Distance from home pulls like a tether under tension — the farther out, the higher the hum. Open plazas, packed trains, the middle of a long queue, a bridge with no median: each reads not as scenery but as a trap with the exits measured. Attention runs a constant cost-of-leaving calculation. The body carries the low charge of someone braced to need an exit they might not have. The world doesn't shrink because they want it to; it shrinks because each avoided place teaches the nervous system the avoidance worked, and the safe radius quietly contracts.

## Duration
Chronic and progressive without intervention, but spatially modulated moment to moment. Within the safe place — home, the known radius, beside the safe person — the character can be calm and fully themselves. Anxiety rises with each step out: distance from safety, density of the crowd, difficulty of exit, absence of a companion. A scene can be entirely comfortable indoors and tip into dread the instant the plan requires the open square or the long ride. Anticipatory dread can begin hours before a forecast outing.

## Presentation & Tells
- Always wants a companion for anything beyond the safe radius; "can you come with me?"
- Sits or stands near exits; on arrival, locates the doors, the route out, the aisle seat.
- Declines or cancels outings that go far, run long, or can't be left easily; offers practical-sounding excuses.
- Keeps a tight orbit — the same shops, the same streets, the same chair — and resists straying from it.
- Visibly more at ease the closer to home; tension rising with distance, palpable on the way out, easing on the return.
- Carries safety props — phone charged and close, water, medication, a way to call someone.
- May not leave home at all in severe periods; arranges life so the world comes to them.

## Interiority / POV Voice
Home is not a preference; it is the one place the body stops bracing. Everywhere else, the narrating mind runs the same arithmetic — *how do I get out of here, how fast, who would help me, how far is back* — and the answer at the center of an open square or the middle of a stalled train is *I couldn't,* and that single word is the whole fear. It isn't the crowd or the space that terrifies; it's the imagined moment of going wrong *there,* in front of everyone, with no exit and no one — the helplessness, not the place. So the world narrows, and the narrowing is dressed up in reasons (it's far, it's not worth it, I'm tired) that the honest voice underneath knows are translations of *I can't.* There is grief threaded through it: a sharp awareness of the life happening out past the safe radius, the people who simply go, and a self that remembers going too, before the map started shrinking. And there is the particular tenderness toward the safe person — the one whose presence turns a trap back into a street — and the shame of needing them that much.

## Dramatic Affordances
The world made small is inherently dramatic — the visible cost of a life lived inside a contracting circle, and the question of what could be worth stepping past its edge. Drama lives in the threshold: the door, the platform, the open square, and the character standing at the line they cannot cross — or crossing it, and what it takes. The safe person is a rich relationship engine: indispensable, treasured, and sometimes quietly resented on both sides. Conflict comes when life demands the far place — an emergency, a funeral, a chance that won't wait. Arcs bend toward widening the radius one hard step at a time, the un-glamorous heroism of the bus stop and the corner shop, and the terror and exhilaration of reclaiming ground.

## Fences — Responsible Use
Real people live with agoraphobia; this card renders a character, not a verdict, and certainly not "lazy" or "antisocial." The housebound character is not weak or self-indulgent — they are trapped by a nervous system that has learned safety too narrowly, and they often grieve the world they can't reach. Avoid the recluse-as-quirk or the "just push them out the door" cure; forced exposure without consent is harmful. Don't reduce it to agoraphobia-means-never-leaves-the-house — severity ranges widely. Honor the safe person as a real relationship, not a crutch joke. No operational detail on harmful coping. Render the longing outward, not just the staying in.

## Interaction Notes
The signature pairing is with **psyche-panic-disorder** — agoraphobia very often grows from a panic attack in public, where the lesson "it could happen again, somewhere I can't escape" generalizes into avoidance of all such places. With **psyche-generalized-anxiety-disorder**, the diffuse worry feeds the escapability calculus. Distinguish from **psyche-specific-phobia**: a fear of the elevator-as-object is phobia; a fear of being trapped where help can't reach across many settings is agoraphobia. Comorbid **psyche-major-depressive-disorder** is common and often a consequence — the isolation breeds it, and the depression saps the motivation graded exposure requires. Against environmental condition cards, open expanses, dense crowds, public transit, long lines, and any setting far from home or hard to leave are powerful amplifiers; the safe place, the safe person, a clear and close exit, and a short known route are the dampeners.
