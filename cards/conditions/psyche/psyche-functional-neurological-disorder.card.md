# Functional Neurological Symptom Disorder (Conversion Disorder) — Psyche Profile

## Description
A psyche-profile condition card. It colors a *character's* behavior, perception, and interiority — not a location. Functional Neurological Symptom Disorder (FND), historically called conversion disorder, is a genuine disturbance of movement, sensation, or function — weakness, tremor, non-epileptic seizures, blindness, loss of speech — that arises from a problem in how the nervous system *runs* its signals rather than from structural damage to it. The symptoms are real, involuntary, and not under the person's control.

## Clinical Profile
- **Taxonomy:** DSM-5-TR, *Somatic Symptom and Related Disorders* — *Functional Neurological Symptom Disorder (Conversion Disorder)*. ICD-11, *Dissociative neurological symptom disorder* (6B60), under dissociative disorders, reflecting the dissociative thread in much of the presentation.
- **Core features:**
  - One or more symptoms of altered voluntary motor or sensory function — limb weakness/paralysis, tremor, dystonia, gait disturbance, functional (non-epileptic) seizures, sensory loss, blindness, deafness, aphonia/loss of speech, swallowing trouble.
  - Clinical findings show **internal inconsistency** — the symptom doesn't match how a structural neurological disease would behave (a classic example: a leg that gives way standing but moves normally on a test that distracts attention). This positive feature, not a "rule everything else out" by exclusion, is how it's diagnosed.
  - The symptoms are **genuinely experienced and involuntary** — the person is not producing them on purpose.
  - Significant distress or impairment results.
  - A preceding psychological stressor is common but **not required** for the diagnosis.
- **Onset & course:** Onset across the lifespan, often young-to-middle adulthood; can be sudden, sometimes following injury, illness, panic, or stress. Course ranges from **brief and self-limiting** to **chronic and disabling**; early, validating treatment improves outcome.
- **Prevalence:** A leading reason for neurology referral; functional symptoms account for an estimated 10–30% of neurology outpatient presentations. Discrete FND incidence is roughly 4–12 per 100,000 per year. More frequently diagnosed in women.
- **Common comorbidities:** psyche-ptsd and trauma histories, psyche-depersonalization-derealization and other dissociative experience, anxiety and psyche-panic-disorder, psyche-major-depressive-disorder, and overlap with psyche-somatic-symptom-disorder.
- **Differential:** Distinguished from structural neurological disease by **positive inconsistency signs**, not by absence of findings — FND can co-exist with genuine neurological illness. Distinguished from factitious disorder and malingering by the **absence of intentional production or external gain** — FND symptoms are involuntary. Distinguished from psyche-somatic-symptom-disorder by the neurological *form* of the symptom (FND is loss/alteration of function; SSD is distressing symptoms like pain or fatigue).

## Sensory / Behavioral Impact
A part of the body stops obeying. The character wills the leg forward and nothing comes, or the hand starts to shake and will not be stilled, or words form in the mind and die before the throat. The experience is one of **disconnection** — the limb is theirs and not theirs, present and unreachable. During functional seizures consciousness may cloud or go. Around the symptom, attention and energy collapse inward; the effort of managing a body that has become unreliable is enormous. Many describe a fog, a sense of watching themselves from a half-step away (a dissociative texture), and exhaustion after an episode.

## Duration
Highly variable: a single dramatic episode that resolves, or a fluctuating chronic course. Within a scene the symptom can **shift suddenly** — a tremor that quiets when the character is absorbed in something else and returns the instant attention lands on it; a weakness that worsens under scrutiny, fear, or the pressure of being watched, and eases in safety or distraction. This attention-dependence is intrinsic to FND, not evidence of faking.

## Presentation & Tells
- A limb that drags, gives way, or won't lift; a hand or head that trembles, sometimes entrainable to a rhythm and not to another.
- Functional (non-epileptic) seizures: shaking, collapse, or unresponsiveness that doesn't follow an epileptic pattern — often with closed, resisting eyes and a gradual, exhausted recovery.
- Sudden loss of speech, voice dropping to a whisper, or apparent loss of sight or sensation in a way that doesn't map cleanly onto nerve territory.
- The symptom visibly easing when the character is distracted and intensifying when observed or asked to demonstrate it.
- Bafflement and fear rather than indifference — though a flatter "la belle indifférence" can appear, it is neither reliable nor required.
- A history of bouncing between specialists, of being told "the scans are normal," and of hearing that as *they think I'm making it up.*

## Interiority / POV Voice
From the inside, the body has committed a betrayal that the mind cannot explain or undo. The character orders the leg to move with everything they have and watches it refuse, and the horror is precisely that there is no *I'm choosing this* to grab onto — the will is intact and the bridge from will to limb has gone out. There is often a dissociative drift, a sense of being slightly behind glass, the world and one's own hand a beat removed. Worse than the symptom is the suspicion in other people's eyes: the character can feel the question *are you really?* and it lands as an accusation against something they would give anything to control. They may not be able to point to a cause — the stressor, if there is one, can be invisible to them — which makes the standard explanation ("it's stress, it's psychological") feel both true and unbearable, because it sounds like *you did this to yourself* when the lived fact is *this happened to me.* What they reach for, underneath, is one clinician who will say *this is real, it has a name, and it can get better.*

## Dramatic Affordances
This character reaches for an explanation that holds, for a body they can trust again, for someone to witness the symptom without doubting it. Drama lives in the corridor of normal scans — the scene where every test is clean and the leg still won't walk, and the character must survive being disbelieved by the very people meant to help. It opens conflict with a medical world built to find structural disease and unsure what to do with a working machine that won't run; with loved ones torn between support and the gnawing doubt the culture has handed them; with the self, ordering a limb and meeting silence. The quiet truth lives in the turn from disbelief to recognition — the moment "the scans are normal, so nothing's wrong" becomes "the scans are normal *because* this isn't damage — it's the running, and the running can be retrained" — and the character, for the first time, is both believed and offered a way back.

## Fences — Responsible Use
- The symptoms are **real and involuntary**. This is the load-bearing fence. FND is not faking, not "hysteria," not attention-seeking, not a character "putting it on." Never write a reveal where it turns out they were pretending.
- Retire the slur-adjacent framing. "Hysterical," "psychosomatic" used as dismissal, and the played-for-doubt collapse are exactly the stigma that has harmed real patients for over a century.
- The attention-dependence (symptom eases with distraction, worsens under observation) is a **diagnostic feature**, not a gotcha — do not stage it as the moment a skeptic "catches them out."
- A psychological stressor is common but not required; do not force a tidy buried-trauma cause as if FND were a puzzle with a key. Sometimes there is no clean why.
- Real people live with this and many recover, especially with validation and the right therapy; the card renders a character, not a verdict. Depict recovery as plausible, not as proof they were faking.
- No operational detail: no symptom-production or diagnosis-gaming how-to of any kind.

## Interaction Notes
Stacks closely with psyche-depersonalization-derealization and the dissociative register generally — the behind-glass texture is shared, and ICD-11 files FND among dissociative disorders. With psyche-ptsd a functional symptom can be the body carrying what the narrative can't, especially around the activation of a trauma memory. With psyche-panic-disorder, a panic surge can tip into a functional seizure or transient weakness. Borders psyche-somatic-symptom-disorder when pain and functional loss coexist. With environmental condition cards — a clinical exam room with eyes on the character, a sudden alarm, a crowded space — observation and threat can drive the symptom up, while a calm, trusted, low-scrutiny setting can let it ease; the attention-sensitivity makes setting a direct lever on presentation.
