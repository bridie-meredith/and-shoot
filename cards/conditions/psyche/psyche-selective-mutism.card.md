# Selective Mutism — Psyche Profile

## Description
A psyche-profile condition card. It colors a *character's* behavior, perception, and interiority — not a location. Selective Mutism is a consistent failure to speak in specific social situations where speech is expected — typically school or with strangers — despite speaking normally in others, usually at home with close family. It is an anxiety condition, not defiance and not an inability to talk: the voice is there and cannot get out.

## Clinical Profile
- **Taxonomy:** DSM-5-TR — Anxiety Disorders (reclassified from the childhood-disorders chapter). ICD-11 — Anxiety or fear-related disorders (6B06, Selective mutism).
- **Core features:**
  - Consistent failure to speak in specific situations (e.g. school, public, with unfamiliar people) despite speaking in others.
  - The child speaks normally — often freely and fluently — in safe settings, usually home with immediate family.
  - The mutism interferes with school/social functioning and isn't explained by lack of language knowledge or a communication/speech disorder.
  - Duration of at least one month (not limited to the first month of school).
  - Strongly associated with social anxiety; widely understood as a fear-driven freeze, not willful silence.
  - Children may communicate nonverbally (nodding, pointing, gestures, writing) while unable to vocalize.
- **Onset & course:** Onset usually before age five, often becoming visible at school entry when the demand to speak with non-family arrives. Frequently improves with treatment; without it, can persist and shade into adult social anxiety. Relatively rare.
- **Prevalence:** Approximately 0.5–1% of children; uncommon, and frequently under-recognized.
- **Common comorbidities:** Social anxiety disorder (very high overlap), separation anxiety, other anxiety disorders; sometimes language/developmental differences that compound the anxiety.
- **Differential:** Distinguished from oppositional/defiant silence (here it's fear, not refusal), from autism-related communication differences (the child speaks fully in safe settings), from a primary speech/language disorder, and from transient first-weeks-of-school quiet.

## Sensory / Behavioral Impact
The character carries two different bodies into two different rooms. At home, in safety, speech flows — chatter, jokes, full sentences, an ordinary voice. Cross the threshold into a feared setting and the throat closes; the impulse to speak meets a physical wall. It is not that they choose not to talk — it is that the words arrive and the body will not release them, like a hand that won't open. In the feared setting they often go still and watchful, communicating in nods, points, and a fixed neutral face, exquisitely aware of every eye and every expectation that they speak. The pressure to talk makes it worse, not better; a kind adult waiting for an answer can lock the silence tighter.

## Duration
Situation-bound and consistent rather than episodic — reliably present in feared settings, reliably absent in safe ones. Within a scene, the determining variable is *who is present and where*: the same child fluent at the kitchen table goes mute when a stranger enters or the setting shifts to school. It can ease as a setting becomes deeply familiar and trust builds over time, and it can be momentarily breached (a whisper to a parent, a word behind a hand), but direct demand to "use your words" typically deepens the freeze.

## Presentation & Tells
- Speaks freely at home, falls silent at school or with strangers — the split is the diagnostic signature.
- Communicates nonverbally in feared settings: nodding, head-shaking, pointing, pulling a parent's sleeve, writing or drawing answers.
- Frozen, watchful stillness when addressed; a blank or anxious face, eyes down or fixed.
- Whispers to a trusted person but not to the questioner; will route speech *through* a safe person ("ask Mum").
- Visible distress, freezing, or shutdown when directly pressed to speak; may go more silent under pressure.
- Hovers at the edge of group activity; participates physically but not vocally.
- May mouth words, or speak in a near-inaudible whisper only to the one safe person present.

## Interiority / POV Voice
Inside, the words are *right there.* That is the strangeness of it from within — the answer is formed, the sentence is ready, and between the thought and the mouth there is a closed door that will not open no matter how hard it is pushed. It isn't sulking and it isn't a decision; it's a freeze, the body's oldest no. Every face turned toward them, waiting, makes the door heavier. They can feel the kind teacher's patience as pressure, the question hanging in the air growing larger the longer it goes unanswered, and the awful arithmetic of the moment — *if I speak, they'll all hear my voice and look at me, and if I don't, the silence gets worse* — both branches unbearable, so the body picks neither and simply locks. There is relief in the nonverbal: a nod is a release valve. And there is the strange double life — knowing they are funny and loud and themselves at home, and watching the people at school meet only the silent version and decide that's who they are. They are not absent in there. They are full of words. The words just can't get out.

## Dramatic Affordances
The locked voice is a vivid, sympathetic engine — a character whose interior is rich and articulate and whose surface is silence, which lets a story carry the reader inside a person no one in the scene can hear. Drama lives in the gap between the two selves and in the moments at the threshold: the well-meaning adult applying exactly the wrong pressure, the patient one who learns to wait without demanding, the single trusted person speech can route through. The first word in a feared setting is an enormous, earned beat. Conflict comes from being misread as rude, slow, or stubborn when the truth is fear. Arcs bend toward widening the circle of safe people and safe rooms one small breach at a time — and the tender truth that being *heard at last,* by someone who waited the right way, can open the door no demand could.

## Fences — Responsible Use
Real children (and some adults who carried it forward) live with selective mutism; this card renders a character, not a verdict. It is not defiance, not stubbornness, not "won't" — it is "can't," a fear response, and writing it as a discipline problem or a manipulation gets the condition exactly backwards. Do not stage the cure as forcing, bribing, or shaming a child into speaking; demand and pressure deepen the freeze and can cause real harm. Avoid the trope of the "creepy silent kid" — the inner life is ordinary, warm, and full. Honor the at-home fluency: this is a child with a normal voice trapped behind anxiety, not a child who lacks one. No operational detail on coercive "treatment."

## Interaction Notes
Selective mutism overlaps so heavily with **psyche-social-anxiety-disorder** that many clinicians view it as an early, severe presentation on the same spectrum — the social-evaluative fear so intense that speech itself shuts down; a child with selective mutism very often grows toward, or alongside, social anxiety. With **psyche-separation-anxiety-disorder**, the two co-occur in children, and the safe person whose presence the separation-anxious child needs is frequently the same person the mute child can route a whisper through. **psyche-generalized-anxiety-disorder** can sit beneath as broader worry. Against environmental condition cards, unfamiliar settings, the presence of strangers or authority figures, group attention, and any direct demand to speak are the amplifiers; the home setting, the presence of a single trusted person, a nonverbal channel offered without pressure, and patient time are the dampeners.
