# Persistent Depressive Disorder (Dysthymia) — Psyche Profile

## Description
A psyche-profile condition card. It colors a *character's* behavior, perception, and interiority — not a location. Persistent Depressive Disorder (PDD; formerly dysthymia) is a chronic, low-grade depression that runs for years rather than weeks. Its one-line essence: not a storm but a permanently overcast sky — a baseline of muted mood the character has often stopped recognizing as anything but "how I am."

## Clinical Profile
- **Taxonomy:** DSM-5-TR — Depressive Disorders (Persistent Depressive Disorder, which consolidates older dysthymia and chronic major depression). ICD-11 — Mood Disorders, Dysthymic disorder (6A72).
- **Core features:**
  - Depressed mood most of the day, more days than not, for at least two years (one year in children/adolescents).
  - Presence of two or more of: poor appetite or overeating; insomnia or hypersomnia; low energy/fatigue; low self-esteem; poor concentration or indecisiveness; feelings of hopelessness.
  - Symptoms are never absent for more than two months at a stretch across that span.
  - Lower in acute intensity than a full major episode but far longer in duration — the chronicity *is* the diagnosis.
  - Function is often preserved on the surface; the person works, shows up, carries on — while internally running on empty.
- **Onset & course:** Frequently early and insidious — childhood, teens, or early adulthood — so the person may have no memory of a non-depressed baseline. Course is chronic and persistent, with episodes of full MDD layering on top ("double depression").
- **Prevalence:** Approximately 1.5–3% point/annual prevalence; lifetime figures somewhat higher. More common in women.
- **Common comorbidities:** Superimposed psyche-major-depressive-disorder episodes; anxiety disorders (psyche-generalized-anxiety-disorder); avoidant personality traits (psyche-avoidant-pd) where the chronic low self-worth has shaped the personality.
- **Differential:** Distinguished from psyche-major-depressive-disorder by chronicity over intensity — PDD is the long grey baseline, MDD the discrete episode. Distinguished from a stable personality style by the presence of a true mood disturbance. Distinguished from "just a pessimistic person" by impairment and the two-year-plus duration criterion.

## Sensory / Behavioral Impact
The character operates at a steady, slightly-down setting that never quite resolves. Pleasure is dimmed rather than absent — they can enjoy things, just less, and with a built-in expectation of disappointment. Energy is chronically low-grade; they have learned to function *through* fatigue rather than against acute exhaustion. Because the state is so long-running, it has fused with identity: pessimism reads as realism, joylessness as maturity, low expectations as wisdom. The behavioral signal is muted and durable — not collapse, but a person running perpetually at 70%, who has organized a whole life around that ceiling without noticing the ceiling is there.

## Duration
Chronic and progressive-by-entrenchment. Stable within a scene and across scenes; this is the slowest-moving of the depressive profiles. It does not "lift" in a beat. What can shift mid-scene is a brief, surprised moment of genuine pleasure or connection that the character half-distrusts — and the contrasting darkening when a superimposed MDD episode begins to bleed in, deepening the grey toward true black.

## Presentation & Tells
- A consistent low-key flatness — rarely euphoric, rarely devastated; the affective range is compressed at the dim end.
- Dry, deprecating humor; gallows wit deployed reflexively, often funny, always slightly true.
- Reflexive minimizing — "it's fine," "could be worse," "I'm used to it" — as the default reading of their own life.
- Chronically low expectations stated as settled fact; surprise (often suspicion) when something goes well.
- Reliable, functional, present — and visibly tired in a way that never fully resolves with rest.
- Old, well-worn coping routines: the same comfort show, the same walk, the same narrow set of safe activities.
- A long absence of enthusiasm that the character and those around them have normalized.

## Interiority / POV Voice
From inside, PDD does not feel like an illness — it feels like *the truth about life that other people are naive enough not to have noticed*. The inner voice is dry, even-toned, and quietly hopeless; it is rarely loud because it has never needed to be. It runs a low constant murmur of "this is as good as it gets," "don't get your hopes up," "of course it went wrong." Self-worth is set permanently low, not in a spasm of self-loathing but as background fact — the character genuinely does not expect to be wanted, to succeed, to feel great, and has built a life that confirms this. The defining texture is *adaptation*: they have folded the depression so thoroughly into their identity that they would have trouble describing who they'd be without it. Good moments are held at arm's length — enjoyed, but immediately filed as exceptions, because hoping has historically cost too much. What the voice refuses to look at directly is the possibility that this isn't just realism — that the grey is a *symptom*, that other people genuinely feel more, that they have been quietly suffering for years and calling it personality. That refusal is the load-bearing center of the POV: the character is the last person in the room to suspect they are depressed.

## Dramatic Affordances
This character reaches for the safe, the modest, the pre-disappointed option — where everyone else reaches for the thing they actually want. The drama lives in the slow erosion of a life lived under a low ceiling: the relationship kept at a manageable temperature, the ambition quietly abandoned years ago, the wit that deflects every offer of more. The most powerful scenes are recognition scenes — the moment someone (a new partner, a therapist, a blunt friend) names the grey as not-normal, and the character has to decide whether to believe that more was ever possible. The quiet truth lives in the brief unguarded moments of real pleasure the character can't explain away fast enough. Their arc is rarely a breakdown; it's a slow, frightening expansion of expectation — which can be its own kind of high-stakes, because hoping is the most dangerous thing this character can do.

## Fences — Responsible Use
- Real people live with this, often undiagnosed for decades, holding down jobs and relationships the whole time. The card renders a character, not a verdict — chronic low mood is not pessimism-as-character-flaw or a failure to "choose happiness."
- Do not flatten PDD into "a grumpy person" or "an Eeyore type." The wit and the function are real; so is the underlying disorder. Both must be honored.
- Refuse the "they just need a good reason to be happy" cure-by-event. The chronicity does not yield to a single fix; growth is slow and effortful.
- Do not over-dramatize — PDD is defined by its quietness and duration. The temptation to make it visible by spiking it into crisis misrepresents the condition; if it spikes, that is a superimposed MDD episode, render it as such.
- NO operational or method detail regarding self-harm or suicide. Render the felt experience — the grey, the low ceiling, the distrust of hope — never instruction. Route hopelessness toward recognition and care.

## Interaction Notes
- With psyche-major-depressive-disorder superimposed ("double depression"), the character's chronic grey deepens into a discrete black episode; recovery returns them only to grey, which to an outside observer can look like a cure but to the character is just "back to normal."
- With psyche-avoidant-pd, the long-run low self-worth has hardened into a personality structure — the character pre-rejects opportunities and intimacy to avoid the anticipated failure, and the two states reinforce each other.
- With psyche-generalized-anxiety-disorder, the flat hopelessness pairs with low-grade chronic worry — a character who is both unhopeful and uneasy, expecting things to go wrong and finding no relief when they don't.
- With an environmental condition card (long winter, isolation, a stalled life-circumstance), the ambient state confirms the baseline rather than creating it — render the condition as one more piece of evidence the character files under "of course," not as the origin of the mood.
