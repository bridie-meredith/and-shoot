# Factitious Disorder (incl. Imposed on Another / Munchausen) — Psyche Profile

## Description
A psyche-profile condition card. It colors a *character's* behavior, perception, and interiority — not a location. Factitious Disorder is the falsification or induction of illness in oneself (or, in the *imposed on another* form, in a person under one's care) in the **absence of any obvious external reward** — the motive is internal, bound up with the role of being sick or being a devoted caretaker of the sick, not money, drugs, or escape from duty. This is the disorder's defining and most painful paradox: deliberate deception driven by a need the person often cannot name.

## Clinical Profile
- **Taxonomy:** DSM-5-TR, *Somatic Symptom and Related Disorders* — *Factitious Disorder*, with two specifiers: *imposed on self* and *imposed on another* (formerly "Munchausen syndrome" and "Munchausen syndrome by proxy"). ICD-11, *Factitious disorders* (6D50, imposed on self; 6D51, imposed on another).
- **Core features:**
  - Falsification of physical or psychological signs or symptoms, or induction of injury or disease, associated with identified deception.
  - The person presents themselves (or another) as ill, impaired, or injured.
  - The behavior is evident **even without obvious external rewards** — this is what separates it from malingering.
  - In the *imposed on another* form, the perpetrator presents the **victim** as ill; the diagnosis is given to the perpetrator, not the victim, and the conduct is a recognized form of **abuse**.
  - The deception is conscious, but the underlying motivation is typically not — the "why" is murky to the person themselves.
- **Onset & course:** Often emerges in early adulthood, frequently after a real illness or hospitalization (one's own or a loved one's). Course can be **episodic or chronic**; some courses are severe, with repeated presentations across many facilities.
- **Prevalence:** Genuinely uncertain and likely underestimated because of the concealment involved — roughly ~1% of hospital patients in some estimates; the imposed-on-another form is rarer and harder to count. Treat any figure as approximate.
- **Common comorbidities:** Personality difficulties (notably psyche-borderline-pd; in the imposed-on-another form, traits overlapping psyche-antisocial-pd), histories of early trauma or psyche-ptsd, depression (psyche-major-depressive-disorder), and a border with psyche-somatic-symptom-disorder (where, by contrast, no deception is present).
- **Differential:** Distinguished from **malingering** by motive — malingering is feigning for a clear external payoff (money, leave, evading consequence) and is *not* a mental disorder; factitious disorder is feigning where the reward is the sick/caretaker role itself. Distinguished from psyche-somatic-symptom-disorder and psyche-illness-anxiety-disorder, where symptoms are **genuinely experienced and not fabricated** — there is no deception in those conditions. Distinguished from delusional illness conviction, where the person *believes* they are ill rather than knowingly feigning.

## Sensory / Behavioral Impact
The character is fluent in the world of illness — at home in waiting rooms, easy with clinical vocabulary, calmest when wearing the patient's or the caretaker's role. Outside that role they can seem unmoored; inside it they are competent, attended to, and located. There is a chronic low hunger for the particular kind of care, concern, and significance that sickness summons. In the imposed-on-another form, the caretaking presents as devotion — vigilant, tireless, conspicuously sacrificing — and the character's sense of self organizes around being needed by someone who must stay ill to keep needing them.

## Duration
Chronic, often lifelong as a pattern, expressed in episodes. Within a scene the role is usually stable and protective; it can crack when the character is confronted, separated from the medical setting, or threatened with exposure — which can produce alarm, denial, anger, or abrupt flight to a fresh setting where the role can begin again.

## Presentation & Tells
*(Surface signals an outside-POV character might register — not a how-to, and never a checklist for "catching" anyone.)*
- A medical story that is dramatic, extensive, and somehow always escalating; a history that doesn't quite cohere across tellings.
- Conspicuous fluency in clinical terms and procedures; ease and even relief in hospital settings that others find draining.
- Symptoms that behave oddly — present vividly when described, harder to pin down when looked for.
- In the imposed-on-another form, a caretaker who is intensely, performatively devoted; whose child or dependent's condition improves when separated from them and worsens in their sole presence.
- Resistance to discharge or to "good news"; a flatness or distress when told there is nothing wrong, where most would feel relief.
- Visible discomfort, deflection, or anger when the account is gently questioned.

## Interiority / POV Voice
This is the hardest interior to write honestly, because the deception is conscious and the need beneath it is not. From the inside there is rarely a villain's calculation; there is a hollow that the sick-role, or the indispensable-caretaker role, briefly fills. Being attended to feels like being real. The character may hold the contradiction without resolving it — *I know this isn't true and I cannot stop needing it to be* — and that knowing is its own torment, walled off so it can't be looked at directly. In the imposed-on-another form the interior is more frightening precisely because it can feel, to the perpetrator, like love: the closeness, the importance, the being-needed are experienced as devotion even as the conduct is abuse. The narrative must hold both at once and let neither cancel the other — the genuine inner deprivation *and* the real harm done. What the character refuses to look at is the cost: to the people exhausted by the crises, to a dependent made sick to keep a bond alive, and to the buried, unbearable question of why being well is not enough to be loved.

## Dramatic Affordances
This character reaches for the crisis, the bedside, the role that makes them central and cared-for or indispensable. Drama lives in the long con of sympathy and the slow dawning of doubt in someone close — the partner, the nurse, the relative who starts to notice the seams. It opens grave, high-stakes story in the imposed-on-another form: a child whose safety depends on an adult naming what they don't want to see, a clinician caught between presumption of devotion and a pattern that won't add up. The quiet truth lives in the rare moment the role drops and the deprivation underneath shows — a person who learned that only sickness, theirs or another's, reliably brought care, and who is more frightened of being unremarkable than of being caught.

## Fences — Responsible Use
- **No operational detail.** Do not write, imply, or supply any method for falsifying or inducing illness, evading detection, or deceiving clinicians or caregivers. Render the *lived experience and outward presentation* for characterization only — never a manual. This fence is absolute and non-negotiable.
- This is a **serious disorder rooted in real psychological need**, not cartoon villainy. Resist the pure-manipulator caricature; the deception is conscious but the driving need usually is not, and most people with the self-imposed form are suffering, not scheming for gain.
- Distinguish it cleanly from malingering (external payoff, not a disorder) and from the genuinely-experienced somatic conditions (psyche-somatic-symptom-disorder, psyche-illness-anxiety-disorder, psyche-functional-neurological-disorder) — conflating "psychosomatic" or "anxious about health" patients with "fakers" is exactly the harm this card must not feed. Those people are not lying.
- The **imposed on another** form is **child/dependent abuse** and must be framed as such — a victim is being harmed. Hold the perpetrator's inner deprivation and the victim's reality together; do not let "but she loved him" excuse the harm, and do not let the harm reduce the perpetrator to a flat monster.
- Real people live with this and can be helped; the card renders a character, not a verdict. But the victim's safety is never the perpetrator's story to soften.

## Interaction Notes
Borders psyche-somatic-symptom-disorder along a bright line — same somatic-disorders chapter, opposite truth-conditions: SSD symptoms are genuine, factitious symptoms are falsified; do not blur them when both appear in a cast. Trauma and attachment injury (psyche-ptsd, early neglect) frequently underwrite the need the role is filling. Personality structure colors expression — psyche-borderline-pd toward the emptiness and the desperate bid for care, psyche-antisocial-pd traits toward the colder, controlling end of the imposed-on-another form. With psyche-major-depressive-disorder the sick-role can be where an unspeakable inner state finds a language. With environmental condition cards — the hospital ward, the ICU vigil, the sympathetic crowd — the setting supplies the exact social oxygen the role runs on; a setting that withholds attention or invites scrutiny destabilizes it.
