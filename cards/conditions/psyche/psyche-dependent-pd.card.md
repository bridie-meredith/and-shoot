# Dependent Personality Disorder (DPD) — Psyche Profile

## Description
This is a psyche-profile condition card: it colors a *character's* behavior, perception, and interiority (not a location). Dependent personality disorder is a pervasive and excessive need to be taken care of, producing submissive, clinging behavior and fear of separation — a person who experiences self-direction as dangerous and being alone as a kind of helplessness, and who organizes a life around keeping a caretaker close.

## Clinical Profile
- **Taxonomy:** DSM-5-TR — Personality Disorders → Cluster C (anxious/fearful) → Dependent Personality Disorder. ICD-11's dimensional model maps the picture onto pronounced *negative affectivity* with marked dependency/submissiveness, anxiety, and need for reassurance.
- **Core features:**
  - Difficulty making everyday decisions without excessive advice and reassurance.
  - Needs others to assume responsibility for major areas of life.
  - Difficulty disagreeing for fear of losing support or approval.
  - Trouble initiating projects or acting independently (from doubt in own judgment, not lack of motivation).
  - Goes to lengths to obtain nurturance and support, even volunteering for unpleasant tasks.
  - Discomfort or helplessness when alone, from fear of being unable to care for oneself.
  - Urgently seeks a new relationship as a source of care when one ends.
- **Onset & course:** Roots often in childhood overprotection, illness, or anxious attachment; consolidates by early adulthood. Chronic and trait-like, modifiable with therapy and graduated experiences of competent independence.
- **Prevalence:** Approximately 0.5–1% in community samples.
- **Common comorbidities:** Avoidant (psyche-avoidant-pd) and borderline (psyche-borderline-pd) personality traits; histrionic (psyche-histrionic-pd) features; depressive and anxiety disorders.
- **Differential:** Distinguished from avoidant PD (the avoidant person withdraws from relationships out of fear of rejection; the dependent person clings *into* them out of fear of being alone). Distinguished from borderline PD (where abandonment fear coexists with rage, identity instability, and self-harm, vs. DPD's submissive, appeasing, conflict-averse stance). Normal interdependence or culturally valued deference is not DPD absent the impairment and the fear.

## Sensory / Behavioral Impact
The character's attention orients to the caretaker — what they want, what will please them, whether they're still close — more than to the character's own preferences, which can be genuinely hard to locate. Decisions stall; the character looks outward for the answer, the permission, the reassurance. Affect is appeasing, eager to smooth, quick to apologize and accommodate. Energy and confidence rise sharply in a trusted person's presence and drop, sometimes into a felt helplessness, in their absence.

## Duration
Chronic and trait-like across a lifetime, but the moment-to-moment state swings with proximity to the attachment figure. Within a single scene, the supported character can function with surprising competence; the threat of a caretaker's displeasure or departure can collapse them into anxious appeasement or near-paralysis. The end of a relationship is acute and dangerous, driving an urgent search for a replacement source of care.

## Presentation & Tells
- Defers decisions — "whatever you think," "you choose," "what would you do?" — even on small things.
- Seeks reassurance repeatedly; checks and re-checks that they've done right, that the other isn't upset.
- Agrees readily, struggles to voice disagreement or say no; goes along to keep the peace.
- Volunteers for thankless tasks and tolerates poor treatment to preserve the relationship.
- Visibly anxious or at a loss when a key person is away; needs to know when they'll be back.
- Self-doubt as a verbal habit — "I'm probably wrong," "I can never decide these things."
- After a breakup, moves quickly and intensely to attach to someone new.

## Interiority / POV Voice
From inside, being alone is not solitude — it is exposure, the floor gone, a competence the character is sure they don't have. The narrating voice runs toward the other person for ballast: *what does she think I should do, is he upset with me, will they still be there.* Their own preferences come in faint and unreliable, second-guessed before they're finished, so the simplest choice — what to order, which way to go — opens a small vertigo, and the relief of someone else deciding is enormous and immediate. Disagreement feels physically dangerous, as if the wrong word could end everything, so the voice swallows objections and rounds toward whatever keeps the peace, often without quite noticing it's doing it. There is real love in this, and real fear braided through it — the caretaker is the ground the whole self stands on, and the prospect of losing them isn't sadness but a kind of falling. When supported, the voice is warmer and steadier than anyone expects; the dependence isn't incapacity so much as a deep conviction, learned somewhere early, that they cannot stand up alone. What it refuses to look at is its own strength — the times it managed fine, the judgment that was sound — because to see that would mean the scaffolding could come down, and the height is terrifying.

## Dramatic Affordances
The character reaches for the other person — for permission, for protection, for the relief of being decided-for — which makes them loyal, accommodating, devoted, and quietly self-erasing. They are the one who will stay, who will forgive, who will hold a relationship together past the point others would leave. Drama lives in the gap between devotion and self-loss: the partner who keeps them small, the decision they finally make alone, the leaving that feels like falling, the slow discovery that they can in fact stand. The arc of finding one's own ground — through loss, through a relationship that refuses to carry them, through forced independence — is the natural and moving one. The quiet truth: the dependence is a real terror, not weakness of character, and the same capacity for devotion becomes, once it's no longer fear-driven, a genuine gift.

## Fences — Responsible Use
Real people live with DPD; this card renders a character, not a verdict. Refuse the contemptuous read of "clingy," "needy," "doormat," or "pathetic" — the dependence is a fear-driven survival pattern, not weakness or a character flaw, and the capacity for loyalty and care inside it is real. Do not confuse it with ordinary interdependence, with cultures and circumstances that legitimately value deference and family reliance, or with the temporary dependence of illness or crisis. Avoid the lazy "love cures them" beat; growth comes from experiences of *own* competence, not from a better caretaker. People with DPD are vulnerable to exploitation precisely because of the pattern — render that vulnerability with care, and resist making the character merely an object lesson or a burden. Let them be a full person whose terror of standing alone coexists with real strengths they can't yet see.

## Interaction Notes
Stacks with psyche-avoidant-pd (fear of rejection plus fear of aloneness — wanting people desperately and being terrified both of losing them and of being judged by them) and with psyche-borderline-pd (the abandonment fear shared, but appeasement where borderline brings volatility). With psyche-histrionic-pd the need for care fuses with the need for attention. Comorbid depression deepens the felt helplessness and self-doubt. Environmental condition cards matter: settings that demand independent decision and self-direction (a sudden loss, being thrown on one's own resources) destabilize and reveal the pattern; stable, supported settings let the character function and mask how much they're leaning. Pair deliberately with a controlling or exploitative figure for the central tension — or with a caretaker who refuses to carry them, for the growth arc.
