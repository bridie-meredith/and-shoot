# Pica — Psyche Profile

## Description
A psyche-profile condition card that colors a *character's* behavior, perception, and interiority — not a location. Pica is the persistent eating of non-nutritive, non-food substances, developmentally inappropriate and not part of a culturally supported practice — a pull toward the non-edible that the character experiences as craving, comfort, or compulsion rather than as choice.

## Clinical Profile
- **Taxonomy:** DSM-5-TR — Feeding and Eating Disorders. ICD-11 — Feeding or eating disorders, code grouping 6B84.
- **Core features:**
  - Persistent eating of non-nutritive, non-food substances over a sustained period.
  - The behavior is inappropriate to the person's developmental level.
  - It is not a sanctioned cultural or social practice (some practices around earth, for example, are culturally normative and are not pica).
  - The substances vary by person — earth, ice, paper, hair, chalk, paint, and the like are commonly described; specifics are person-dependent.
  - When it occurs alongside another condition, it is severe enough to warrant independent clinical attention.
- **Onset & course:** Most often emerges in childhood; also notably associated with pregnancy and with certain nutritional deficiencies (such as iron), where it may remit when the underlying state is addressed. In the context of neurodevelopmental conditions it can be more persistent.
- **Prevalence:** Hard to estimate and likely under-reported; elevated among young children, during pregnancy, and among people with `psyche-intellectual-disability` or `psyche-autism-spectrum`.
- **Common comorbidities:** `psyche-intellectual-disability`, `psyche-autism-spectrum`, `psyche-ocd`, and occasionally `psyche-schizophrenia`; also linked to nutritional-deficiency states and pregnancy, which are medical rather than psychiatric.
- **Differential:** Distinguished from culturally sanctioned consumption of certain substances (not pica) and from intentional self-harm by ingestion (different intent); from ordinary mouthing in very young children by persistence and developmental inappropriateness.

## Sensory / Behavioral Impact
The character is drawn toward a particular substance with a pull that feels bodily and specific — a craving for a texture, a coolness, a grit, a chalk, a particular dry resistance against the teeth. The act can be soothing, regulating, almost meditative, or it can be near-automatic, barely noticed. Attention snags on the substance when it is present. The aftermath may carry no distress at all for the character, while carrying real medical risk the character does not register as risk. There can be secrecy, born of past correction, more than of internal shame.

## Duration
Variable. Tied to a deficiency or to pregnancy it may be time-limited and resolve with the underlying cause; tied to a neurodevelopmental condition it tends to be chronic and stable. Within a scene the urge surges in the presence of the preferred substance and quiets in its absence; stress, boredom, or under-stimulation can sharpen it. It is a steady trait far more than a flaring one.

## Presentation & Tells
- Reaching for and mouthing or eating a specific non-food substance, sometimes openly, sometimes hidden after past scolding.
- A particular soothing or absorbed quality during the act — a self-regulation that reads as calm.
- The substance kept close: in a pocket, a drawer, a known spot; territory the character returns to.
- For the soothing presentation, the act paired with stress, transition, boredom, or sensory under-load.
- Genuine puzzlement or defensiveness when challenged — the character may not see it as a problem.
- Physical residue — discoloration, dental marks, the substance on hands or mouth.
- In neurodevelopmental contexts, braided into a broader self-stimulatory and sensory-seeking pattern.

## Interiority / POV Voice
The craving does not arrive as a strange idea; it arrives as an ordinary want, as specific and as legitimate-feeling as a thirst. The character wants the cold weight of the ice, the dry give of the chalk, the particular grain of the thing, and the wanting does not file itself under "wrong" — it files under "this is what I need right now." When they do it, something in them settles. The world, which had been too loud or too flat, comes into a tolerable register. They are not, from the inside, doing anything peculiar; they are meeting a need with the obvious thing that meets it. The strangeness lives entirely in other people's faces — the alarm, the *don't*, the snatching-away — and so the character learns, if they've been corrected enough, to do it where the faces aren't. What they may genuinely not look at is the harm, because the harm is abstract and the relief is immediate, and the body that craves the thing does not also send a warning about the thing. For a character with an intellectual or developmental condition the inner account may be simpler and more concrete still: this feels good, this helps, why are you upset.

## Dramatic Affordances
This condition gives a character a behavior that is intimate, self-soothing, and easily mistaken for something it isn't — defiance, attention-seeking, or madness — when it is most often a body meeting a need it can't name. The drama lives in the gap between the character's calm and the alarm of those around them, and in the tenderness or clumsiness of how others respond. It opens scenes of caregiving, of a pregnant character's secret craving, of a child returning to a hidden spot, of an autistic character regulating in the only language their body knows. The quiet truth lives in being understood rather than corrected — someone who asks *what does it give you* instead of *why would you do that*. It can also signal an unaddressed deficiency or distress, which is a thread worth following honestly.

## Fences — Responsible Use
Real people live with this; the card renders a character, not a verdict, and never an instruction. Do NOT specify substances as a how-to, quantities, or anything actionable — name that the behavior occurs; never write it as a guide, and never glamorize or sensationalize it. Refuse the freak-show framing — pica is not "crazy," not inherently a sign of psychosis, and not a punchline; it is frequently tied to ordinary causes like pregnancy, nutritional deficiency, or sensory regulation. Do not write it as willful disgustingness or as a marker of low character. Be especially careful with characters who have `psyche-intellectual-disability` or `psyche-autism-spectrum` — render the behavior with dignity and curiosity, not as proof of deficiency. The medical risk is real; treat it with care, not with melodrama, and do not detail it operationally.

## Interaction Notes
Stacks most commonly with `psyche-intellectual-disability` and `psyche-autism-spectrum`, where it sits inside a broader sensory-regulation and self-stimulatory pattern, and with `psyche-ocd` (the act can take on ritual structure and intrusive pull). It can co-occur with `psyche-schizophrenia` but should NOT be written as a default sign of psychosis — that conflation is exactly the stigma to avoid. Outside the psychiatric frame it is strongly tied to pregnancy and to nutritional-deficiency states, which are medical and may resolve with the underlying cause. In environmental conditions of scarcity, confinement, boredom, or sensory under-stimulation the pull intensifies; in supportive, well-stimulated, well-nourished conditions it may quiet.
