# Illness Anxiety Disorder (Hypochondriasis) — Psyche Profile

## Description
A psyche-profile condition card. It colors a *character's* behavior, perception, and interiority — not a location. Illness Anxiety Disorder (IAD), historically called hypochondriasis, is a preoccupying fear of having or acquiring a serious illness that persists even when somatic symptoms are mild or absent and medical evaluation comes back reassuring — the dread is the disorder, not the disease.

## Clinical Profile
- **Taxonomy:** DSM-5-TR, *Somatic Symptom and Related Disorders* — *Illness Anxiety Disorder*. ICD-11, *Hypochondriasis* (6B23), placed under obsessive-compulsive and related disorders, reflecting its kinship with anxiety/OCD-spectrum conditions.
- **Core features:**
  - Preoccupation with having or getting a serious illness.
  - Somatic symptoms are **absent or only mild** — the fear is out of proportion to any actual bodily evidence. (Where prominent symptoms drive the picture, the diagnosis is psyche-somatic-symptom-disorder instead.)
  - High anxiety about health; easily alarmed by personal health news or others' illness.
  - Excessive health-related behaviors (checking, researching, seeking reassurance) **or** maladaptive avoidance (refusing appointments, avoiding hospitals).
  - Preoccupation present 6+ months, though the feared illness may change.
  - Two informal patterns: the **care-seeking** type (constant checks and appointments) and the **care-avoidant** type (terror that keeps them away from doctors entirely).
- **Onset & course:** Typically begins in early-to-middle adulthood; can be triggered by a major stressor, a real illness in oneself or a loved one, or a health scare. Course is often **chronic and relapsing**, waxing with stress.
- **Prevalence:** Approximately 1–5% of the general population over a 1–2 year period; common in primary-care settings.
- **Common comorbidities:** psyche-generalized-anxiety-disorder, psyche-panic-disorder, psyche-major-depressive-disorder, and OCD-spectrum overlap (psyche-ocd); shares borders with psyche-somatic-symptom-disorder.
- **Differential:** Distinguished from psyche-somatic-symptom-disorder by the **near-absence of actual somatic symptoms** — IAD is fear-of-illness; SSD is distress-from-symptoms. Distinguished from psyche-ocd when health is the sole content (in OCD the obsessions/compulsions range wider). Distinguished from psyche-generalized-anxiety-disorder when health-catastrophe is the dominant, organizing fear rather than one worry among many. Not a delusion — the person can usually acknowledge their fear may be excessive, which separates it from delusional illness conviction.

## Sensory / Behavioral Impact
The character scans. Every neutral bodily event — a freckle's shade, a swallowed-wrong sip, a heartbeat felt at rest — becomes a data point routed straight to the worst case. Attention is hypervigilant and externally hungry for confirmation: search results, symptom lists, the faces of clinicians read for the flicker that means *they see it too.* Affect is anxious, sometimes spiking to dread; sleep frays around 3 a.m. researching. In the avoidant pattern the body's signals are met with flinch-and-look-away, and the character's energy goes into *not* finding out.

## Duration
Chronic and relapsing across a life; within a scene, a hovering background dread that **spikes** on any cue — a news segment about a disease, a friend's diagnosis, a sensation noticed. A clear scan or a doctor's flat reassurance drops it briefly, but the relief has a short half-life and the dread refills, often around a new candidate illness.

## Presentation & Tells
- Frequent doctor visits and second opinions — or, in the avoidant type, a conspicuous refusal to be seen at all.
- Reassurance-seeking that resets: "Are you sure it's nothing? But couldn't it be—?"
- Hours lost to symptom-searching; bookmarked condition pages; a vocabulary of disease that outpaces a layperson's.
- Body-checking — palpating lymph nodes, scanning skin, taking the pulse — performed quietly, repeatedly.
- A health scare in the news or in someone nearby visibly reorganizes their mood and conversation.
- Brief calm after a clean result, then a tell-tale return: the question rephrased, the worry re-pointed.

## Interiority / POV Voice
Inside, the logic is airtight and merciless: it *could* be the bad thing, and if it could be, then not-checking is reckless, and so the only responsible person in the room is the one who keeps looking. Reassurance is heard but does not land, because the mind has already moved to the next clause — *yes, but they didn't test for that.* There is a grim moral coloring to it; the worry feels like diligence, like love even, the price of taking one's mortality seriously while everyone else sleepwalks. What the character refuses to look at is that the searching never ends in safety — that no result is clean enough, because the function of the search is not to find an answer but to discharge the dread, and the dread regenerates. Underneath is a child's bargain: if I watch closely enough, I can keep the catastrophe from happening. And underneath that, plain mortality, looked at too directly and never quite blinked away.

## Dramatic Affordances
This character reaches for the search bar, the appointment, the person to be reassured by — or, in the avoidant key, the closed door and the changed subject. Drama lives in the loop: the clean result that should end the scene and doesn't, the loved one whose patience is a finite resource being spent. It opens the cruel irony where a real symptom is finally dismissed because the character has cried wolf, or where avoidance lets a treatable thing grow. The quiet truth lives in the moment the fear is named as fear — *I'm not actually afraid of the lump, I'm afraid of dying and I don't know how to stop checking* — when the catastrophe-vocabulary drops and what's left is just a frightened person.

## Fences — Responsible Use
- This is a **real anxiety disorder**, not vanity or self-indulgence. The fear is genuinely felt and genuinely painful; do not write it as performance.
- Refuse the "hypochondriac" punchline. The comic worried-well figure is a stigmatizing caricature that has kept real sufferers from being heard.
- Do not collapse IAD into "stupid" or "won't listen." The character *can* often see the fear is excessive and is tormented precisely by being unable to act on that insight.
- Render the avoidant type with equal weight — the person too frightened to be seen is as real as the over-seen one, and arguably at greater risk.
- Real people live with this; the card renders a character, not a verdict. Reassurance-resistance is a feature of the disorder, not a character flaw to be scolded.
- No operational detail: no health-system-gaming or self-checking how-to.

## Interaction Notes
Sits a half-step from psyche-somatic-symptom-disorder (fear-of-illness vs. distress-from-symptom — the two can co-occur and trade dominance). Stacks naturally with psyche-ocd when the checking and reassurance-seeking take on ritual structure, and with psyche-panic-disorder when a body sensation is misread as a heart attack. With psyche-major-depressive-disorder the dread can shade toward hopeless certainty. With environmental condition cards — a hospital corridor, a glowing screen at night, a sickbed in the next room — the ambient setting can drive the spike or, in a calm trusted space, briefly settle it.
