# Neurocognitive Disorder due to Traumatic Brain Injury — Psyche Profile

## Description
This is a psyche-profile condition card. It colors a *character's* behavior, perception, and interiority — not a location. Neurocognitive disorder due to traumatic brain injury is an acquired cognitive impairment caused by a physical insult to the brain — a blow, a blast, a fall, a crash — producing deficits (most often in attention, processing speed, executive control, memory, and emotional regulation) that persist past the acute injury. Unlike degenerative dementia it stems from a *fixed event*, and its course typically stabilizes or improves rather than relentlessly worsening.

## Clinical Profile
- **Taxonomy:** DSM-5-TR — Neurocognitive Disorders, "Major or Mild Neurocognitive Disorder Due to Traumatic Brain Injury." ICD-11 — chapter 06, Neurocognitive disorders grouping (Dementia / mild neurocognitive disorder due to TBI), with the injury itself coded under chapter 22 (injury).
- **Core features:**
  - Cognitive decline arising from a TBI with at least one of: loss of consciousness, post-traumatic amnesia, disorientation/confusion at injury, or neurological signs (the deficit must be temporally tied to the injury).
  - The leading deficits are typically **executive function, attention, processing speed, and memory** — more than the pure memory-first profile of Alzheimer's.
  - Prominent **neurobehavioral and emotional change**: irritability, short fuse, impulsivity, disinhibition, emotional lability, apathy, blunted or exaggerated affect — frontal-lobe-mediated and often the most disruptive piece.
  - Frequent physical and sensory sequelae: fatigue, headache, dizziness, light/noise sensitivity, sleep disruption, sometimes seizures.
  - Severity scales with injury severity (mild/concussion through severe) and with location; deficit *pattern* depends on which region was hurt.
- **Onset & course:** Onset is dated to the injury — abrupt, not insidious. Course is usually **static or gradually improving** over months to a couple of years (especially after milder injury), then plateauing; severe injury can leave permanent deficits. Distinct from degenerative dementia's downward slope — though repetitive head trauma carries its own later-life risks.
- **Prevalence:** TBI is extremely common (millions of cases yearly); persistent neurocognitive disorder is a subset concentrated in moderate-to-severe injuries — roughly on the order of low-single-digit percent of the general population carry lasting TBI-related cognitive impairment, far higher in injured cohorts (veterans, athletes, survivors of severe trauma).
- **Common comorbidities:** PTSD (psyche-ptsd) — especially when the injury was itself traumatic (combat, assault, crash); depression (psyche-major-depressive-disorder); anxiety; substance use; chronic pain and sleep disorder.
- **Differential:** Distinguished from **delirium** (psyche-delirium) by the dated injury and the stable-or-improving course versus acute fluctuation. Distinguished from **degenerative dementia** (psyche-major-neurocognitive-disorder) by the abrupt traumatic onset and non-progressive trajectory. Distinguished from **PTSD** by the organic cognitive deficit — though the two overlap heavily and can be hard to disentangle, and frequently coexist.

## Sensory / Behavioral Impact
The character runs slower than they remember running — thoughts arrive a beat late, conversations move too fast to track, multi-step tasks drop a step. Attention is leaky; a phone notification or a loud room derails the whole train. The fuse is short and the brakes are soft — irritation flares before the reasoning that would have caught it, and the apology comes after. Tears or laughter can break loose at the wrong size for the moment. Fatigue is bone-deep and cognitive, not just physical — thinking *costs* now in a way it didn't, and the tank empties by afternoon. Light is too bright, sound too sharp, and the headache lives behind the same eye. They are often acutely aware of the gap between who they were and who they are now.

## Duration
Chronic but typically **non-progressive** — the deficit is the lasting signature of a past event, not a worsening disease. Within a scene the character's baseline is relatively *stable*, but it degrades sharply under load: fatigue, overstimulation (crowds, noise, screens), stress, alcohol, poor sleep, or cognitive demand stacking up all tip them from coping into floundering — the "I was fine this morning, by 3pm I'm useless" curve. What shifts a scene: rest, quiet, one-thing-at-a-time pacing, and accommodation steady them; sensory overload, time pressure, and being asked to multitask collapse them. Over a longer arc, gradual recovery and learned compensation are real and worth rendering.

## Presentation & Tells
- Loses the thread in conversation; asks for repeats; "wait, what were we saying?"
- Visible lag — pauses before answering, processes a beat behind the room.
- Snaps at small frustrations, then is genuinely remorseful; the temper is faster than the person.
- Forgets appointments, names, where the car is parked; relies heavily on lists, alarms, notes.
- Wears down visibly across a day — words slur with fatigue, errors climb, retreats from stimulation.
- Squints in bright light, flinches at loud noise, wears sunglasses or earplugs indoors.
- Says or does the blunt, unfiltered thing, then can see it landed wrong.
- Emotional weather that doesn't fit the room — crying at a commercial, flat at a funeral, laughter that won't stop.
- Mourns the old self out loud: "I used to be able to do this in my sleep."

## Interiority / POV Voice
From inside, the most haunting fact is the *comparison* — this character usually remembers competence they no longer have, so the voice carries a constant doubled awareness: the task in front of them and the ghost of how easy it used to be. Write the lag from the inside — the sense of reaching for a word or a name and finding the shelf empty where it has always been, the room's conversation moving like water they can't keep their feet in. Write the fuse honestly: the irritation that arrives *before* the judgment that would have softened it, so the angry words are out before the self that would have stopped them is even online — and the helpless, shamed clarity that follows. Render the fatigue as a cost meter draining, the afternoon collapse, the rationing of a depleting resource that used to be infinite. There is grief here, often unspoken: a person negotiating with a brain that broke and a self that didn't change its expectations. And there can be fierce, organized compensation — the systems, the notebooks, the routines built as scaffolding around the holes — and pride in them, and the fear of the day the scaffolding isn't enough. Avoid the empty stare; this lens is frequently *sharp and self-aware*, which is precisely what makes it hurt.

## Dramatic Affordances
This is the changed-person story with an unimpeachable cause — the relationships have to renegotiate around someone who is recognizably themselves and recognizably altered, and no one gets to be the villain. It opens the return-to-life arc: the rehab grind, the relearning, the small wins that are enormous and the plateaus that aren't talked about. It opens the loved-one's grief for a living person and the guilt of grieving them. It sets the temper against the remorse for a built-in engine of conflict-and-repair. It is rich for the soldier, the athlete, the crash-survivor — the injury entangled with PTSD so that the cognitive and the traumatic can't be cleanly told apart, and the character can't tell which symptom is which. It opens questions of identity and continuity (am I still the person who signed this, made that promise, loved like that?) and of capacity and dignity. The quiet truth lives in the accommodations — the partner who learned to slow down, the list on the fridge, the love that adapted its shape.

## Fences — Responsible Use
Refuse the two cheap registers: the *vegetable* (the injured brain as an empty husk — most TBI survivors are awake, sharp in places, and painfully aware) and the *unstable time-bomb* (TBI as a license for a character to become randomly, frighteningly violent). Irritability and disinhibition are real, but they are usually directed *inward* in shame far more than outward in danger; people with TBI are more often harmed, exploited, and abandoned than dangerous, and the temper is a regulation deficit followed by remorse, not menace. Do not treat cognitive deficits as comic or as a personality downgrade — render the preserved intelligence and the grief over the gap. Honor the *non-progressive* truth: this character can recover and adapt; do not write them off. Avoid conflating TBI with intellectual disability or with degenerative dementia. Real people live with this — many of them veterans, athletes, and survivors — and the card renders a character mid-recovery, not a verdict on their mind. NO operational detail about causing harm — render lived experience and presentation only.

## Interaction Notes
- **+ psyche-ptsd:** the signature pairing when the injury was traumatic (blast, assault, crash). The two interlock — flashbacks, hypervigilance, and avoidance tangle with processing-speed and memory deficits, and the character (and clinicians) often cannot cleanly separate "trauma symptom" from "brain-injury symptom." Render the entanglement, not two tidy diagnoses side by side.
- **+ psyche-major-depressive-disorder:** depression is common after TBI and amplifies fatigue, apathy, and slowed cognition — a downward feedback loop, and the depression is treatable even when the deficit isn't.
- **+ psyche-delirium:** acute decompensation can ride on a TBI baseline (post-injury, or with later illness); contrast the acute fluctuation against the stable deficit.
- **+ psyche-major-neurocognitive-disorder:** useful as a foil — both present cognitive decline, but TBI is fixed-event-and-improving where dementia is insidious-and-progressive. Repetitive head trauma is also a later-life risk vector, so an older injured character can carry both threads.
- **+ environmental condition cards:** noise, crowding, bright/flickering light, screens, time pressure, and sleep loss are direct *aggravators* — a loud chaotic setting doesn't just inconvenience this character, it actively degrades their function within the scene. Quiet, dim, low-demand environments are restorative. Setting is an active load variable here.
