# Insomnia Disorder — Psyche Profile

## Description
A psyche-profile condition card. It colors a *character's* behavior, perception, and interiority — not a location. Insomnia disorder is a persistent dissatisfaction with sleep quantity or quality — trouble falling asleep, staying asleep, or waking too early — that the character can't fix by trying, and that bleeds into their daytime as fatigue, irritability, and a mind that won't downshift.

## Clinical Profile
- **Taxonomy:** DSM-5-TR — Sleep-Wake Disorders (Insomnia Disorder). ICD-11 — Sleep-Wake Disorders, "Insomnia disorders" grouping (chronic insomnia, code 7A00).
- **Core features:**
  - Predominant complaint of dissatisfaction with sleep: difficulty initiating sleep, difficulty maintaining it (frequent or long night wakings), or early-morning waking with inability to return to sleep.
  - The sleep trouble causes real daytime impairment — fatigue, mood disturbance, concentration lapses, reduced function — not just a quiet night.
  - Occurs despite adequate opportunity and circumstances for sleep (the bed is available, the room is quiet; the character still can't).
  - For the disorder (vs. a passing rough patch): present at least 3 nights/week for at least 3 months.
  - Hyperarousal is central — a body and mind that stay "on," a nervous system that won't drop into rest even when exhausted.
  - Conditioned arousal: the bed itself becomes a cue for wakefulness; the character lies down and snaps alert.
- **Onset & course:** Can begin at any age; common in adulthood and rises with age and with menopause. Often situational at first (stress, loss, illness, a deadline), then self-perpetuating — the worry about not sleeping becomes the thing that prevents sleep. Course is frequently chronic and relapsing, flaring with stress.
- **Prevalence:** Approximately 10–15% of adults meet criteria for chronic insomnia disorder; transient insomnia symptoms are far more common (roughly a third of adults in a given year).
- **Common comorbidities:** Depression and anxiety disorders (bidirectional — each worsens the other), chronic pain, PTSD, substance use. See psyche-major-depressive-disorder, psyche-generalized-anxiety-disorder, psyche-ptsd.
- **Differential:** Distinguish from circadian-rhythm disorders (the character *can* sleep, just not on the wanted schedule), from sleep deprivation by choice (no adequate opportunity), from narcolepsy (excessive daytime sleepiness with sleep that comes *too* easily — see psyche-narcolepsy), and from the normal short sleeper who needs little sleep and feels fine.

## Sensory / Behavioral Impact
Runs the character at a low, constant deficit. Attention is grainy — they lose the threads of conversations, re-read the same line, miss the obvious. Reaction time lags. Affect is brittle: small frustrations land hard, and the mood floor sits lower than the character would set it sober and rested. The body carries it — gritty eyes, a headache behind the brow, a heaviness that never resolves into actual sleep. Temperature regulation feels off; they're cold, then clammy. Time distorts at night: minutes on the clock stretch, and the character does math on how many hours are left before the alarm. By late afternoon there's a flat, scoured feeling — not sleepy enough to nap, not awake enough to be sharp.

## Duration
Chronic and relapsing by definition (the disorder requires months), but the *intensity* swings night to night and within a single night. Inside a scene the character is stable-tired — the impairment is a baseline, not an event — but it can sharpen mid-scene: a stressor spikes the hyperarousal; mention of bedtime or of last night's sleep can pull their attention inward; a second wind near midnight can flip exhaustion into wired alertness. A good night (rare, precious) visibly lifts them the next day, which they'll mention or guard against jinxing.

## Presentation & Tells
- Dark circles, puffy or red-rimmed eyes; an effortful, propped quality to alertness.
- Yawning that doesn't relieve anything; rubbing the eyes, the back of the neck.
- Caffeine as infrastructure — a cup always in hand, refills tracked, a flinch when it's offered too late in the day.
- Microlapses: loses the thread, asks you to repeat, trails off mid-sentence.
- Irritability disproportionate to the trigger, then a beat of guilt or apology ("sorry — I didn't sleep").
- Talks about sleep with a specific, weary expertise — sleep hygiene, what they've tried, the clock math.
- Night-shifted habits: still awake when others have gone to bed, texts time-stamped at 3 a.m., a lit window.
- A wired-tired contradiction — visibly exhausted yet unable to stop, leg jiggling, scrolling.

## Interiority / POV Voice
The inside of insomnia is a mind that won't stop narrating. Lying in the dark, the character isn't thinking about anything important — they're aware of *thinking*, of the machinery running, and the awareness is the trap. The harder they reach for sleep, the further it backs off, like a word on the tip of the tongue. There's a running tally: *if I fall asleep now I get five hours. Now four and a half. Now four.* Each calculation costs more sleep than it saves, and they know that, and they do it anyway.

The bed is hostile. Daytime exhaustion promises that tonight will be different — the body so tired it can't possibly stay up — and then they lie down and something clicks on, alert and bright and useless. The voice is bargaining (*just let me sleep, I'll do anything*), then accusatory (*everyone else manages this, why can't I do the most basic animal thing*), then resigned. By day the mind is the opposite: foggy, slow, the words a half-second behind. They watch rested people move through the world with an envy that has no edge of anger left in it, just tiredness. They guard the idea of a good night superstitiously and refuse to hope out loud, because hoping seems to scare sleep away. Underneath it all is a particular loneliness — being awake while the world sleeps, the only lit window, carrying a problem no amount of trying will solve.

## Dramatic Affordances
The 3 a.m. scene is its native habitat — the character awake when no one else is, available for the confession, the quiet realization, the phone call no one makes in daylight. Insomnia is a slow-burn cost: it grinds a character down across a long arc without a single dramatic blow, so the audience watches competence erode and judgment thin. It pairs with high-stakes plots (the exhausted surgeon, the detective who won't stop, the new parent) where the deficit raises the danger. The wired-tired contradiction gives a character a manic, over-sharp edge that can read as brilliance or as someone about to break. Relationally it isolates — the partner asleep beside them, the gap of consciousness. And the rare good night, rendered honestly, is a small mercy the audience feels.

## Fences — Responsible Use
Real people live with chronic insomnia; this card renders a character, not a verdict, and not a punchline. Avoid the lazy shorthand where insomnia is just "quirky night-owl energy" with no cost, or conversely a glamorous tortured-genius prop. It is not cured by "just relaxing," by willpower, or by a single good talk — characters (and other characters) who treat it that way should be wrong on the page, not endorsed. Do not write operational detail on medication misuse, on dangerous self-treatment, or on substances used to force sleep — render the desperation and the lived experience, never a how-to. Don't conflate insomnia with laziness or weakness; the character is *trying*, and the trying is the trap. Keep the suffering real without making the character only their suffering.

## Interaction Notes
- **With psyche-narcolepsy:** clinical opposites that can be confused on the surface (both are tired) but are physiologically inverse — the insomniac can't reach sleep; the narcoleptic can't keep it away. A scene pairing the two can play the irony.
- **With psyche-generalized-anxiety-disorder / psyche-ptsd:** tightly bidirectional. Worry and hyperarousal feed the insomnia; the insomnia thins the character's defenses against worry. Nightmares (PTSD) make the bed an actively feared place, deepening conditioned arousal.
- **With psyche-major-depressive-disorder:** early-morning waking is a classic depressive signature; insomnia and low mood spiral together, each lowering the floor of the other.
- **With environmental condition cards:** stacks hard with anything that degrades the sleep setting — noise, light, heat, an unfamiliar or unsafe location. A loud, bright, or threatening environment turns a manageable night into a wall.
