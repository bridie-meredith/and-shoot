# Major Depressive Disorder — Psyche Profile

## Description
A psyche-profile condition card. It colors a *character's* behavior, perception, and interiority — not a location. Major Depressive Disorder (MDD) is a mood disorder defined by discrete episodes of pervasive low mood and/or loss of pleasure that flatten a person's emotional, physical, and cognitive baseline for weeks at a time. Its one-line essence: the color drains out of the world, and the effort cost of every ordinary thing goes up.

## Clinical Profile
- **Taxonomy:** DSM-5-TR — Depressive Disorders. ICD-11 — Mood Disorders, Depressive disorders grouping (single episode 6A70 / recurrent 6A71).
- **Core features:**
  - At least two weeks of either depressed mood most of the day, or markedly diminished interest/pleasure in nearly all activities (anhedonia) — one of these must be present.
  - Significant change in appetite or weight; sleep disturbance (insomnia or hypersomnia).
  - Psychomotor agitation or retardation observable to others; fatigue or loss of energy nearly every day.
  - Feelings of worthlessness or excessive, inappropriate guilt.
  - Diminished ability to think, concentrate, or decide.
  - Recurrent thoughts of death; the symptoms cause clinically significant distress or impairment.
- **Onset & course:** Can begin at any age; median onset often mid-to-late twenties, with first episodes increasingly seen in adolescence. Course is episodic and frequently recurrent — many who have one episode have others; some episodes remit fully between, others leave residual symptoms.
- **Prevalence:** Approximately 8–10% point/annual prevalence in many surveyed populations; lifetime prevalence roughly 15–20%. Diagnosed about twice as often in women as in men.
- **Common comorbidities:** Anxiety disorders (esp. psyche-generalized-anxiety-disorder), trauma-related conditions (psyche-ptsd), substance use (psyche-alcohol-use-disorder), and chronic low-grade depression underneath (psyche-persistent-depressive-disorder — "double depression").
- **Differential:** Distinguished from ordinary grief or sadness by pervasiveness, duration, and the loss of self-worth/function. Distinguished from psyche-persistent-depressive-disorder by episode structure (discrete vs. chronic-low). Distinguished from bipolar depression by the absence of any prior manic/hypomanic episode — a hidden history of mania reclassifies the picture entirely.

## Sensory / Behavioral Impact
The character moves through a scene at a reduced rate. Time stretches and thickens; simple decisions (what to eat, whether to answer) acquire weight out of proportion. Pleasure response is muted or absent — food has less taste, music less reach, a friend's joke lands at a distance. Attention is foggy and adhesive: thoughts circle back to the same dark grooves and resist redirection. The body itself feels heavier; getting up, showering, crossing a room can register as genuine labor. Affect is flattened on the surface even when pain runs high underneath, so the visible signal often *understates* the internal state.

## Duration
Episodic and chronic-within-the-episode. An episode runs weeks to many months; within a single scene the depressed baseline is stable and not easily moved. Diurnal variation is common — mornings are frequently the worst, with a partial lift toward evening. A scene can show a flicker of contact (a brief laugh, a moment of warmth) that the character cannot hold; the flicker fades and the weight returns rather than the depression "lifting."

## Presentation & Tells
- Slowed speech, longer pauses, a flatter or quieter voice; sometimes a forced brightness that costs visible effort.
- Reduced eye contact; gaze drifting to the floor or middle distance.
- Neglected self-maintenance — unwashed hair, same clothes, an apartment that has stopped being kept.
- Cancelled plans, unanswered messages, a slow withdrawal framed as "tired" or "busy."
- Self-deprecating asides delivered as fact, not bid for reassurance.
- Tearfulness with no proximate trigger — or a striking *inability* to cry despite obvious pain.
- Difficulty starting tasks; standing in a doorway, staring at a screen, the coffee going cold.

## Interiority / POV Voice
From inside, the defining feature is not sadness so much as *distance* — a pane of glass between the self and everything that used to mean something. The inner voice is tired and certain. It does not argue; it concludes. It narrates the self in a low, flat register and treats bleak verdicts ("I'm a burden," "this won't get better," "no one would notice") as plain observation rather than symptom. Effort is the recurring theme: the mind constantly tallies the cost of the next action and finds it too high. Anhedonia reads, from the inside, as a kind of bewildered absence — the character can *remember* that this song, this person, this view once moved them, and feel the place where the feeling should be and isn't. Guilt is sticky and globalizing; one small lapse becomes evidence of a whole defective character. The future foreshortens — it is hard to picture next month, and harder to believe it will feel different. What the voice refuses to look at directly is any genuine hope, which it has learned to distrust as a setup for disappointment. Crucially, the depressed narrator usually does not experience this as illness; it experiences it as *finally seeing clearly*. That conviction — that the bleakness is truth, not distortion — is the load-bearing center of the POV.

## Dramatic Affordances
This character reaches for stillness, withdrawal, and the smallest viable version of any task — where everyone else in the scene reaches for momentum. The drama lives in the gap between what they manage to do and what it costs them to do it: showing up to a thing they cannot enjoy, holding a face for someone they don't want to worry. Relationships strain along the line of the loved one who keeps offering solutions to a person who cannot use them yet. Powerful scenes: the tiny act of self-care that is actually a triumph; the friend who simply sits without fixing; the flat refusal of a comfort that would have once worked. The quiet truth lives in the moments of contact the character can't sustain — proof, to the audience if not to them, that the feeling is still in there. Recovery, when it comes, is undramatic and gradual; the honest arc is the return of small appetites, not a single cathartic break.

## Fences — Responsible Use
- Real people live with this, many of them functional and unseen. The card renders a character, not a verdict on their worth or willpower. Depression is not weakness, laziness, or a failure of gratitude.
- Refuse the "snap out of it" frame — that advice could be given by a *callous* character, but the narration must not endorse it as a cure.
- Avoid the romantic-tragic-poet caricature that aestheticizes the illness. Avoid the inverse: that a relationship, a trip, or a single good day "fixes" it.
- Anhedonia and flat affect mean the most-suffering character is often the *least* visibly dramatic. Don't require visible sobbing to make the pain legible.
- NO operational or method detail regarding self-harm or suicide. Render the *lived experience* — the weight, the distance, the foreshortened future — never a manual. If thoughts of death are present, keep them at the level of felt experience and route toward connection and care, not instruction.

## Interaction Notes
- With psyche-persistent-depressive-disorder underneath, an MDD episode reads as a deepening of an already-grey baseline ("double depression") — the lift, if it comes, only returns to chronic-low, not to well.
- With psyche-generalized-anxiety-disorder, the flat depressive register alternates with anxious rumination; the character is both too tired to act and too wired to rest.
- With psyche-alcohol-use-disorder, self-medication can blunt the edge briefly and worsen the trough after — a downward coupling, not relief.
- With an environmental condition card (grief, isolation, winter, a confined space), the ambient state amplifies withdrawal and diurnal worsening; render the condition pressing *down* on an already-lowered baseline rather than originating the mood.
