# Panic Disorder — Psyche Profile

## Description
A psyche-profile condition card. It colors a *character's* behavior, perception, and interiority — not a location. Panic Disorder is recurrent, unexpected surges of intense terror — panic attacks — that crest within minutes with a violent physical signature, plus a persistent dread of the next one that quietly reorganizes the character's life around avoiding it.

## Clinical Profile
- **Taxonomy:** DSM-5-TR — Anxiety Disorders. ICD-11 — Anxiety or fear-related disorders (6B01, Panic disorder).
- **Core features:**
  - Recurrent panic attacks — abrupt surges of intense fear peaking within minutes.
  - Physical storm: pounding heart, sweating, trembling, shortness of breath, choking sensation, chest pain, nausea, dizziness, chills or heat, numbness/tingling.
  - Cognitive terror: fear of dying, of having a heart attack, of "losing control" or "going crazy," and depersonalization/derealization during the peak.
  - At least some attacks are *unexpected* — they come out of a calm moment, which is part of what makes them so frightening.
  - At least a month of persistent worry about more attacks (anticipatory anxiety) and/or maladaptive behavior change to avoid them.
  - The fear of the fear becomes the engine — the disorder is sustained by dread of the next attack more than by the attacks themselves.
- **Onset & course:** Typically late adolescence to mid-thirties. Course waxes and wanes; can remit and relapse. Untreated, frequently progresses toward avoidance and, in a subset, agoraphobia.
- **Prevalence:** Approximately 2–3% over a 12-month period; higher in women.
- **Common comorbidities:** Agoraphobia, generalized anxiety disorder, depression, other anxiety disorders, alcohol use; medically, often presents first to emergency care as a suspected cardiac event.
- **Differential:** Panic *attacks* occur across many disorders; panic *disorder* requires the unexpected attacks plus the persistent dread/avoidance. Distinguished from medical causes (cardiac, thyroid, asthma) which must be ruled out, and from situationally-bound fear (specific phobia, social anxiety) where the trigger is identifiable.

## Sensory / Behavioral Impact
Between attacks the character is interoceptively hyper-aware — tuned to their own heartbeat, breath, and any flicker of dizziness, because those are the early signs they've learned to fear. A normal bodily sensation gets read as the onset and can prime the very attack it's scanning for. During an attack, perception narrows to a tunnel: the body floods, the room tilts or goes unreal, time both races and stalls. Afterward comes a wrung-out exhaustion and shame. Behaviorally, life quietly bends around exits, proximity to help, and any place where "if it happened, I couldn't get out."

## Duration
Episodic at the level of attacks — minutes-long, self-limiting peaks — laid over a chronic baseline of anticipatory dread. Within a scene an attack can begin from a calm moment or from a triggering cue (a racing heart from exertion, a stuffy room, a memory of the last one) and resolve within roughly ten to thirty minutes, leaving a long tail of depletion. The baseline worry persists between scenes.

## Presentation & Tells
- Checks pulse, presses a hand to the chest, asks "do I look okay?" — covert interoceptive monitoring.
- Sudden pallor or flush, visible trembling, gasping or over-breathing, gripping a chair or doorframe.
- Bolts for the exit, the bathroom, fresh air, or "just needs a minute outside."
- Sits near doors, scopes the room for the way out on arrival, dislikes the middle of a crowded row.
- Carries water, mints, a phone held close, sometimes medication — talismans against the next one.
- Cancels at the last minute; declines plans that can't be left easily.
- After: apologetic, drained, embarrassed, eager to wave it off and not be made a fuss over.

## Interiority / POV Voice
It arrives like a switch thrown by someone else. One second ordinary, the next the heart slams and the floor of the self drops out — *this is it, this is the one that's real, this is my body finally doing the thing.* The mind splits: a screaming certainty of catastrophe running alongside a thin, appalled spectator who knows it's "probably just panic" and cannot make the body believe it. The room goes glassy and far away; their own hands look borrowed. There's no arguing with it in the moment — reason is a voice shouting from the far end of a tunnel. What the narrating mind does *between* attacks is the quieter horror: it listens. To the pulse, the breath, the lightheaded half-second on standing — cataloguing the body for betrayal, because the last attack came from nowhere and so the next one could be hiding in any heartbeat. They build a private map of the world rated by escapability, and they hate that they do it, and they do it anyway.

## Dramatic Affordances
The attack is inherently cinematic — a body in mutiny, terror with no visible cause, the gap between how fine the moment looked and how it felt. But the deeper drama is the shrinking: the slow surrender of places, plans, and freedoms to the dread, and the shame of explaining why you can't just go. Strong scenes play the witness's response — the friend who stays and breathes with them versus the one who says "you're fine, it's nothing." Relationships strain on cancellations and detours; trust is built by someone who learns the difference between rescuing and steadying. Arcs bend toward turning back toward the feared sensations rather than away — the hard, unglamorous work of letting the body finish the alarm without obeying it.

## Fences — Responsible Use
Real people live with panic disorder; this card renders a character, not a verdict. A panic attack is not "drama," "attention-seeking," or weakness — it is a misfiring alarm in a system built to keep you alive, and the person experiencing it is genuinely convinced they may be dying. Do not stage it as someone "freaking out" who should "calm down"; "calm down" is noise to a brain in full alarm. Avoid the trope of panic as comic flailing. Don't conflate it with simple stress. No operational detail on harmful coping or self-medication. Render the terror as real *and* the catastrophe as not-coming — both truths at once.

## Interaction Notes
The signature pairing is with **psyche-agoraphobia** — repeated attacks teach avoidance of any setting where escape feels impossible or help unavailable, until the world contracts. With **psyche-generalized-anxiety-disorder**, the diffuse background worry gains a sharp recurring peak, and "fear of the next attack" becomes one more domain of chronic dread. **psyche-social-anxiety-disorder** adds the horror of attacking *in front of people.* Comorbid **psyche-major-depressive-disorder** deepens the post-attack shame and the sense that the shrinking world will never widen. Against environmental condition cards, enclosed, crowded, hot, or hard-to-exit settings (packed trains, theaters mid-row, bridges, queues, MRI tubes) are amplifiers; open air, a clear exit, and a calm companion are dampeners.
