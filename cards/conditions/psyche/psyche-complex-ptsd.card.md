# Complex PTSD — Psyche Profile

## Description
A psyche-profile condition card. It colors a *character's* behavior, perception, and interiority — not a location. Complex PTSD is what trauma becomes when it is prolonged, repeated, and inescapable — usually beginning in childhood or in captivity: the core PTSD injury plus a deep wound to how the person regulates emotion, sees themselves, and relates to others.

## Clinical Profile
- **Taxonomy:** ICD-11 — Disorders specifically associated with stress (6B41). **Not separately coded in DSM-5-TR**, which folds much of this picture into PTSD plus its dissociative subtype and associated features; the C-PTSD construct is the WHO's, and clinicians citing DSM render it as severe/chronic PTSD with comorbid features.
- **Core features:** the three PTSD clusters (re-experiencing, avoidance, sense of threat) PLUS three "disturbances in self-organization":
  - **Affect dysregulation:** emotional reactions that are too big and too fast, or a shutdown to numbness; difficulty calming once activated.
  - **Negative self-concept:** persistent beliefs of being worthless, defeated, contaminated, or fundamentally bad; pervasive shame and guilt.
  - **Disturbed relationships:** difficulty feeling close to others, avoidance of relationships, or chaotic cycles of closeness and distance.
- **Onset & course:** typically follows sustained trauma — childhood abuse/neglect, domestic violence, trafficking, captivity, war. Chronic by nature; symptoms are woven into identity, which makes them feel like "just who I am" rather than an injury.
- **Prevalence:** approximate community estimates around 1–4%; substantially higher in populations exposed to chronic interpersonal trauma.
- **Common comorbidities:** depression (psyche-major-depressive-disorder), dissociative disorders (psyche-dissociative-identity-disorder), substance use, somatic complaints; symptom overlap with borderline personality disorder (psyche-borderline-pd) is significant.
- **Differential:** from PTSD (psyche-ptsd) by the added self-organization disturbances and the prolonged trauma history. From borderline PD (psyche-borderline-pd) — both involve affect dysregulation and relational instability, but C-PTSD's self-concept is stably negative (defeated) rather than unstable, and abandonment terror and identity diffusion are less central.

## Sensory / Behavioral Impact
Baseline arousal sits high but the more constant texture is internal: a running weather system of shame and self-monitoring. The character reads other people's moods with hyper-accuracy — a survival skill from an environment where another's mood meant safety or harm — and adjusts themselves to manage it. Closeness registers as both craving and threat. Emotion, when it breaks the dam, breaks hard; recovery from it is slow. Dissociation — going flat, foggy, far away — is a frequent off-ramp when feeling becomes too much.

## Duration
Chronic and identity-deep, but the *surface* fluctuates within a scene. A perceived rejection, a raised voice, a moment of unexpected kindness can flip the character from competent to flooded — or from open to walled — in a breath. Co-regulation by a trusted other can settle them; a threat to the relationship or a shame trigger can collapse them inward. Unlike single-event PTSD, the trigger here is often *relational* rather than sensory.

## Presentation & Tells
- Apologizes reflexively; takes blame fast, even for things outside their control.
- Reads the room obsessively; mirrors the dominant person's mood.
- Oscillates between over-accommodation and sudden, defensive withdrawal.
- Deflects warmth or praise — can't quite let it land.
- Goes flat and far-away under stress (dissociative off-ramp).
- Disproportionate reaction to criticism or perceived abandonment, then shame at the reaction.
- Speaks of themselves with a casual, settled contempt that startles others.

## Interiority / POV Voice
The negative self-concept is not a mood; it is the operating system. The character does not feel bad about a mistake — they feel that the mistake *confirms* something already known about them. Other people are weather to be managed: the inner voice is forever calculating what they want, what will set them off, how to be smaller or more useful. Closeness is the thing most wanted and least trusted; when someone gets near, the alarm and the longing fire together and the character can't always tell which one is steering. There is a deep, wordless conviction of being unsalvageable — *if they really knew me* — that no amount of evidence dislodges, because the evidence was laid down before there were words. And there is the going-away: when the inside gets too loud, the lights dim, the body keeps moving, and the character watches their own life from a few feet back.

## Dramatic Affordances
This character will dismantle their own happiness to stay safe — the deepest, quietest drama in fiction. They reach for invisibility, for usefulness, for control of how others feel, none of which anyone else in the scene would think to reach for. The arc that matters is the slow, terrifying work of letting someone close enough to contradict the verdict — and the way they sabotage exactly that. They make devastatingly loyal allies and partners who flinch from the thing they most want. The quiet truth lives in the moment kindness arrives and they don't know how to be held; the high drama lives in the relationship where the wound and the cure are the same person.

## Fences — Responsible Use
C-PTSD is an injury from sustained harm, not a character defect, and not "drama" or "manipulation" — the over-accommodation and the withdrawal are both survival learned under threat. Do not collapse it into "damaged goods" or use the abuse history as flavor for a love-interest's tragic backstory. Be careful with the borderline overlap (psyche-borderline-pd): rendering a C-PTSD character as "crazy ex" repeats the exact stigma both diagnoses suffer. **People with C-PTSD are far more often the harmed than the harmful.** Never depict the originating abuse in operational detail. Real people carry this and build whole lives around it; the card renders a character, not a verdict.

## Interaction Notes
Stacks with depression (psyche-major-depressive-disorder) — the defeated self-concept and hopelessness reinforce each other. Pairs with dissociative disorders (psyche-dissociative-identity-disorder) where chronic childhood trauma produced structural dissociation; the flat far-away off-ramp can deepen into amnesia or parts. Read alongside PTSD (psyche-ptsd) for the shared intrusion/avoidance core. With substance use, the substance is often the only reliable affect regulator the character has found. Environmental condition cards that supply *relational* pressure — a domineering authority, an unstable household, an audience to be managed — drive the disturbance far harder than physical-danger cues do.
