# Avoidant/Restrictive Food Intake Disorder (ARFID) — Psyche Profile

## Description
A psyche-profile condition card that colors a *character's* behavior, perception, and interiority — not a location. ARFID is an eating disorder of avoidance or restriction driven by *something other than* weight or shape concern — by sensory aversion, by lack of interest in eating, or by fear of a feeding-related harm like choking — leading to nutritional shortfall, weight effects, dependence on supplements, or significant disruption to daily and social life.

## Clinical Profile
- **Taxonomy:** DSM-5-TR — Feeding and Eating Disorders. ICD-11 — Feeding or eating disorders, code grouping 6B83.
- **Core features:**
  - An eating or feeding disturbance — avoidance or restriction — that fails to meet nutritional and/or energy needs.
  - Driven by one or more of: sensory sensitivity (texture, smell, taste, appearance, temperature), apparent low interest in eating or low appetite, or fear of an aversive consequence (choking, vomiting, pain).
  - Results in at least one of: notable weight effect or faltering growth, nutritional deficiency, dependence on supplements or tube feeding, or marked interference with psychosocial functioning.
  - Critically, the avoidance is NOT motivated by body-image, weight, or shape concern — this is the defining contrast with anorexia and bulimia.
  - Not better explained by lack of available food or by a culturally sanctioned practice.
- **Onset & course:** Often begins in infancy or childhood and can persist into adolescence and adulthood; can also arise after a frightening feeding event (a choke, an illness) at any age. Course is variable — some narrow further over time, some broaden with support.
- **Prevalence:** Estimates vary; roughly low single-digit percentages depending on population, notably elevated among autistic people and in children with feeding difficulty.
- **Common comorbidities:** `psyche-autism-spectrum`, anxiety disorders (`psyche-generalized-anxiety-disorder`, `psyche-specific-phobia`), `psyche-ocd`, and gastrointestinal or sensory-processing conditions.
- **Differential:** Distinguished from `psyche-anorexia-nervosa` by the *absence* of weight/shape-driven restriction and body-image disturbance; from ordinary picky eating by the severity and the functional or nutritional impact; from a primary medical condition by the disturbance persisting beyond what the medical issue explains.

## Sensory / Behavioral Impact
The character's relationship to food runs through the senses and the nervous system, not the mirror. A wrong texture is not a preference but a full-body recoil — gag, flinch, shutdown. Smell and appearance can close a meal before it starts. For the low-interest presentation, hunger signals are faint or absent, and eating is a chore the body never demands. For the fear presentation, the throat tightens at the thought of the feared food and the whole act feels dangerous. Energy and concentration suffer when intake is low. Social meals — where the menu is out of the character's control — are a source of dread.

## Duration
Often chronic and stable, with a fixed "safe" repertoire that can persist for years; can narrow under stress and broaden with patient, low-pressure exposure. Within a scene the character is composed around safe foods and acutely stressed when confronted with an unsafe one, an unfamiliar setting, or pressure to "just try it." A single bad experience can foreclose a whole food in an instant.

## Presentation & Tells
- A small, fixed set of "safe" foods, often brand- or preparation-specific; visible distress when these aren't available.
- Eating the same things, prepared the same way; foods not touching; rejecting anything altered or unfamiliar.
- Inspecting, smelling, or poking food; cutting tiny test bites; long hesitation; gagging or genuine nausea at textures.
- Quiet dread before social meals; eating beforehand; ordering the one reliable item; "I already ate."
- Slow, effortful eating in the low-interest pattern — forgetting to eat, finishing little, no hunger cues.
- Anxiety, not vanity, at the center — distress about *eating*, never about how the body looks.
- Frustration at being called "fussy" or "difficult" when the experience is involuntary.

## Interiority / POV Voice
People think it is about being difficult. It is not. The fork comes up and the body knows — before any decision, before thought — that this thing is wrong, and the wrongness is not an opinion, it is a fact the throat already has its hand on. To the character, the world divides cleanly into food and not-food, and most of what others call food is firmly the second thing: a slick texture that means *no*, a smell that means *no*, an appearance that the stomach has already voted against. The safe foods are not a limitation; they are a refuge, the small reliable territory where eating is possible at all. Outside that territory every meal is a negotiation with a body that does not negotiate. For the low-interest version it is quieter — eating simply never announces itself, the body forgets, and food is a task someone else thinks is urgent. For the fear version the whole act is shadowed by the memory of the time it went wrong, and the throat tightens around the possibility every single time. What the character refuses to look at is how much room the avoidance has taken — how the world has narrowed to fit it — because the alternative is the terror of the wrong texture, and that terror is real, and it is theirs, and being told it is silly does not make it smaller.

## Dramatic Affordances
This condition gives a character a fault line that runs straight through the most ordinary social ritual there is — the shared meal — and a private, sensory world no one else can feel from the outside. The drama lives in being misread: dismissed as fussy, pressured to "grow out of it," shamed at a table where the rules of hospitality and the rules of the character's body collide. It opens scenes of small bravery (the first new food in years), of an ally learning to cook within the safe territory, of a relationship tested by a restaurant. For autistic characters it braids into a larger sensory life. The quiet truth lives in being believed — someone who stops pushing and simply makes the safe thing without comment. Arcs run on slow, low-pressure widening of the world, never a dramatic cure.

## Fences — Responsible Use
Real people live with this; the card renders a character, not a verdict. ARFID is NOT anorexia in disguise — the restriction has nothing to do with weight, shape, or body image, and writing it as secret weight-control is a category error that erases the disorder. Refuse the "just picky," "spoiled," or "needs discipline" framings — the aversion is involuntary and somatic, not willful, and pressure tends to narrow the repertoire further, not widen it. Do not play gagging or food-refusal for comedy at the character's expense. Avoid the parent-blaming trope. Do NOT include nutritional figures or any restriction described as method. Progress is incremental and patience-driven; do not stage a single forced bite as a breakthrough.

## Interaction Notes
Stacks most often with `psyche-autism-spectrum` (sensory-processing overlap is central) and with anxiety conditions — `psyche-generalized-anxiety-disorder` broadens the dread, `psyche-specific-phobia` sharpens the choking/vomiting-fear presentation, and `psyche-ocd` can layer ritual onto the safe-food rules. Unlike the other eating disorders it does NOT carry body-image disturbance, so it should never be stacked as a variant of `psyche-anorexia-nervosa`. In environmental conditions of scarcity, travel, or imposed communal eating the character's safe territory collapses and the strain becomes acute — a strong, humane source of conflict.
