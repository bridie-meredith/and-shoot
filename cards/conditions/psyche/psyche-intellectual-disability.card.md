# Intellectual Developmental Disorder (Intellectual Disability) — Psyche Profile

## Description
This is a psyche-profile condition card: it colors a *character's* behavior, perception, and interiority (not a location). Intellectual disability is a neurodevelopmental condition of below-average general intellectual functioning together with limits in everyday adaptive skills, beginning in the developmental period — a slower, more concrete, more support-dependent way of meeting the world, present across a wide range of severity.

## Clinical Profile
- **Taxonomy:** DSM-5-TR — Neurodevelopmental Disorders → Intellectual Developmental Disorder (Intellectual Disability), severity graded mild / moderate / severe / profound by adaptive functioning. ICD-11 — 6A00 Disorders of intellectual development.
- **Core features:**
  - Deficits in intellectual functions — reasoning, problem-solving, abstract thinking, planning, judgment, learning from experience.
  - Deficits in adaptive functioning across conceptual (language, reading, money, time), social (relationships, communication, social judgment), and practical (self-care, work, money management, safety) domains.
  - Onset during the developmental period.
  - Severity defined by needed level of support, not by IQ number alone (mild requires occasional support; profound requires pervasive support).
  - Concrete rather than abstract reasoning; literal understanding; slower acquisition and generalization of skills.
  - Wide individual variation in communication, independence, and capability.
- **Onset & course:** Developmental period (childhood); lifelong and stable in nature, though adaptive skills can grow substantially with support, teaching, and accommodation. Not progressive (distinct from dementia, which is a *loss* of established function).
- **Prevalence:** Approximately 1% of the population; mild forms are the large majority.
- **Common comorbidities:** Autism spectrum (psyche-autism-spectrum), ADHD (psyche-adhd), epilepsy, anxiety, depression, specific genetic syndromes; mental-health conditions are common and often under-recognized.
- **Differential:** Distinguished from specific learning disorder (psyche-specific-learning-disorder, which spares general intelligence and affects a specific academic domain), from autism (a different core, though they co-occur), and from acquired neurocognitive decline (which is a loss of previously-held function, not a developmental difference).

## Sensory / Behavioral Impact
The character meets the world concretely — the visible, the literal, the here-and-now are accessible; abstraction, hypotheticals, irony, and long chains of inference are harder or out of reach depending on severity. New skills come slower and need more repetition, and don't always transfer from one setting to another. Decisions and consequences a step or two ahead can be hard to hold. Time, money, and reading may be areas of real difficulty. Many are warm, socially motivated, emotionally present, and quick to trust — which is a vulnerability as much as a grace. Pace, patience, plain language, and concrete examples change everything about how the character functions in a scene.

## Duration
Lifelong and developmental — stable in nature, not episodic and not progressive. Within a scene the character's functioning shifts with task complexity, familiarity, fatigue, emotional load, and how the people around them communicate: clear plain speech and a known routine bring competence; fast abstract talk, pressure, or condescension bring confusion, distress, or shutdown. Skill grows over time with teaching and support.

## Presentation & Tells
- Concrete, literal understanding; misses sarcasm, idiom, or implied meaning; answers the surface question.
- Slower processing; asks for repetition, needs time, "wait — say it again?"
- Simpler vocabulary and sentence structure; reaches for the plain word.
- Difficulty with money, time, reading, or multi-step instructions; relies on routines and helpers.
- Warm, open, socially eager; trusting, sometimes too readily; sensitive to tone and to being talked down to.
- Visible frustration or distress when overwhelmed, rushed, or shamed; may withdraw or escalate.
- Depends on familiar people, scripts, and structures for hard tasks.
- Real, specific competence in practiced domains — a job, a craft, a route walked daily — done well and proudly.

## Interiority / POV Voice
From inside, the world is concrete and immediate and mostly legible — it is the abstract layer that thins out: the joke that everyone else laughed at, the instruction with three steps that blurred after the first, the form whose words rearrange into nonsense. The narrator knows when they've been left behind in a conversation and often hides it, having learned that asking again invites the look — the slowed, loud, sing-song voice that means *they think I'm stupid*, which stings worse than not understanding. There is pride, real and earned, in the things done well: the task mastered by repetition, the kindness given freely, the route known by heart, the person cared for. Wanting is simple and strong — to be liked, to be useful, to be treated as a grown person, to not be the one always needing help. The inner voice is plain and direct, feeling-forward, present-tense; it doesn't editorialize much because the editorial layer is exactly the layer that runs thin. Underneath runs a sediment of being underestimated, decided-for, talked-over, and protected past the point of dignity — and a quiet, fierce wish to be trusted with one's own life.

## Dramatic Affordances
The character offers what the clever people in the scene have lost — directness, present-tense honesty, uncomplicated loyalty, a heart that doesn't hedge. They name the simple true thing the room has overcomplicated. Drama lives in the dignity fight: the right to choose, to work, to love, to be told the truth, against a world that defaults to deciding for them; the caregiver's tension between protection and respect; the vulnerability to exploitation by those who'd take advantage of trust. The quiet truth lives in the gap between capability and the cap others place on it, and in the specific grief of being loved-but-not-respected. Strong material: the moment the character is trusted with something real and rises to it; the ally who speaks *with* them and not *for* them.

## Fences — Responsible Use
Real people live with intellectual disability and deserve full personhood; this card renders a character, never a punchline, a saint, or an inspirational prop. Refuse the "slur" register entirely. Reject both the holy-innocent cliché and the burden cliché — render an actual person with wants, anger, humor, sexuality, and the right to risk. People with intellectual disability are not perpetual children; adults are adults. Intellectual disability is not mental illness and not the same as autism, learning disorder, or dementia, though these can co-occur or be confused. Do not write capability as fixed — adaptive skill grows with support. People with intellectual disability are far more often the targets of abuse and exploitation than perpetrators of harm; if vulnerability is in the scene, render it as theirs to be protected from, not as their failing. NO operational detail that could enable real-world exploitation.

## Interaction Notes
Stacks frequently with psyche-autism-spectrum (overlapping support needs but distinct cores — concrete-cognition vs. social-communication-difference) and with psyche-adhd. Co-occurring anxiety and depression are common and often missed because distress gets attributed to the disability itself ("diagnostic overshadowing") — worth rendering accurately. With psyche-specific-learning-disorder the distinction matters: that condition spares general intelligence. Environmental condition cards are decisive: fast, abstract, high-literacy, time-pressured settings disable the character; plain-language, patient, structured, respectful settings reveal real competence. The same person looks profoundly different in a courtroom and in a kitchen they know.
