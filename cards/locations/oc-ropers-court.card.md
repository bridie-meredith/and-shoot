# Roper's Court and Tributary Lanes — Rope-Making Ward, Third Coverage Expansion

## Geography

A craft-trade ward in Flea Bottom, adjacent to one of the other three coverage wards (the stitch-house lane ward or Pig Tallow Lane network). The ward takes its name from the dominant trade: rope-making, which is a specialist smallfolk industry in a port-adjacent city. King's Landing's proximity to the Blackwater Rush and Blackwater Bay creates sustained demand for cordage — rigging rope, mooring lines, cargo ties — and a ward that specializes in it occupies a stable if low-prestige economic niche.

Rope-making requires space and linear geometry: the process of laying hemp fibers into twisted rope requires a rope-walk — a long clear lane down which the spinner can walk backward while twisting. Roper's Court's tributary lanes are wider-spaced and longer than typical Flea Bottom alleys as a result, or the residents have cleared specific lanes for this purpose. The ward has a physical texture shaped by its trade: long sight lines in the tributary lanes (for the rope-walk), fiber and tar residue underfoot, hempen smell.

The court itself is a central open space — larger than the connecting-alley courts of Pig Tallow Lane, necessarily, since rope-makers use a central drying and coiling area. The tributary lanes radiate off the court at multiple angles, each a potential rope-walk in the making.

## Layout

**Roper's Court (central space):** The defining feature — an irregular open area where the tributary lanes converge. Used for rope coiling, drying finished line, and the social functions a Flea Bottom court serves: informal meeting, children's movement, weather shelter (when the buildings' overhang catches). The court is not large by outside-Flea-Bottom standards; it is large relative to its neighborhood, which makes it visible from multiple tributary lane-mouths simultaneously.

**Tributary lanes:** Multiple lanes branch off the court, narrowing as they extend. The near tributaries (opening directly off the court) are wider — wide enough for a rope-walk where two people can pass the twisting spindle between them. The far tributaries, at the ends of the branching network, narrow to standard Flea Bottom single-file width. Taylor's observation (b01c04s03) places the far tributary lane at the point where foot traffic in the early morning thins to single-file — this is the end of the accessible rope-walk geometry, where the lane narrows enough that the ward's function gives way to pure residential cramming.

**Fiber-work stations:** Positioned at intervals along the wider tributaries — not permanent structures but recurring workspace: anchor posts driven into the lane walls, fiber-bundles piled at one end, finished coil-points at the other. These are not card-level fixed props but they are consistent environmental features.

**Court perimeter buildings:** The structures surrounding the court are a mix of rope-making workshops on the ground floor and residential tenements above. The workshop faces (ground floor) are open during working hours — wide doorways through which finished rope is carried. At night, shuttered.

## Sensory Vocabulary

**Smell:** Hemp is the dominant note — raw fiber has a green-vegetable smell when fresh, a dry dusty smell when aged. The tar used for waterproofing certain rope grades (rigging rope particularly) adds a sharp resinous overlay that concentrates in the tributaries where waterproofing work is done. The hemp-and-tar combination is distinctive and specific to rope-making work; it does not appear in the Hook, Pig Tallow Lane, or the stitch-house ward at the same intensity. A character who has been in Roper's Court carries the smell on their clothing.

**Sound:** In working hours: the rhythmic creak of rope-twist — the spindle turning produces a characteristic sound as fibers resist, a low rhythmic creak-and-drag audible the length of a tributary lane. Conversation in the tributary lanes is possible but carries (the geometry of a long lane transmits sound well). In the early morning grey (Taylor's preferred time for coverage here): near-silence. The rope-walk requires daylight to do accurately; work does not begin before first light. Before the ward wakes, the court is quiet enough that a person walking in it can hear footsteps from two tributary lanes over.

**Light:** The court's central open space is relatively well-lit during the day — the open geometry catches more sky than the Hook's cramped alleys. The near tributaries have adequate working light; the far tributaries, where the lanes narrow and the overhang of upper tenements increases, are dark by mid-afternoon even in summer. In the early morning grey that Taylor uses: the court is in dull pre-dawn light; the far tributaries are dark enough that movement through them requires familiarity with the lane.

**Texture:** Hemp fiber residue is constant — short fibers shedding from the rope-walk land on the lane surfaces and are tracked through the court by foot traffic. The ground in the rope-walk tributaries is permanently fibrous, a distinct texture from the compacted-earth Flea Bottom standard. Tar spots on the cobbled sections of the wider tributaries. The court's central area is drier than the Hook's alley floors because it catches less drainage from surrounding buildings.

## Fixed Props

None card-level. The rope-walk anchor posts and spindle equipment are ward property and runtime state. Finished rope coils staged for delivery are runtime state.

## Exits

- **Tributary lane network (multiple exits):** The ward's geometry provides multiple exit points — each tributary lane-mouth opens to a different section of the Flea Bottom alley network. This makes the ward structurally different from the cooper's yard (single exit) and more similar to Pig Tallow Lane's multi-exit pattern.
- **Court mouth (primary approach):** The main entrance to the court from the adjacent ward network — the natural approach for someone coming from the Hook, Pig Tallow Lane side, or the stitch-house lane ward.
- **Far tributary lane exits:** The narrow far-end lanes open to the outer Flea Bottom network, connecting eventually to the lanes below the Street of Steel approaches.

**Coverage geometry note:** Taylor walks this ward in early-morning grey specifically because the thin foot traffic allows the cleanest feed-geometry — she can read the ward's full network pattern without the saturation-noise of dense midday bodies. The tributary lanes' linear geometry, combined with low traffic, produces the most precise single-ward insect-feed resolution she achieves across the four-ward expansion.

## Hazards

- **Far tributary darkness:** The narrow far-end tributaries are dark enough in early morning and evening that navigation requires either lamplight (conspicuous) or familiarity. A newcomer moving through at speed risks a twisted ankle on the uneven lane surface.
- **Sound transmission in rope-walk geometry:** The long tributary lanes transmit sound unusually well for Flea Bottom. A conversation at ordinary speaking volume in a tributary lane can be heard from the court entrance. Low-voiced exchanges require shorter distance and more awareness than in the Hook's contorted alleys.
- **Tar hazard in waterproofing areas:** Fresh-applied tar in the waterproofing stretch of a tributary lane is a skin and clothing contact hazard. Older tar spots are merely a grip hazard.
- **Single-file far tributary as containment:** The narrowing far-end lanes that reduce to single-file traffic are the ward's chokepoint. A person in the far tributary lane when the lane-mouth is blocked has no lateral exit — walls on both sides, one direction only.
- **Ward-resident recognition:** The rope-making trade is relatively specialized and the ward's worker population is stable — the same faces at the same stations over years. A stranger who appears on multiple successive days (Taylor walking the ward for coverage extension) may acquire notice faster here than in the more transient Hook.

## Ambient Interruption Hooks

- A rope-walk starting before dawn — an urgent commission pulling workers to the spindle before first light, disrupting the early-morning quiet Taylor uses for clean-feed coverage
- A finished coil delivery staging in the court, blocking the central space with large-diameter rope rolls and the dray-cart maneuvering around them
- A tar-pot accident in a waterproofing tributary — fire risk, quick ward response, the sound and smell change propagating through the lane network
- A child running a fiber-count errand through the court at the wrong hour — witness to a person who is in the ward before its workers have arrived
- The far tributary lane mouth blocked by a collapsed overhanging shelf (common in Flea Bottom's aging structure) — forcing a reroute through a less familiar lane
- Two rope-walk workers disputing a length measurement, the argument loud enough to carry the full tributary lane — covering the sound of another person's passage but also drawing attention to the lane
