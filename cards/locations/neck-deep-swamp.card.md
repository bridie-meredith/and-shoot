# Neck Deep Swamp

## Geography

The interior of the Neck: the vast marshland that compresses Westeros to its narrowest point between the Bite and Blazewater Bay. Not the causeway — the causeway is the only passage ordinary armies use; this is everything else, which is to say virtually all of the territory, and it is impassable by any conventional means. Reed beds twelve feet high, open water that looks like ground, water channels that look like open ground, fog that sits chest-height on still mornings, and lizard-lions. The Neck refuses armies not through walls but through terrain.

The crannogmen know it as home. Everyone else knows it as something to go around, and they are correct to.

The Moat Cailin causeway crosses the edge of this territory but not its interior. The interior belongs to no lord in any meaningful sense. Greywater Watch floats somewhere in it; its location is known only to the crannogmen and moves with the season and the water.

## Layout

- **Surface water channels:** The primary navigation medium. Channels vary from six inches deep and a yard wide to twenty feet deep and wide enough for a flat-bottomed boat. They are not reliably distinguishable from each other from above or in fog. A channel that was crossable by wading last month may be navigable by boat this month.
- **Reed beds:** Phragmites reed, twelve to fifteen feet high at maturity, growing so densely from the waterlogged muck below that a person on the water surface cannot see beyond three yards in any direction. Sound travels; sight does not. A boat moving through reed beds is audible fifty yards away.
- **Open water meres:** Larger bodies of still water where the reed beds open out. Visible sky, visible surroundings for a hundred yards. The meres feel safer; they are not. This is where lizard-lions cross open water.
- **Floating mats:** Vegetation mats — sedge, reed root systems, accumulated organic matter — that have detached from the bed and float across the meres. They can bear the weight of a person for several seconds before giving way. They look exactly like solid ground.
- **Crannogmen platforms:** Where present: artificial islands of staked timber and woven reed, anchored enough to be a fixed address but constructed to be moved. Houses of thatch above water level. The platforms themselves are stable underfoot; the approach is via shallow-draft boat.

## Sensory Vocabulary

- **Sound:** Frogs — constant, layered, species-upon-species from dawn through the night; their pattern changes when something large moves in the water. A bittern booming from somewhere in the reed bed — an enormous low pulse that travels over water. Insects: the specific whine of biting flies in a cloud. Reed warblers and sedge warblers in constant thin song during daylight; abrupt silence across the whole reed bed is the signal for a lizard-lion in motion. Water: everywhere, in motion, but none of it fast — gurgles and quiet laps rather than current. A boat hull on open water at night is surrounded by sounds that require experience to interpret as non-threatening.
- **Smell:** Methane from decomposing vegetation, rising in bubbles when the bottom is disturbed — the smell of a marsh fart: sulfur-sharp and immediate. The overall ambient is rotting plant matter and standing water, an earthy-sour smell that saturates clothing and skin within an hour. Mud: the specific muck of the Neck's bed has its own character, neither river mud nor sea mud — darker, heavier, and more persistent.
- **Light:** Fog in the morning, most mornings, sitting at or above the water level and reducing visibility in the channels to a boat-length. Burns off by mid-morning in summer; persists all day in the shoulder seasons. Above the reed bed height: open sky, often bright. Below: green-filtered where the reeds thin enough to let light down; dark where they don't.
- **Temperature:** Warm and wet in summer — the mire holds heat and humidity. Cold and wet in winter, with ice forming on the surface water at the edges but not in the deeper channels. The cold and damp combination in winter is more punishing than the equivalent temperature in open country.
- **Insects:** The biting flies operate in clouds from late spring through early autumn. A person without covering is bleeding from a dozen sites within minutes of entering the mire. The crannogmen use plant-based repellents; outsiders do not know the plants.

## Fixed Props

None catalogued. Crannogmen platforms move; what counts as a fixed prop on them is tracked at the project level.

## Exits

- **The causeway approach:** From the Neck's interior, the Moat Cailin causeway is audible before visible on still days — cart traffic, boots on stone. The channels near the causeway are shallower and the reed beds thinner; the transition from swamp to road takes several hours of careful navigation.
- **North (into the North proper):** Where the marsh gradually gives way to firmer ground as the land rises. The transition zone is still treacherous for a long distance; the first solid ground underfoot after hours in the mire is an experience.
- **South (Riverlands border):** The southern edge of the Neck is less clearly defined than maps suggest. The marsh thins into boggy ground, then into pasture that floods seasonally.
- **By water:** A flat-bottomed boat with someone who knows the channels can cross the Neck in two days in good conditions. The same journey on foot has never been reliably accomplished by an outsider.

## Hazards

- **Lizard-lions:** Ambush predators of the crocodilian type. Lie submerged at the channel margins, visible only as a slightly irregular shadow. Aggressive in spring nesting season. The sudden event — a boat struck from below, a person at a channel edge who is not there a moment later — is the characteristic form of the encounter. Survival after contact is rare and almost always involves crannogmen assistance within minutes.
- **Floating mats:** Weight-bearing until they are not. A person confident in the surface is the person in trouble.
- **Channel navigation failure:** In fog or at night, a channel turns. The person or boat following a channel that was going north is now going west. The reed beds provide no reference point. This is not immediately dangerous and then it is.
- **Fever:** The miasmic association with marshland is experiential fact in the Neck. Outsiders in the mire for more than a week develop fevers with some regularity. The crannogmen are largely immune; they manage the illness in outsiders with plants whose identity they do not share.
- **The insects:** See Sensory section. Blood loss from biting flies over several days is not fatal but it is incapacitating. Eyes and lips swell. Sleep becomes difficult. Outsiders consistently underestimate how much this matters.

## Ambient Interruption Hooks

- Simultaneous frog silence across the reed bed — something large is moving in the water; the direction is unclear
- A crannogman boat, appearing from a reed channel at three yards, having been moving for minutes without audible approach
- The fog lifting mid-morning to reveal that the landmarks used to navigate the last hour are not visible from this position
- A methane bubble rising and popping near the boat — the smell is immediate; the mud below is disturbed by whatever is moving under the surface
- Dragonfly in impossible number — thousands, moving in a coordinated pattern that has no natural explanation
- The reed bed ahead, which appeared open, turning out to be a floating mat of sedge over open water; the boat's bow goes over the edge before the difference is clear
