# The Fairstead River Dock and Ferry Crossing

## Geography

The northern terminus of Fairstead's functional geography: the dock where the Blue Fork tributary is wide enough and deep enough to be crossed by a flat-bottom ferry. The dock sits approximately 200 paces north of the market square, connected by the dock road (the main north-south artery). The tributary at this point is approximately 30 paces across — too wide to ford on foot in most seasons, crossable by a shallow draft boat in all seasons. The flat-bottom ferry is the only reliable crossing for travelers and goods.

The ferryman's cottage is on the east bank (Fairstead side), approximately 10 paces from the dock edge. The west bank has a wooden post and a bell — the signal for requesting the ferry from the far side. The ferry itself is a flat-bottomed wooden platform approximately 6 paces long by 3 paces wide, propelled by poles and a rope guide stretched across the tributary at the crossing point. It can carry approximately four adults, two loaded pack animals, or one small cart.

The dock is the town's throat: everything that enters or leaves Fairstead by water passes through here. The garrison posts a man here on census and collection days; otherwise the ferryman (Tomm Weyl, the third Weyl to hold the position in living memory) manages the crossing traffic independently.

## Layout

**East bank (Fairstead side):**
- The dock itself — a wooden platform extending 4 paces into the water, wide enough for loading and unloading. The planking is old but sound; Tomm maintains it with obsessive regularity because a failed dock is a failed livelihood.
- The ferryman's cottage — a single-room structure, the smallest habitable space in Fairstead, with a loft for sleeping. Tomm spends most of his daylight hours outside it, watching the crossing. He knows everyone who passes. This is not a character trait; it is a job requirement.
- The livestock transit pen — a small wooden-rail pen at the dock's north end, for holding animals brought to cross. Animals crossing on the ferry must be managed carefully; the pen allows sorting and preparation before boarding. The pen is the dock's primary insect-density source.
- The rope anchor — a heavy iron ring set into the dock platform, where the guide rope is secured on the Fairstead side. The rope runs across the tributary to a matching anchor on the west bank. Tomm adjusts rope tension seasonally.
- A small storage shed — ropes, poles, dock tools, tarred cloth for covering cargo in rain.

**West bank (far side):**
- A cleared landing area, maintained by Tomm when he makes the crossing.
- The bell post — a wooden post with a iron bell, for signaling a crossing request from travelers approaching from the west.
- An unpaved road connecting to the west-road toward Pinkmaiden territory.

**The tributary itself:**
- At dock width, the tributary runs approximately 1.2 m deep in dry season, up to 1.8 m in spring flood. Not dangerous for a healthy adult who slips in; dangerous for a child, especially in spring.
- Visibility into the water: moderate. The Blue Fork tributaries carry silt; the water is not clear. Water beetles and waterbugs are visible on the surface in calm weather.

## Sensory Vocabulary

**Smell:** River mud, dominant. The specific combination of wet silt, decomposing vegetation from the riverside plants, and the damp wood of the dock and ferry. Fish if Tomm has been fishing from the dock (he does). The livestock transit pen adds its note on days when animals are being crossed.

**Sound:** The tributary current — a constant low sound, varying by season (louder in spring flood, quieter in late summer). The rope guide's tension creak as the ferry crosses. Tomm's pole against the riverbed when the ferry needs help in low-water season. The dock planking under foot. Birds along the riverbank — herons, kingfishers, the usual riparian population. From upstream or down: occasional boat traffic on the broader water system.

**Touch:** Dock planking, slightly wet even in dry weather (river mist in early morning). The rope guide, rough and tarred. The ferry platform underfoot — stable when loaded correctly, listing slightly if passengers crowd one side. Cold off the water in any season; river air carries less warmth than land air.

**Light:** Open to the sky along the tributary; the banks are lined with willows and alder that provide some shade on the approach but clear at the dock itself. The dock has no artificial light; it does not function after dark except in emergencies.

**Insect ecology (Taylor-relevant):** The dock is Taylor's third-richest terrain after the dye-yard and the market square animal pens. Water beetles and waterbugs on the tributary's calm sections — these are species she has taken longer to calibrate than the terrestrial insects, and her behavioral models for aquatic insects are less accurate than for the fly populations she grew up with in Brockton Bay (which were different species). Mosquitoes in the marshy near-bank areas north and south of the dock (abundant in warm seasons; limited in winter). Horseflies at the livestock transit pen — high sting potential, medium controllability. Dragonflies along the water's edge (limited control; high autonomous mobility makes them difficult to direct). The riverside's edge vegetation holds additional beetle populations she has not fully mapped.

## Fixed Props

- Dock platform (wooden, fixed iron rope anchor)
- Ferry rope guide (crosses tributary; Tomm manages tension)
- Livestock transit pen (small wooden-rail enclosure)
- Storage shed (dock tools, tarred cloth)
- Bell post with iron bell (west bank)
- Ferryman's cottage (east bank)

## Exits

- **Ferry crossing to west bank** — requires Tomm's cooperation or theft of the ferry; the crossing itself takes approximately 8 minutes one-way
- **Dock road south** — 200 paces to the market square; the main Fairstead artery
- **River north by boat** — to the upper tributary villages; seasonal accessibility depending on water level
- **River south by ferry** — downstream toward the Blue Fork circuit; the main commercial corridor

## Hazards

- **Tomm Weyl as the information node:** Tomm knows every person who crosses. He knows when they came, what they were carrying, who they were with. He is not the lord's man — he does not report to the garrison — but he talks. Information from the dock travels through Fairstead's social network within hours. He is the first person who knows when a new institutional visitor arrives; he is often the first person Mira Stonefield's network hears from.
- **Garrison posting during census and collection:** During official collection periods, a garrison man is posted at the dock. He checks papers (for those who have papers) and asks the nature of business (for those who do not). He is the institutional monitoring of transit. Anyone Taylor wants to move through the dock during these periods has to pass this man.
- **The ferry itself as chokepoint:** There is no other crossing of the tributary within a day's walk in either direction. If Tomm is not at the dock, or if the ferry is secured on the wrong bank, the crossing is blocked. This is a vulnerability Taylor needs to account for in any contingency where she needs to move people quickly.
- **Visibility at the dock:** The dock is open to the water and the opposite bank. Anyone crossing can be seen from the far bank during the crossing. Anyone watching from the dock road can see who is boarding. The livestock pen area provides some visual cover but not for the loading area.

## Ambient Interruption Hooks

- A new traveler arrives from south of the Blue Fork. Tomm helps them off the ferry and asks the usual questions (where from, where to, what trade). The traveler's answers — or non-answers — travel to Mira Stonefield's network within two hours if the information is interesting.
- The garrison man is posted at the dock for a collection cycle. He is conducting the routine check. A smallfolk man who has been at the dock for legitimate reasons — perhaps fetching goods from the south bank landing — has to account for himself. The accounting is routine. Taylor's passive sense gives her the dock's layout before she arrives.
- Tomm is fishing from the dock edge, pole in the water. He is watching the far bank. He has been watching the far bank for twenty years. He sees the approach before anyone else in Fairstead does. He is the town's earliest warning system for arrivals from the south.
- A boat passes on the tributary — not the ferry, but a passage boat moving goods upstream or down. Tomm hails it; the boatman might stop or might not. If they stop, whatever they carry and whatever they know travels into Fairstead's information network through Tomm.
- The ferry rope guide needs attention — a high-water event has shifted the west anchor slightly. Tomm spends a morning re-tensioning. He is not at the dock during this work; he is visible from the dock, working in the tributary's shallows. A crossing request during this window cannot be accommodated until he finishes.

## Interaction Notes

**For Taylor:** The dock is an information node she cannot fully control because it runs through Tomm, who is not in her coalition and is not the kind of person who keeps things quiet on request. Her passive surveillance of the dock (mosquito-adjacent insects; waterbugs) gives her positional data but not the conversations that happen at the dock's platform level. She knows who arrives; she does not know what they say to Tomm.

**The ferryman as structural character:** Tomm Weyl is a carry-forward character (not in the starter cast); this card should not be read as establishing him fully. He is referenced here as a structural element of the location. His character, when authored, will carry the load of his function — the information node that Taylor cannot directly access.

**Census and collection chokepoint:** This is the function the dock serves in the antagonist-escalation arc. When the steward wants to know who is moving, the dock is where that monitoring is effective. A garrison posting at the dock creates a transit constraint Taylor has to work around. The ferry chokepoint is both a logistical fact and an institutional lever.

**Insect calibration note:** Taylor's aquatic-insect calibration is lower than her terrestrial calibration. She is aware of this. The dock is familiar enough terrain (she has been here regularly since she could walk to the market with her father) that she has some working models for the mosquito and water-beetle populations, but these models are less reliable than her market-square fly models. She does not use the dock insects for offensive deployment; she uses them for ambient surveillance at moderate confidence.
