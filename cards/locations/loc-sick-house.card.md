# Location — The Sick-House (Charity Apothecary)

A charitable sick-house and apothecary operated by a member of a noble household. The public-facing piety operation: a space for dispensing medicines and nursing the sick that is also, by the nature of its cover, a space that is operationally useful for whoever runs it.

The loophole structure is the location's defining property. A charitable sick-house is an expected noble duty, unsupervised by social norm, morally unimpeachable. Objecting to it requires the objector to appear wicked. This defense belongs to the *space*, not the operator — it is activated by the existence and genuine function of the location, not by any argument made in the moment.

**Cross-reference:** `loc-still-room` is the private back-room laboratory that a sick-house may adjoin or supply — the inner sanctum to the sick-house's outer hall. `loc-red-keep-interior` covers the wider castle context.

---

## Geography

Located in a service wing or secondary portion of a larger institutional complex — adjacent to or accessible from the medical authority's domain (a maester's tower, a physician's chambers), but positioned away from the main household's daily traffic pattern. Patients — servants, smallfolk, household staff and their families — approach from a service passage, not through the formal apartments.

Positioning matters: the sick-house must be accessible to the populations it serves without being in a space that courtiers or household authorities routinely pass through. Its location in a side wing or service corridor is not incidental; it is the geographic version of the loophole logic.

---

## Layout

### The dispensary counter and waiting area

The public-facing space. A long counter where medicines are dispensed, patients are assessed briefly, and the sick-house's official ledger is maintained. The waiting area holds six to ten patients on plain benches. The register is clean and legible; the ledger records patient names, complaints, and compounds dispensed in exactly the format a household administrator would expect to see.

### The nursing ward

Two or three cot-beds behind a simple curtain. For patients too ill to receive medicine and leave. The curtain provides a visual separation from the dispensary; patients waiting at the counter do not see who is in the beds. This is also the space that requires nightwatch — a nursing patient too ill to be left alone requires attendance through the night.

### The supplies alcove

Shelved storage at the back of the dispensary: dried herbs, prepared tinctures, stoppered medicinal jars. The front row of the alcove shows what the ledger records; the back rows may not. The ledger documents what was designed to be seen; what is stored behind or below the visible row belongs to a different accounting system.

### The record desk

The sick-house's official ledger. Written clearly, reviewed by household authorities as evidence of charitable activity. It has never contained a discrepancy between what it records and what an administrator would expect. It was designed to be seen.

## Sensory Vocabulary

**The dispensary register:**
Dried herbs, lavender, elder-flower, imported gum-resin. The faint sweet-sharp of tinctures. Clean linen. Beeswax candles at the counter; a warmer oil lamp over the nursing ward. Cleaner than a working laboratory — the sick-house is maintained at the charitable standard, which means it smells like what it says it is.

**The nursing ward:**
Illness does not fully leave the nursing ward. Under the clean linen and the compound smell, there is sick-sweat and old poultice. This is a place where people have been very ill. The competence visible in the nursing is practiced, not academic — the person tending patients here has done this many times and does not fumble.

**The waiting area:**
Coughing, shuffling, the sound of jars, the rasp of the ledger. A low-voice register — patients in a charity dispensary are quiet, grateful, or sick. The sounds of people waiting in a place they are glad exists.

## Exits

**Primary:** the main entrance from the service passage. Patients arrive and leave this way; it is the known public face of the operation.

**To the medical authority's domain:** an internal connection to adjacent medical spaces — a shared passage, a connecting door. This is how supplies move between a private laboratory and the sick-house's official stock. The connection is known to the medical authority; it may or may not be well-known to the household's security apparatus.

**Back access:** for supplies and materials that need to enter the sick-house without passing through the main service passage under observation. The specific mechanism varies by setting; the function is consistent.

---

## Hazards

**The piety defense and its limits:**
The loophole holds as long as the charity genuinely operates and any attacker has no specific provable charge. Attacking a charitable operation in the abstract looks wicked. Attacking a specific questionable practice under charitable cover does not look wicked — it looks responsible. The defense erodes under specificity.

**The two-ledger structure:**
The sick-house ledger documents the charitable operation as it was designed to appear. Any supplementary supply activity is recorded elsewhere. The hazard is anyone comparing the official ledger against supplementary accounts — a cross-reference of supply volumes, an inquiry into source.

**The patient population as witnesses:**
Patients see what happens at the counter and in the ward. The population — servants, smallfolk — does not have standing to report most of what it observes. The exception is a patient connected to someone with standing, or a patient whose observations reach the wrong person in passing.

**The nursing watch:**
A nursing patient too ill to be left alone requires overnight attendance. The operator's absence from normal sleeping routines during a nursing watch is observable if anyone compares multiple staff accounts. The cover for it (a sickly person's irregular sleep, performed illness, the charitable duty itself) works once; it requires more management after the second or third occurrence.

**The connecting-passage risk:**
If the connection to an adjacent private laboratory is mapped by a sufficiently attentive household figure, the sick-house's cover for the laboratory's supply chain becomes visible. The sick-house's supply needs are legitimate; a laboratory's supply needs are different in character; a connecting passage between the two makes the difference auditable.

## Ambient Interruption Hooks

- **A patient who should not be here:** someone whose presence is not explained by the populations the sick-house officially serves; she needs to know their purpose before they are seen at the counter.
- **The household administrator's welfare inspection:** a comparison of the sick-house ledger against household supply accounts; a cross-reference she has not had to defend in formal review before.
- **A patient's unexpected recovery:** the medical authority is professionally curious about which compound produced a result their own treatment did not; the question comes from interest, not suspicion; the question is still a problem.
- **The overnight watch observed:** a household figure notes the absence from the sleeping routine; asks a maid; the maid confirms a second absence the previous week; the pattern is being assembled without anyone intending to assemble it.
- **A supply delivery at the wrong time:** a delivery through the back access arrived while the connecting passage is being used by someone who should not see it arrive; the transfer needs to be diverted.
- **The record desk inspected under pressure:** an urgent external accusation against the operation — real or fabricated — triggers an immediate ledger review; the review will confirm the ledger is clean; the ledger being clean is not the same as the operation being safe.
