# Northern Highland Moorland

## Geography

Upland plateau north of the Neck and south of the mountain passes, where the land rises out of forest into open country: blanket bog running miles without break, the only vertical features being the distant ridgeline and the occasional tor of dark rock emerging from the peat like something the ground is slowly digesting. The sky here is enormous and frequently hostile — cloud systems move fast across the plateau without tree cover to interrupt them; a clear morning can become a whiteout fog inside an hour.

The surface is deceptive. From a distance: rough grassland, heather, apparently solid. Underfoot: sphagnum moss saturated with water, ground that gives under weight and doesn't return, channels of standing black water that look like ground but are not. The Northmen who live near this country know the lines of safe passage by memory and muscle. Southerners crossing it by sight die badly and slowly.

This terrain is used by no one and avoided by most. The nearest comparable use is the Watchmen's patrol routes between ranger checkpoints — and those men do not cross the moorland, they follow the edges.

## Layout

- **The surface:** Heather (purple in late summer, rust-brown through the long winter, regrowing stems in spring), sphagnum moss in every visible depression, bog cotton marking the wet spots with white tufts. The heather can be walked on; the sphagnum cannot be trusted.
- **Peat hags:** Irregular raised mounds of peat, waist to chest high, formed where erosion has eaten channels between them. The channels between hags are filled with dark water or are simply soft peat with no bottom that will accept a man's leg to the hip without warning. Navigation between hags requires patience and willingness to backtrack.
- **Safe lines:** Drier ground runs in narrow strips between the wet zones, identifiable by the presence of harder rush grass and, in places, a faint path worn by deer and the occasional experienced shepherd. These lines shift with the seasons — a safe line in August may be underwater in April.
- **Open sky:** The plateau has no natural windbreaks. Wind comes from the north or the west, depending on the season, and moves without interruption. In fog, the plateau offers no landmarks; the ground looks the same in every direction.

## Sensory Vocabulary

- **Sound:** Wind, primarily — constant at height, cycling between a low moan through the heather and a sharper whistle where it passes over exposed peat. The curlew call: a descending bubbling cry from a bird that is almost never visible, heard out of apparent nowhere and then silence. Grouse flushing from cover — the sudden explosive wingbeat and their guttural bark. The sound of a boot going through ice-crust on a cold morning: a crack and then silence as the foot sinks. Water, everywhere, audible in small channels even when invisible.
- **Smell:** Peat earth — sweet-sharp, damp, ancient; the smell of a thing that has been dead and partly decomposed for hundreds of years and then stopped at that point. Heather when crushed underfoot. Cold air coming off snow on the northern ridgeline.
- **Light:** Flat in overcast — shadows are absent, the heather goes brown and the peat black and there is no depth to what you see. In low sun: the sphagnum pools reflect light upward, the wet peat gleams, and the heather casts long shadows that obscure the ground texture and make safe route assessment genuinely harder.
- **Temperature:** Exposed. Wind at altitude removes heat faster than temperature alone suggests. A man damp from a bog crossing loses warmth faster than a man standing still. In winter the surface freezes — a deceptive solid crust over liquid bog, weight-rated for nothing useful.
- **Visibility:** Mist comes in off the ridgeline without warning. Full visibility to zero in ten minutes is possible; a quarter-mile visibility that persists for days is more common. Once fog settles on a moor of this type, there is no navigating it by sight.

## Fixed Props

None. The plateau offers no shelter and no constructed features. The occasional cairn, if present, marks a route or a grave from a previous attempt to cross; the difference is not always legible.

## Exits

- **South edge:** Where the plateau drops back into forest and farmland. The treeline is visible on a clear day and invisible in fog; on a clear day, it takes three hours of careful walking to reach it from the plateau center.
- **North edge:** Toward the mountain passes and the country beyond. Colder, rockier. The bog transitions to stone and boulder field above a certain elevation.
- **East and west edges:** Both drop eventually to valleys with watercourses. The watercourses are crossable but the valley approaches require descending through the worst of the hag fields, which collect water along the plateau margins.

## Hazards

- **Bog suck:** A foot placed wrong into saturated sphagnum goes in fast and comes out slowly. At depth a person cannot extract themselves without external leverage. This is not immediate drowning; it is a slow situation that becomes lethal with cold and exhaustion.
- **Fog navigation failure:** A person who loses direction in plateau fog with no compass and no landmark knowledge has a small chance of reaching an edge and a larger chance of walking circles until exposure or bog takes them. Fog on the plateau can last days.
- **Frost crust:** Winter ice on bog ground looks like solid surface. It is not. The crust supports a standing person long enough for confidence and then fails under a second step.
- **Exposure:** Wind and wet at altitude. A man in the wrong clothes stops moving in four to six hours. A man in the right clothes survives; the difference is information that southerners consistently lack.
- **Peat hag falls:** Dropping between hags into a water-filled channel is survivable if you can get purchase on the hag walls; they are friable peat and will not hold a grip.

## Ambient Interruption Hooks

- A curlew calling from somewhere in the heather — the bird is invisible
- Grouse flushing from cover underfoot, the sound and sudden movement startling enough to cause a fall
- A change in wind direction: the mist that was coming from the north arrives from the east instead; the reference point just used to orient is now wrong
- A cairn, encountered in fog, that could be a route marker or a grave marker
- The sound of running water below foot level — a stream in a peat channel, invisible, running under the surface
- Another figure on the plateau, a long way off, moving in a direction that suggests they know a line you don't
