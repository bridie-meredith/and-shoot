# Westeros — Northern Register

Shared regional dialect. Composes with `westeros-grrm-mannerisms` (universal eccentricities), and is overlaid on the class register (`westeros-smallfolk` or `westeros-noble-courtly`). The North's distinctness is structural — Old Tongue traces, old-gods religious register, austere Anglo-Saxon vocabulary, and the felt presence of cold and the Wall.

The Northern register is **not phonetically marked** in the canon prose; GRRM does it through syntax, vocabulary, and tag-line. Phonetic markers (occasional dropped *g*, *aye*) carry over from smallfolk register; do not stack them.

---

## Direct samples

> "Winter is coming."
> — *House Stark words.* Functions as proverb, warning, register-anchor. Used by Northerners in casual reference, not only as House motto. *Winter's coming, that one* — used to describe a child of unusual gravity.

> "There must always be a Stark in Winterfell."
> — *Northern proverb.* Carries the doubled meaning — household duty and the structural-religious sense the old gods supposedly require it.

> "The North remembers."
> — *Universal Northern phrase.* Said after wrong has been done and not yet answered. Said quietly. The reader / listener is meant to hear it as both observation and threat.

> "Aye, m'lord. The cold's not done with us yet."
> — *synthesized.* Smallfolk register surfacing in the North: *aye* preserved, *the cold* spoken of as an actor in its own right. The North personifies cold and winter in casual speech where southron registers do not.

> "He was a true son of the North. He kept his word and his hands clean."
> — *synthesized.* The Northern eulogy register. *True son of the North* is the load-bearing phrase. Praise via simplicity — *kept his word, hands clean, did the duty* — rather than via Latinate admiration.

> "By the old gods. By the heart-tree at Winterfell. I swore it and I will not unswear it."
> — *synthesized.* Old-gods oath structure. Anaphoric *by the* repetition (different from Faith *by the Father, by the Mother*). The closer *I will not unswear it* uses the *un-* prefix structurally — un-swearing is treated as the named opposite of swearing, and noted as not-done.

> "Long winters make hard men. Hard men make long winters."
> — *synthesized.* The Northern pivoting-proverb. Two clauses, parallel, mutually reframing. Reach for proverb at high register; deploy plainly at low register.

> "The wolf is at the door."
> — *idiom.* Used literally and figuratively. Trouble has come; the metaphor is not exhausted.

> "We do not kneel."
> — *Universal Northern self-positioning.* Uttered against southern authority, against Targaryen demand, against the perceived pretension of court. Carries history, especially in mountain clans and beyond-the-Neck Houses.

## Cadence

Composes with smallfolk or noble cadence below; modifications:

- **Slower than southron.** Northerners speak with longer pauses between sentences. The pause is not deference — it is consideration. Southern listeners often hear it as slowness; Northerners hear southern pacing as glib.
- **The repeated short declarative.** *Hard land. Hard people. Hard winters.* Three short sentences in series, parallel structure, no connective. Smaller version of the noble tricolon, harsher in texture.
- **Pacing under threat: silence increases.** A Northerner under threat says less, not more. The Northern silence is itself a register.
- **The understated punchline.** *He died well enough.* / *It was a hard winter.* / *We managed.* Massive events compressed into laconic understatement. The reader supplies the rest. This is the deepest Northern voice-tell.

## Vocabulary

### Reaches for

- **Old gods register:** *the old gods, the old gods of the forest, the heart-tree, the godswood, the weirwood, the gods of the wood, the silent gods, the carved face.* Not theology — invocation of place and presence.
- **First Men vocabulary:** *blood of the First Men, the old blood, the old way, the first men's gods, before the Andals came.* Lineage anchor.
- **Cold and winter as nouns with weight:** *the cold, the long cold, the long dark, the long winter, the deep winter, the white winter.* Personified; takes verbs of agency. *The cold takes them. The winter knows.*
- **Northern toponymy:** *the Wall, the Gift, the Wolfswood, the Neck, the kingsroad, the white knife (river), the long lake, the rills.* Used precisely; substitutions read as wrongness.
- **Specific Northern fauna and flora:** *direwolf, sentinel pine, ironwood, weirwood, snow bear, shadowcat (also Vale), the white walkers (mythic), the others (mythic).* In casual speech *direwolves are running* not *unusual large wolves were observed*.
- **Honor / oath vocabulary, intensified:** *kept his word, broke his word, oathbreaker, sworn man, sworn bannerman, sworn to.* The Northern register treats word-breaking as the fundamental class of failure; the noun *oathbreaker* is heavier in a Northerner's mouth than elsewhere.
- **Plain-Anglo over Latinate.** Even more pronounced than the GRRM-mannerisms baseline — Northerners avoid Latinate vocabulary almost entirely. *Considerable* never; *sufficient* never; *nevertheless* never. *Hard, true, false, fair, foul, clean, dirty, wrong, right.*

### Refuses to say

- **Faith-of-the-Seven invocation casually.** A Northerner who follows the old gods does not say *Seven save us, Mother of mercy.* If the speaker does, they are either a follower of the Seven living in the North (some southern-influenced lords, some traveling septons) or marked by the listener as wrong. White Harbor and Manderly-bannermen are the exception — Manderlys keep the Seven, and their register reflects it.
- **Courtly Latinate flourishes.** *Presumptuous, regrettable, considerable* in a Northerner's mouth flag southron influence — sometimes welcome, sometimes the basis of mockery.
- **Southern-style oaths.** *On my honor as a knight* sits oddly on a Northerner. The northern oath frame is *by the old gods, by the heart-tree, by my father's name, by the blood of the First Men.*
- **Diminutives and term-of-endearment register.** *Sweetling, dear one, my pet* almost never. The Northern affective register is closer to gruffness or silence; warmth is shown by act, not by word.

### Register markers

- **Aye preserved across class.** Where southern noble register thins out *aye* (it survives in soldiery), Northern noble register keeps *aye* alongside *yes*. A Northern lord may say *aye* in council without flagging as lower-class.
- **First-name address among lords.** Northerners address each other by first name more readily than southern lords do. *Eddard* in a Stark's hall, not *Lord Stark*. Outside the North this would read as either intimacy or breach; inside the North it is structural.
- **Northern lords' sworn-bannermen address.** The bannermen address their liege as *my lord*, *Lord X*, or by House (*Lord Stark*). Honorifics are full and uncontracted as in southern noble register; the warmth comes from elsewhere.

## Syntax

Composes with class register; modifications:

- **Coordination preferred over subordination.** Northerners — including noble Northerners — chain clauses with *and* more than southern nobles do. The southern periodic sentence (long subordinated setup) is rare. Sentences land flat.
- **Subject-implied openings.** *Hard winter, that one. Old wolves at the door. Snow's coming early.* Dropped subjects, articles, copulas. Common at all class levels.
- **The retroactive-reframe single sentence.** A short closing sentence that recontextualizes everything before it, GRRM-baseline pattern, used heavily in Northern register. *And then we burned them.* / *And then it snowed for nine years.* / *He was my brother.*
- **Subjunctive thinned.** Where southern noble register keeps the subjunctive (*were he here, should the lord wish*), Northern register flattens to indicative (*if he was here, if the lord wishes*). Maester-trained Northern lords keep the subjunctive; born-and-bred Northern lords often do not.

## Voice tells

(For Northern-POV interiority and narration.)

- **The cold as constant background.** A Northern interior tracks the cold — temperature, breath, the sound of wind on stone — without naming it directly. The cold is the structural ground of attention, the way standing is the structural ground for noble interiors.
- **Reading the season.** Northerners read the season the way southerners read a court — looking for tells, predicting the turn, planning for what is coming. *The leaves had turned early. The geese were south already. The wolves had come down to the holdfast walls.* The reader supplies the *winter is coming*; the prose names the signs.
- **History as land.** Northern memory anchors to places more than to dates. *Where the king fell. Where the wolves were taken. The grove where the white tree died.* The land carries the memory; the speaker references the place and the listener supplies the event.
- **Restraint under grief.** Northern grief surfaces in body-fact and silence, not in declaration. *He did not speak at the burial. He sat the long evening at the fire and watched the embers fall.* The southerners' eloquent eulogy is alien; Northerners eulogize with shortness.
- **The wolf-tell.** Stark POVs and Northern POVs in general have a particular awareness of dogs and wolves — direwolves where they exist, plain wolves and dogs otherwise. The animal in the room is registered before the people in the room.
- **Suspicion of warmth.** Southern smiles, southern courtesies, southern courtship register read to a Northern interior as unreliable on principle. The interior may not say so; the structure of attention shows it.

## Non-verbal tics

(Physical / behavioral tics that go alongside the spoken register. The dialogue-writer fork should not write these into the dialogue file; the studio and impersonator deploy them. Listed here so the dialect is fully described.)

- **Slow nod over verbal acknowledgement.** A Northerner agrees by nodding, often with a beat of pause first, before any *aye* surfaces. The nod precedes the word.
- **Stillness instead of fidget.** Northerners under stress hold still rather than move. The opposite of southern courtly fidget. Hands flat, feet planted. The stillness is the tell.
- **Eyes-to-the-fire.** When a Northerner is thinking through a hard thing, the gaze drops to whatever fire is nearest — hearth, brazier, candle. A held gaze on the flame for several beats is the Northern equivalent of looking out a window.
- **The long pull at ale or mead.** Drinking is unhurried. Northerners are not drinkers in the southern lordly-feast sense; they drink steadily and without apparent effect. A long pull, set the cup down, no comment.
- **The hand on the pommel.** Sword-hand on the sword-hilt at moments of formal weight — at oaths, at greetings of consequence. Not threat. Affirmation that the bearer is sworn-and-armed, present in body as well as word.
- **The weirwood-touch.** Northerners who keep the old gods touch the weirwood when entering or leaving the godswood. Brief, palm flat to bark. Not theatrical; habitual. Outside the godswood the gesture does not appear.
- **Refusing the kneel.** Northerners stand when southerners would kneel. Not always — feudal homage requires the kneel — but in chambers, in council, in the field, the Northerner stands a beat longer than the southern register expects.
- **The cloak over the shoulder, not closed.** Even in cold, a Northerner working or walking keeps the cloak over the shoulder rather than wrapped — fastened by clasp, free arm. Wrapping the cloak around oneself is what the southerners do.
- **The careful greeting of dogs and wolves.** A Northerner crossing a threshold acknowledges any dog in the room before the people. Eye contact, a hand offered, a slow approach. To not do this is southron.

## Memory monuments — how the North speaks of them

The Northern register has a specific way of voicing or refusing to voice the events that weigh on it. (See `cards/conditions/memory-monuments/` for the monuments themselves; this section describes how the Northern dialect references them.)

- **The Long Night.** Universal mythic load. Spoken of with religious-historical weight. *Before the Long Night. After the Long Night came the building of the Wall.* Reach for *the Long Night* by full phrase; never abbreviate. The First Men's foundational event.
- **The Wall itself.** Spoken of as a structure with agency. *The Wall holds. The Wall stands. The Wall has not yet fallen.* The Wall as actor. To say *the Wall is just stone* is a heresy in Northern register.
- **Aegon's Conquest / the Kneeling.** Northern register frames this as *the day Torrhen knelt* or *when the king who knelt gave up the crown*. Carries shame and survival both. Not invoked casually; reach for it only at moments of real political weight. Often softened with *what had to be done* — the Northerner does not romanticize the bending of the knee.
- **The Hour of the Wolf.** (Post-Dance event.) *When Cregan came south.* The North invokes this with quiet pride, with the understated punchline cadence — *and then he hanged them.* Used to remind southern listeners of what the North can do when wronged.
- **Robert's Rebellion / the Sack / the Trident.** Specific to main-canon era. The North refers to these by event, not by year — *the rebellion, the sack of King's Landing, the prince at the Trident.* Loss of the Stark generation is named obliquely (*after we lost Brandon and the lord*, not by listing the dead).
- **The Mad King.** Universal. The Northern register says *King Aerys* in formal record, *the Mad King* in casual. The casual form carries the felt weight; the formal does not.
- **Bael the Bard / the Rat Cook / the Night's King.** Mythic Northern monuments embedded in song and tale. Smallfolk and lords alike know the stories. Reach for them in proverb and warning. *Like the Night's King* is a curse-frame.
- **The taking of the Watch as memorial register.** When a Northern family has sent a son to the Wall, the family register names him as *gone north* or *taken the black* with a finality — not death but not return either. This is its own monument-by-absence.

The Northern speaker often invokes a monument by **not** naming it. *We have seen worse than this. We have buried more than this.* The listener is expected to know what *worse* refers to.

## Sub-regional notes

- **Mountain clans (Liddle, Wull, Norrey, Flint, etc.):** Heavier *aye*, more First Men vocabulary, more old-gods invocation, idiom of the high country (*the high lonesome, the wind off the spires, the long climb*). Sub-card warranted if a project surfaces a clan character.
- **Beyond the Wall (Free Folk, wildlings):** Distinct enough to warrant a separate card (`westeros-free-folk`). Structurally Northern but with significant divergence — *kneeler* register, *the free folk, you know nothing*, total old-gods saturation, no Faith register.
- **Skagosi:** Distinct, archaic, reportedly with cannibalism. Sub-card warranted if a project goes there.
- **White Harbor (Manderlys):** Faith of the Seven preserved. Northern register with southern-Faith overlay. Speech less old-gods, more Seven-inflected. Sub-card warranted for a Manderly-focused project.
- **Mormonts (Bear Island):** First Men strong, women-as-warriors register, austere even by Northern standards.

- The non-verbal tics section is canonical for this card and applies across class — both Northern lords and Northern smallfolk hold still, watch fires, touch weirwood.
- Memory monuments section anchors how the dialect *references* monuments. The monuments themselves are condition cards; this card borrows them by name and describes the speech-register around them.
