# Westeros — Noble / Courtly Register

Shared dialect card. Voice patterns for highborn Westerosi — landed nobility, their immediate kin, and household members raised in courtly register. Per-character variants should `inherits: westeros-noble-courtly` and overlay individual or House-specific tells.

The register is **courtly**, not *court*. Court (King's Landing, the Red Keep, Highgarden, Winterfell's great hall) is one venue this register is deployed in. The same register applies to noble-on-noble exchange in any setting — across a campfire, in a holdfast, on horseback. The class-marker is in the cadence and vocabulary, not the venue.

---

## Direct samples

> "My lord."
> — *canonical pattern, ASOIAF.* The defining noble address. *My lord*, full and uncontracted. Smallfolk *m'lord* compresses; nobles do not. A noble using *m'lord* to another noble is condescension or affectation; either way, marked.

> "Well met, ser. The road has used you ill."
> — *synthesized.* Greeting then observation, the observation phrased as a compliment-of-noticing. Inverted construction (*has used you ill*) over plain (*was rough on you*). Latinate adverb *ill* over plain *bad*.

> "I will not stand to hear it spoken of in those terms. Not in my hall, ser, and not at any table where I sit."
> — *synthesized.* The noble's refusal-by-territoriality. *Not in my hall* names the property as the basis of the refusal. The double-clause structure (*not in X, and not at Y*) is a courtly rhetorical pattern — parallelism for emphasis without raising the volume.

> "You have my thanks, my lord. I shall not forget the kindness."
> — *synthesized.* *I shall* not *I will* — the older modal carries higher register. *The kindness* with definite article — naming the act as a discrete object that can be remembered, owed, repaid. Noble accounting of social debt is precise and articulated.

> "Honored cousin. We are pleased to receive you under our roof. The Seven brought you safely; we shall see to the rest."
> — *synthesized.* Address by relation-and-honorific. Royal-we / household-we appears in formal hosting register even from minor lords. Religious framing as courteous, not pious. *We shall see to the rest* is the courtly understatement that means everything from food and bed to safety from the lord's enemies.

> "Tell my lord uncle that the matter is settled. He need not trouble himself further."
> — *synthesized.* Indirect address through a third party — a runner, a steward, a knight. Nobles often communicate through intermediaries even within the same hall, and the message-format reflects this: third-person reference (*my lord uncle*), formal closure (*need not trouble himself further*).

> "I find your suggestion presumptuous, ser. You have mistaken your standing."
> — *synthesized.* The noble correction. *I find* over *that is* — the verb of judgment is owned. *Presumptuous* is courtly Latinate for *out of line*. The follow-up *you have mistaken your standing* is the actual rebuke, delivered in a register that does not require a raised voice. Loss of register-control here is what marks an angry noble; the calmer the line, the heavier the consequence.

> "We shall see what the morrow brings. There is little to be done before then."
> — *canonical pattern.* The deferral. *We shall see* and *the morrow* are register-locked phrases. *There is little to be done* is structural noble understatement — the literal content may be that nothing can be done at all, or that a great deal can be done but the speaker is choosing not to.

> "She is a Tully, ser. Her courage was not given to question."
> — *synthesized.* House-as-explanation. A noble accounts for a person's behavior by naming the House. The construction *was not given to* (rather than *is not the kind to*) is courtly — passive-voice formality.

> "I would have your counsel, if you can spare it."
> — *synthesized.* The request from a higher to a lower-ranking noble. *I would have* (subjunctive) softens the command-structure underneath. The trailing *if you can spare it* is courtly courtesy; refusal is technically permitted, practically rare.

## Cadence

- **Long sentences with subordination.** A noble speaks in chained clauses — relative clauses, conditional clauses, parallel clauses — even in informal moments. Subordination is a class-marker.
- **Inverted construction is current.** *Long has it been since...* / *Cold are the winds that carry...* / *Glad am I to find you well.* Smallfolk would say *it has been long*; nobles freely invert for rhetorical weight without flagging.
- **Pause habit: pauses fall within sentences.** A noble pauses mid-clause, choosing the precise word. The smallfolk-pause-at-sentence-end is replaced by a different rhythm: inside the long sentence, with phrasing that frames where the listener should attend.
- **Pacing under pressure: slower, not faster.** A noble under threat or grief tightens, not loosens. Sentences become shorter, more controlled, more declarative. The volume rarely rises. A shouting noble has lost something the listener will note.
- **Repetition is structural, not emphatic.** Where smallfolk repeat (*Seven save us, Seven save us all*) for emotional weight, nobles repeat for rhetorical structure: parallel clauses, anaphora, list-of-three. *Not in my hall, and not at any table where I sit, and not under the eyes of any servant who answers to my house.*

## Vocabulary

### Reaches for

- **Forms of address (full, uncontracted):** *my lord, my lady, your grace, your highness, your majesty, ser, septon, maester, my good cousin, honored uncle, kinsman, kinswoman, goodbrother, goodsister.* Always full forms when up-rank; informal contractions only down-rank or among intimate equals.
- **Affirmatives:** *yes, indeed, just so, as you say, of course, certainly, without question.* The smallfolk *aye* is rare in courtly mouths and marks rusticity when it appears.
- **Latinate vocabulary:** *consider, regard, suffer (allow), wherefore, presumptuous, sufficient, considerable, regardless, nevertheless, thereupon, accordingly, henceforth.* Common register, not flagged as ornate.
- **Modal *shall* over *will*.** *I shall ride at dawn. We shall not forget. You shall have my answer by the morrow.* The *shall* is class-marker — its decline in smallfolk speech is one of the cleaner divisions.
- **House and lineage register:** *of House X, the lord of, the heir to, blood of the dragon, the trueborn son of, sister to, niece of.* Nobles articulate genealogy with structural precision. Mistakes (calling a niece a daughter, omitting House X's seniority) are real social events, not slips.
- **Politeness particles:** *if it please you, with your leave, by your grace, if you would, I would beg.* These soften commands without reducing them. The lord *who would beg your indulgence* is not actually begging; he is observing form.
- **Geographic and toponymic:** Houses, holdfasts, lordships named precisely. *The Riverlands* not *the river country*. *The Stormlands* not *down south by the storm coast*. Specificity is class-marker.
- **Time markers:** by the king's regnal year (*in the seventh year of King Viserys*), by named events (*before the Great Council*), or by Faith calendar like smallfolk. Almost never by agricultural cycle alone.

### Refuses to say

- *M'lord, m'lady, milord/milady* — the contracted smallfolk forms. Nobles using these mock or condescend; nobles being addressed with these by another noble are being insulted or treated as parvenus.
- *Aye, nay* in formal context. Survives in soldierly speech and informal moments; in council, in court, the full forms.
- Double negatives. *I don't know nothing* in a noble mouth flags either drunkenness, distress, or imitation of soldiery. Otherwise audit.
- Folk swearing of the *bloody hells, fuck me, gods damn it* variety in formal contexts. Nobles swear; the register-locked oaths are *Seven hells, by the Seven, by my honor, by my house, the gods know.* Breaking into common swearing is a register-collapse and the listener notes it.
- Direct accusation in council. A noble does not say *you lied to me*. They say *I find your account difficult to reconcile with the dispatch I received this morning*. The directness-vs.-indirectness gradient is itself a register tool.
- Praise of one's own House to its face. A Tully does not say *the Tullys are honorable*; another lord says it about them. Self-praise is left to enemies' attribution.

### Register markers

- **Status-sensitivity.** Every form of address is calibrated to the relative standing of speaker and listener. *My lord* to a peer or superior. *My good ser* to a knight beneath one's standing. *Goodman* to a smallfolk one is being polite to. Mistakes here are noticed.
- **House-pronominal.** *We* and *our* slide between personal and House-personal seamlessly. *We shall not allow it* may mean the speaker individually, the speaker's household, or the speaker's House and bannermen — context disambiguates. Listeners are expected to track which.
- **The third-person reference to oneself by title.** *The Lord of Harrenhal would not consent to that* spoken by the Lord of Harrenhal himself. Used in formal contexts and in moments of self-distancing. Variable by character.
- **Religious register is formal, not casual.** Where smallfolk invoke the Seven habitually, nobles invoke them ceremonially: oaths, blessings, set-piece prayer. Casual *Seven save us* in a noble mouth flags either rusticity, panic, or self-conscious folk-affectation.
- **Honor-vocabulary as load-bearing.** *Honor, oath, word, vow, my house, my name.* These words are not decorative for a noble — they are the operating system of the social class. A noble who says *I give you my word* has executed a contract.

## Syntax

- **Subordination over coordination.** Where smallfolk chain with *and / but*, nobles subordinate: *though the road was hard, I would not have delayed; for had I delayed, the message would not have come in time.* The subordinator-rich syntax is the surface marker of literacy and rhetorical training.
- **Parallel structure for emphasis.** Tricolon (*not in my hall, not at my table, not under my roof*) is a high-register move. Used naturally; not flagged as ornate.
- **Inversion (current and unmarked).** *Cold be the welcome that. Long has it been. Greatly do I regret.* Inversion is rhetorical adjustment, not flagged as archaic — though see the regional/era variation note below.
- **Subjunctive intact.** *If it were so. Were he here. Should the king require it.* The subjunctive is alive in noble speech where it has decayed in smallfolk.
- **Periodic sentences in formal address.** Long opening clauses establish the conditions; the load-bearing main clause arrives at the end. *Given the trouble on the eastern road, and the lateness of the hour, and the temper of my lord uncle, I would advise we wait.* The main clause is the last seven words; everything before is setup.
- **Aphorism and proverb.** Nobles quote received wisdom — house words, Faith proverbs, military adages — as part of normal speech. *A man's word is his honor. The Seven judge in their own time. Cold winds blow before snow.* Smallfolk also quote aphorisms but draw from a different bank; the bank is class-marked.

## Voice tells

(For interiority and noble-POV narration.)

- **Awareness of standing as constant background.** A noble interior tracks who is in the room and what their relative standing is at all times. The narration does not name this; it is the structural ground of attention. New people are placed (*a knight of House X, a hedge knight, a Northman*) before they are described.
- **Honor and reputation as load-bearing.** Where smallfolk POV calculates cost in grain and time, noble POV calculates cost in honor and standing. *He could not refuse without insult. To accept would be to acknowledge the slight. To leave the matter open was itself an answer.* The accounting is real; the currency is reputational.
- **House over self.** A noble POV references their own actions through the lens of House obligation more than personal preference. *He could not, as Lord of X, allow it.* This is internal register, not performance — the House identification is structural.
- **Lineage memory.** Noble POV carries genealogy at conversational depth. They know the cousins, the marriages, the bastards, the inheritance lines. When a name is mentioned, the interior often reaches for lineage before character.
- **Indirection as habit.** Noble POV does not name the thing it is most concerned with. *The matter*, *the question of*, *what we discussed*. Direct naming of a sensitive topic is a register-shift the interior notes.
- **The cost of breaking register.** When a noble loses register — shouts, swears commonly, addresses a peer with a contraction — the interior usually records the slip immediately, even if the surface narration does not. The slip is the moment.

## Era and regional notes

- **House of the Dragon era (95–130 AC) vs. main-canon era (~298 AC).** The courtly register is broadly stable across these centuries; the differences are in: (a) Valyrian-derived vocabulary persisting more in HotD-era Targaryen households, (b) inversion and *shall* forms slightly more current in HotD-era and slightly archaic-marked by main-canon, (c) the Faith register tighter in HotD before Maegor's reforms have fully receded. For the Westeros 120 AC project: lean slightly more formal, slightly more *shall*, slightly more inversion.
- **Northern register.** Northern nobles speak in a register that drops some of the Latinate vocabulary, retains *aye* in informal contexts even in noble register, and weights honor-and-oath vocabulary heavier. Old-gods religious register replaces Faith register. To author a Northern noble specifically, overlay these on top of the courtly base.
- **Dornish register.** More direct than other Westerosi noble registers, comfortable with bawdy reference where other regions are not, retains some Rhoynar-derived vocabulary. Differs from the base sufficiently that a per-region card may be warranted later.
- **Iron Islands.** Distinct enough that Ironborn noble register would warrant its own card.

## Authoring notes

- This card is the class-register pattern bank for highborn speech. House-specific or character-specific tells should be authored as per-character variants that `inherits: westeros-noble-courtly`.
- Royal register (Targaryen kings/queens, the Iron Throne) is a further intensification of this register and is not fully covered here. A separate `westeros-royal` shared card may be warranted.
- The `character: <shared>` sentinel is the same placeholder used in `westeros-smallfolk` pending schema reconciliation.
- House words and personal mottos sit on top of this register; they do not replace it. A character who says *Hear Me Roar* says it inside the courtly register, not as a substitute for it.
