# Westeros — Smallfolk Register

Shared dialect card. Voice patterns for baseborn commoners in Westeros across most regions. Per-character variants for specific smallfolk should `inherits: westeros-smallfolk` and overlay regional/personal modifications on top.

For regional variation (Northern, Riverlands, Reach, Stormlands, Crownlands, Westerlands, Vale, Iron Islands, Dorne) the cadence shifts but the class-register patterns hold. This card describes the class layer; future regional cards can overlay accent/idiom on top.

---

## Direct samples

> "M'lord. Beggin' your pardon, m'lord."
> — *canonical pattern, ASOIAF.* The defining smallfolk address. *M'lord* not *my lord*. Two beats: title, soft-apology before the actual statement. Standard opening to any smallfolk-to-noble exchange when smallfolk is forced to speak first.

> "Aye, ser. I seen 'im. Down by the mill, like."
> — *synthesized, canonical pattern.* Affirmative is *aye*, not *yes*. Past tense is collapsed (*I seen* not *I saw*; *I done* not *I did*; *I been* not *I have been*). Trailing *like* as a softener. Dropped *h* in *'im*. Sentence-final hedge is the safety move.

> "Won't be no trouble, m'lord. We'll see to it."
> — *synthesized.* Double negative is normal register, not error. Future contraction *we'll* over *we will*. Subject-implied opening (*won't be*, not *there won't be*).

> "The well's gone bad. Two nights now. The water comes up brown."
> — *synthesized.* Three short declaratives. No subordination — events stacked, not chained. The reader supplies the causal connection. This is smallfolk reporting register: facts in the order they occurred, no analysis.

> "I'm just a poor woman, ser. I don't know nothin' about that."
> — *canonical pattern.* The poor-woman-disclaimer is a smallfolk move under interrogation. Combines: (1) class-positioning to lower expectations, (2) double negative *don't know nothin'*, (3) the *ser* used regardless of whether the man is actually a knight. The disclaimer is often false and is understood as a register-move, not as factual self-description.

> "Old Mother says it's the cold come early. Says we'll lose half the lambs by Maiden's Day if it holds."
> — *synthesized.* Authority-by-elder. Smallfolk attribute prediction or judgment to a named elder rather than claim it themselves. *Says* dropped-subject second-clause is canonical — the speaker drops the subject when the topic is established. Religious calendar reference (Maiden's Day) is the time-marker; smallfolk track time by the Faith calendar and the agricultural year.

> "Begging your pardon, m'lord, but the steward — he were here this morning, and he said —"
> — *synthesized.* Long sentence with em-dashes is rare for smallfolk; when it happens, it is interrupted/corrected speech under pressure. *He were* (singular subject, plural verb) is class-marker; in some regions invariant.

> "Seven save us. Seven save us all."
> — *canonical pattern.* The reflex prayer. Repeated for emphasis. *The Mother have mercy*, *Father judge him true*, *Stranger pass us by* are the variants. Religious utterance is conversational, not ceremonial — these are the equivalent of a sigh or an exclamation, not formal prayer.

> "He's a good boy. Quiet. Don't give no trouble."
> — *synthesized.* Character description in three short sentences. The third sentence is the proof of the second; the structure is claim → quality → evidence. This is also how smallfolk recommend a person to a position.

## Cadence

- **Short sentences. Stacked, not chained.** A smallfolk speaker reports events in occurrence order. They do not subordinate. *I went to the mill. The wheel were broken. I came back.* Three sentences, three facts. A noble would write this as one sentence with subordination.
- **The repeat-for-emphasis pattern.** When a thing matters, it is said twice. *Seven save us. Seven save us all.* *He's a good boy. A good boy.* The repetition replaces the intensifier-vocabulary that smallfolk register lacks.
- **Trailing softeners.** Sentence-final *like*, *I reckon*, *or so they say*, *if it please you*. The softener distances the speaker from the claim — useful when speaking up the hierarchy.
- **Pause habit.** Smallfolk pause to be given permission to continue, not to choose a word. The pause comes at the end of a sentence-unit, not in the middle of one. An interlocutor who does not say *go on* may not get the rest.
- **Pacing under pressure: faster, not slower.** When frightened, smallfolk talk more, hedging more, repeating more, not less. The fragments-under-fear pattern is a noble-register pattern, not a smallfolk one.

## Vocabulary

### Reaches for

- **Forms of address (descending):** *m'lord, m'lady, ser, septon, maester, goodman, goodwife, milord/milady* (variant). *Lord* never used as a bare noun without honorific by smallfolk to nobles' faces.
- **Affirmatives:** *aye, ay, that's the way of it, true enough, I reckon so.* Almost never *yes* in casual register.
- **Negatives:** *nay, no I don't, I never, I done no such thing.* Often doubled.
- **Religious utterance:** *Seven, gods, the Mother, the Father, the Stranger, Maiden's Day, the Smith keep your hands.* Casual invocation, not formal prayer.
- **Family / community:** *my old da, my goodman, my goodwife, the missus, my eldest, the wee one, Old Mother, Old Tom.* Names used with elder-honorifics or possessive-positioning.
- **Agricultural / lived-environment vocabulary:** *the long field, the bog, the mill, the cot, the byre, the ditch, the green, the smith's, the sept-house, the lord's woods.* Geography is named by function, not by toponym.
- **Time markers:** by the Faith calendar (*come Maiden's Day, by Father's Eve, the seventh day*), by agricultural cycle (*at planting, come harvest, after the lambing, in the long dark*), by ruler-year (*in old Lord Strong's time, the year the river froze*). Almost never by ASOIAF year-numbers, which are scholarly register.
- **Sums and counts:** *a copper, a silver, a stag, a moon's worth of grain, a half-bushel.* Coinage and grain measure. Distances by walk-time (*a morning's walk, half a day, a league or so*).

### Refuses to say

- *Indeed, certainly, perhaps, however, nevertheless, in any case.* These are noble/courtly markers; smallfolk usage of them flags either ambition (a smallfolk imitating noble register) or wrongness (a noble in disguise being read as wrong).
- Multi-syllable Latinate vocabulary in casual speech. *Considerable, sufficient, evaluate, arrangement, regardless* — flags as out-of-register.
- Anachronistic idiom from base-card dialects (*okay, fine, alright, sure, hi, yeah*). Hard fence. Audit immediately.
- The first-person plural *we* used for the speaker's social class as a whole. A smallfolk says *I*, *me and mine*, *us here*, but does not say *we smallfolk* or generalize about their own class. Class is a thing the nobles talk about, not a thing the smallfolk frame themselves through.
- Sarcasm or irony directed up the hierarchy. Smallfolk sarcasm exists but stays sideways or down — directed at a peer, an enemy of equal standing, or a child. Never up. Punishments are real.
- Bare proper-noun reference to nobles. Not *Lord Tully said* but *His lordship said* or *the Lord of Riverrun's man said*. Direct naming of nobles in their absence is too familiar; in their presence, requires title.

### Register markers

- **Class-deference is structural and verbal both.** The verbal markers (m'lord, ser, begging your pardon) are layered on top of physical deference (eyes down, body recessed). Removing the verbal markers without compensating in body language reads as insolence and is a real risk.
- **Within-class register is much looser.** Smallfolk talking to smallfolk drop most of the deferential register, swear freely, joke darkly, and use the contraction-heavy collapsed-tense vocabulary fully. The mask is for up-the-ladder.
- **Religious register is conversational.** The Faith of the Seven saturates speech in casual ways. *Mother of mercy, by the Father's grace, Stranger take him, Smith's hammer.* Not pious — habitual. Removing all religious utterance from a smallfolk character flags as wrongness.
- **Old gods register (north of the Neck).** Northern smallfolk who follow the old gods substitute *the gods, the old gods, the heart-tree, the godswood* and avoid Seven-pointed reference. Some Northern smallfolk use both, depending on village. This is a regional-overlay concern.

## Syntax

- **Was/were inversion.** Smallfolk register often uses *was* with plural subjects (*we was there*) and *were* with singular (*he were here*). Both directions appear; the inversion is class-marker. Highly variable by region.
- **Double and triple negatives.** *Don't know nothin'* / *ain't never seen no such* / *can't do no harm to nobody.* Standard register. Treating these as grammatical errors is the noble register reading the smallfolk register; do not "correct" them.
- **Dropped initial *h*.** *'im, 'ere, 'ad it, 'is.* Variable by region; common in Crownlands, Riverlands, Stormlands smallfolk. Less in the North.
- **Collapsed past participles.** *I seen, I done, I been, I come, I give it him.* Standard. *I gave* and *I saw* in a smallfolk's mouth read as imitation or as wrongness.
- **Subject-implied openings.** *Won't be no trouble. Done already. Goes that way every spring.* The dropped subject is the topic established by context; the speaker assumes it carries.
- **Coordination over subordination.** Sentences are connected by *and* and *but* far more than by *because, although, while, since.* When subordination appears, it is short and concrete (*after the cart broke*), not abstract (*due to the failure of the conveyance*).
- **Question-tags.** *In't it? Han't you? Don't he? Won't they?* Used to invite confirmation. The *in't* / *han't* / *don't* forms are class-marker; the question-tag itself is universal but the form is class-typed.

## Voice tells

(For interiority, third-person narration set in smallfolk POV, or a smallfolk-character impersonator.)

- **Survival as the default subtext.** The interior of a smallfolk POV is calculating cost — of grain, of time, of words. Every interaction with authority has a cost-accounting layer running underneath. The narration does not name this; the structure of attention shows it.
- **Religious explanation for the unexplainable.** When a smallfolk POV encounters something they cannot account for — a death, a bad season, a strange child — the interior reaches for the Seven. *The Stranger had taken him. Maybe the Crone knew. The Mother gave and took as she would.* This is not piety; it is the available framework.
- **The named-other.** Smallfolk POV identifies people by relation rather than by name. *The blacksmith's eldest. The miller's wife. Old Tom's youngest girl.* Names appear when the relation is insufficient or the person is being singled out for the listener's attention.
- **Distance from event-history.** Wars, royal succession, large political events appear in smallfolk interior at the texture of weather. *That were the year the lord's men rode through. That were the bad year, after the snow.* The event is a marker; the political content is invisible because it is structurally invisible to the speaker.
- **Body-fact for emotion, by indirection.** Smallfolk register does not narrate feeling directly. Instead: *her hands were shaking when she set the bowl down. He didn't eat that night. The boy hadn't said a word since the morning.* The behavior is the report.
- **Suspicion of the unfamiliar, named flatly.** New people are *strangers, them as just come, the man with the southron face.* The naming is not hostile — it is structural. Strangers are a category that requires assessment.
