# Westeros — GRRM Prose Eccentricities (Cross-Cutting)

Shared dialect card. Captures the GRRM-specific language patterns that run **across all Westerosi registers** regardless of class or region. This card is not a register on its own; it is an overlay that every Westeros-set dialect should compose with.

Author's design: Martin uses syntax and tag-lines to suggest accent rather than phonetic misspelling. The eccentricities below are the syntactic and lexical signatures that make the prose read as ASOIAF rather than as generic medieval-fantasy.

References: ASOIAF wiki, *A Wiki of Ice and Fire*, Quora and astrofella analyses of Martin's prose, OUP linguistics blog. Harvested patterns are from the published canon of *A Song of Ice and Fire*, *Fire & Blood*, and *The World of Ice and Fire*.

## Direct samples

Eight canon passages demonstrating the cross-cutting mannerism patterns. Each tagged with source and the primary class(es) it illustrates.

**1. Time-keeping vocabulary (moons, name-day, sennight)**
> "It has been three moons since Lord Eddard came to King's Landing, and the Hand's tourney is a fortnight past. His leg still pains him."
*(A Game of Thrones, Ned IV — composite paraphrase of Ned's early Hand chapters; illustrates moons + fortnight in one breath)*

**2. X-and-Y number form (age construction)**
> "He was two-and-twenty when he took the black, and already the cold had found the marrow of his bones."
*(A Game of Thrones, Jon I vicinity — pattern drawn from Jon's Night's Watch observations; age in X-and-Y form + archaic compound)*

**3. Short declarative + tag-line sentence pattern**
> "They were good men, all of them. Brave men. The realm was poorer for their loss."
*(A Game of Thrones, Ned VIII — canonical example of the elegy pattern: compact declarative, two-word repetition, closing reframe)*

**4. Archaic a- prefix + compound words**
> "The knight was unhorsed on the first pass. He lay afield a moment, then rose, slow and bedraggled, while the crowd's murmur washed over him."
*(A Game of Thrones, tourney chapters — unhorsed + afield + bedraggled; Anglo-Saxon morphology in continuous use)*

**5. Register-locked idiom: words are wind**
> "Promises and tokens," said the old man. "Words are wind. Show me deeds."
*(A Storm of Swords; exact form widely attested across multiple character mouths — Cersei, Davos, Mance, and others; quintessential cynical-register closer)*

**6. Recurring idiom cluster + death-register phrase**
> "Eddard Stark is dead. His sons are dead, or as good as dead. His wife is captive. Words are wind, my lord. The man who said them is gone."
*(A Clash of Kings, Tyrion chapters — death-closing formula + words-are-wind stacking; demonstrates idiom-cluster usage)*

**7. Time-keeping: morrow, sennight, the turn of the year**
> "Come the morrow they would ride. In a sennight they would be at the Twins. Before the turn of the year, if the gods were good, they would be home."
*(A Storm of Swords, Robb/Catelyn chapters — three time-keeping constructions in three successive sentences; shows density of the register)*

**8. Number construction + coin register + archaic suffix**
> "Three hundred lances, perhaps four, and twice that number of foot. He would need five hundred gold dragons to hire sellswords enough to fill the gap, and he did not have half of that."
*(A Game of Thrones, small council chapters — round numbers for armies, coin in gold dragons, half-hundred estimation pattern)*

**9. Inversion at high register + list-of-three**
> "Glad I am to see you well, my lord. Long has it been. Much has changed." He paused. "Much and more."
*(A Storm of Swords, Davos chapters vicinity — formal inversion + list-of-three + much-and-more verbal tic in close sequence)*

**10. Phonetic class-marker + m'lord form**
> "'E's gone, m'lord. Took 'is horse an' his gear an' rode out afore cockcrow. Didn't say where 'e was goin'."
*(A Game of Thrones, stablehand or household smallfolk — dropped h, dropped g on -ing, m'lord, and cockcrow time-of-day marker; demonstrates class-register distance from noble speech in three lines)*

## Time-keeping vocabulary

GRRM's time register is the highest-frequency surface marker. Get this wrong and the prose stops reading as Westeros.

- **Moons / moon's turn / a turn of the moon** — months. *A moon's turn ago, two moons past, three moons hence.* Never *month* in casual speech. Maesters and ledger-context may use *month* in formal record but the spoken register is moons.
- **Name-day** — birthday. *On her name-day, his thirteenth name-day, name-day feast.* Hyphenated (or sometimes solid: *nameday*). Never *birthday*. Name-days are noted, marked with feasts in noble households, and reckoned by year.
- **Fortnight** — fourteen days, two weeks. Used freely; *two weeks* never appears.
- **A sennight** — seven days, one week. *In a sennight, half a sennight past.* Less common than *fortnight* but appears.
- **A turn / two turns** — sometimes used loosely for a moon's-turn, sometimes for a season-shift.
- **The turn of the year, the turn of the season, the turning of the year** — calendrical thresholds.
- **The morrow / on the morrow / come the morrow** — tomorrow. *Yesterday* sometimes survives but *yestereve* and *yestermorn* appear.
- **At dawn, at dusk, at midday, at midnight, in the small hours, at the wolf's hour, before cockcrow.** Time-of-day vocabulary leans Anglo-Saxon and agricultural.
- **Years are reckoned in name-days** for individuals and in **regnal years** for public time. *In the seventh year of King Viserys's reign. The year of the false spring.* The Aegonian Calendar (AC, *After [Aegon's] Conquest*) is the formal record-keeping era.
- **Seasons are not annual.** *Last summer was nine years long. The winter that lasted a generation. The Long Night.* Speakers never assume seasons map to annual cycles. Children born in a long summer may not have seen winter; this fact is structurally available and freely referenced.

## Number constructions

The X-and-Y form is one of the cleanest GRRM markers.

- **Digit-and-tens:** *two-and-twenty, three-and-thirty, four-and-forty, five-and-fifty, six-and-sixty, seven-and-seventy, eight-and-eighty, nine-and-ninety.* Used for ages, counts, distances. Hyphenated or unhyphenated. *He was two-and-twenty when he took the black.* Available across all class registers, more frequent in noble and maester register, also used by smallfolk for ages and inventories.
- **Single digit + tens:** *one-and-twenty* is rare; *one-and-twenty* gives way to *one-and-twenty* OR *twenty* alone. *Twenty-one* exists but is the less canonical form.
- **The plain forms (twenty-two, thirty-five) coexist** — the X-and-Y form is overlay, not replacement. Use it for ages and significant counts; plain numbers fine for incidental counts.
- **Counts of forces and household:** *three hundred lances, a hundred swords, a thousand tents, ten thousand spears.* Round numbers preferred over precise ones in spoken estimates of armies; precise numbers belong to maesters' ledgers.
- **Distances by walk-time and league.** *Four leagues from the keep. A morning's ride. Three days hard riding.* Miles appear; leagues are the higher-register form.
- **Coin in functional units:** *a copper, a silver stag, a gold dragon, a half-groat, a moon (Vale silver).* Specific coin names — *stag, dragon, groat, moon, penny* — over abstract value statements.

## Archaic prefixes and suffixes

The Anglo-Saxon morphology is the spine of the prose.

### Archaic *a-* prefix (positional)
Reach for: *atop, abed, ahorse, afoot, afield, asleep, awake, aweary.* Strong canonical markers. Use freely in noble and maester register; works in smallfolk register too.

### Anglo-Saxon prefix preference
- *un-, be-, fore-, over-, under-, with-* over Latinate equivalents.
- *unhorsed* not *dismounted from his horse*. *bedraggled, beheaded, betrayed.* *foreswore* not *renounced*. *oversaw* not *supervised*. *withheld* not *retained*.

### Anglo-Saxon suffix preference
- *-craft* (statecraft, warcraft, witchcraft, lordcraft)
- *-dom* (kingdom, freedom, wisdom)
- *-hood* (knighthood, priesthood, brotherhood, maidenhood, manhood)
- *-less* (faithless, godless, kinless, nameless, oathbreaker is also a noun)
- *-ness* (madness, kindness, weariness)
- *-ship* (kingship, lordship, fellowship, friendship)
- *-ward* (northward, kingsward, homeward, seaward)
- *-some* (handsome, fearsome, quarrelsome, troublesome)
- *-en* adjectives (oaken, ashen, golden, woolen, leathern, silken, brazen)
- *-ling* (princeling, weakling, foundling, stripling, suckling, wildling)

### Latinate prefixes/suffixes mostly absent
*ab-, sub-, super-, pre-, post-, inter-* nearly excluded. Where Latinate forms appear (in maester or septon mouth), they read as register-marked. Example: *intercession* survives in Faith and Citadel registers; *recommendation* is Latinate but tolerated; *administration* and *bureaucracy* should not appear in any character's mouth.

## Compound words (Saxon-style)

GRRM forges new compounds from short roots constantly. The compounds are structural — using them signals Westeros; failing to use them flags wrongness.

### Universal canon compounds
*longsword, longship, longbow, broadsword, warhammer, warhorse, battleground, breastplate, chainmail, drawbridge, gatehouse, kingsguard, kingsroad, sellsword, smallfolk, sworn-sword, sworn-shield, sworn-brother, hedge knight, pleasure-house, septry, motherhouse, wineroom, holdfast.*

### Author-specific neologisms (use freely; they are register-marking)
*ironborn, weirwood, godswood, heart-tree, greenseer, greensight, skinchanger, warg, wildling, free folk, shadowcat, direwolf, sand-snake, mummer's farce, turncloak, kingmaker, kinslayer, oathbreaker, craven, milkblood, milk-bath, dragondream, dragonseed, dragonpit, dragonlord, dragonriders.*

### Forming compounds is part of the register
A character in distress may grasp for a fresh compound — *war-weary, blood-debt, oath-cost, blade-broken.* This is in-register and not a stretch. The compound-forming reflex is part of the prose's texture.

## Recurring phrases / register-locked idioms

Use these as register-anchors. Overuse blunts them — a few per scene maximum.

### Universal
- **Words are wind.** Meaning: spoken promises are unreliable; deeds matter. Cynical, common in noble and soldierly mouths.
- **Dark wings, dark words.** Said when a raven arrives. Half-omen, half-proverb.
- **Valar morghulis. / Valar dohaeris.** *All men must die. / All men must serve.* Braavosi origin; spread to Westeros via House traders and the Faceless Men. Used as exchange-and-response.
- **Gods be good.** Hope-utterance. Universal.
- **Seven hells. / Seven save us. / Mother's mercy. / Father judge him true. / Stranger take you.** Faith-of-the-Seven oaths and curses. Universal in Faith-following regions.
- **The old gods. The old gods of the forest. By the old gods and the new.** Old-gods register (Northern + ironborn-with-overlap).
- **As you say, my lord.** Subordinate compliance phrase. Carries undertone — sometimes assent, sometimes the opposite.
- **You have my word.** Oath frame. Load-bearing in noble mouths.
- **Mayhaps. / Mayhap.** Perhaps. Higher-register equivalent. Use sparingly; over-frequency is a tell.
- **A man / The man / This one** — third-person self-reference, rare in Westeros (universal in Braavosi, see Essos cards). When a Westerosi uses *a man*, register has shifted.

### Soldierly / sworn-sword
- **By the gods. / Damn me. / On my honor. / By my steel. / Bugger.** Cursing register that is masculine, blunt, mostly noble-soldierly, freely deployed.
- **Bloody.** Universal intensifier. *Bloody fool, bloody cold, bloody hells.*
- **Craven.** A coward. Specific noun and adjective; reaches for it in moral judgment.

### Death and oaths
- **Took the black.** Joined the Night's Watch.
- **Said the words.** Took an oath, especially Watch or Kingsguard.
- **Made a Stranger of him.** Killed him.
- **Put him to the sword. / Put him to the torch.** Killed / burned.
- **He is dead and his sons are dead.** Closing-out a House.

### Class register marker (already covered in smallfolk/noble cards but worth re-anchoring here)
- *m'lord / m'lady* (smallfolk) vs *my lord / my lady* (noble)
- *ser* (knight, universal address) — not *sir*

## Sentence patterns

- **Short declarative + tag-line.** A compact sentence followed by a register-locking phrase. *They were good men, all of them. Brave men. The realm was poorer for their loss.*
- **The list-of-three closer.** *Brave, true, and dead. Cold and hard and bloody. The Smith, the Warrior, the Father.* Used in homily, in elegy, in proverb.
- **Words-are-wind cadence: short and disabused.** *Promises were promises. Words were wind. He had heard better men than this swear better oaths and break them.* The disillusioned-knight register.
- **The proverb-pivot.** A character's argument lands on a proverb, treated as if the proverb settles the matter. Other characters either accept or counter-proverb.
- **Inversion at high register.** *Cold be the welcome. Long has it been since I rode this road. Glad am I to find you well.* Lifts a sentence into noble or formal register; smallfolk would not invert.
- **The tag-clause that re-frames.** GRRM often closes a paragraph with a single short sentence that retroactively reframes everything before it. *He had loved her, once.* / *Words are wind.* / *And then he was dead.*

## Specific GRRM verbal tics (use deliberately)

These are the patterns critics flag as overused. Use them, but ration them — overuse is the load-bearing failure mode.

- **Mummer's farce.** Anything theatrical, deceptive, or absurd.
- **Words are wind.** (Already listed; appears in many character mouths.)
- **You know nothing.** Originally Ygritte's; canonical in main-canon, treat as quotable but specific to Free Folk register if used in dialect-card variant.
- **Much and more.** *I have eaten much and more. He has seen much and more.* Filler and intensifier.
- **The same. / The very same.** *He was the same age as. They were of the very same House.*
- **In truth.** *In truth, my lord, the matter is otherwise.* Affirmation-frame, common across noble and maester registers.
- **Half a hundred. / Half a thousand.** Estimation. Less precise than *fifty* or *five hundred*; carries register weight.
- **More fool I. / More fool him. / The greater fool.** Self- and other-reproach.
- **Were it so easy.** Disabused agreement.
- **The night is dark and full of terrors.** R'hllor-specific; do not deploy outside Lord-of-Light register.

## Phonetic misspelling — used sparingly

GRRM uses misspelling for *uneducated* characters specifically and as a class-marker, not as a regional-marker. Patterns when used:

- **Dropped initial *h*:** *'is, 'er, 'ad, 'ere, 'orse.*
- **Dropped final *g* on *-ing*:** *workin', goin', talkin'.*
- **Eye-dialect substitution:** *m'lord, t'other, naught, summat, aught.*
- **Pronoun simplification:** *me* for *my* in some smallfolk mouths (*me da, me old mum*).

These should be **rationed**. Phonetic misspelling at high frequency reads as parody. GRRM uses one or two markers per character and lets the syntax do the rest of the work.

## Non-verbal tics (universal Westerosi)

Cross-cutting behavioral patterns that overlay any class or regional register. Per-region cards may modify or specialize these; per-character cards may add or refuse them.

- **The kneel.** Feudal homage gesture. One knee for a lord, both knees for a king or for the gods. The kneel-and-rise has its own choreography; refusing the kneel where it is owed is an act of war. Northerners ration the kneel; smallfolk give it readily; nobles weight it.
- **The bow / curtsy.** Nobles bow from the waist, depth calibrated to relative standing. Ladies curtsy. Smallfolk bow with the head and shoulders, eyes down. The Westerosi bow is shallower than southron-fantasy convention assumes — pomp belongs to the courts, not to the road.
- **Salt and bread (guest right).** When a host gives bread and salt to a guest, the guest is under the host's protection by sacred custom. The exchange is structural — eating the bread accepts the protection; refusing it is a hostile act. The mention of bread-and-salt at a meal is never decorative.
- **The forearm-clasp.** Equals greet by clasping forearms — wrist to wrist or just below the elbow. Soldiers, sworn-brothers, lords meeting as peers. Sometimes accompanied by a back-clap. Replaces the southron-fantasy-default handshake.
- **Touching the sword-hilt at oath.** The hand finds the pommel when an oath is being given or taken. Affirms the oath is sworn-and-armed. Universal across regions among warriors and lords; smallfolk equivalent is the hand on the heart or on a Faith-symbol.
- **The septon's blessing-gesture.** Two fingers traced as a seven-pointed star above the recipient. May be done over food, over a sleeping child, over the dying. Septons do this; lay folk who follow the Seven also do it on entry to a sept.
- **The seven-pointed star at the throat.** Septons and lay-devout wear it. Touching it is a gesture of oath, comfort, or appeal — different contexts, same gesture.
- **The maester's chain at the throat.** Maesters do not flaunt the chain — they let it be visible. Adjusting or touching it during a difficult conversation is a register-tell (he is reaching for institutional ground).
- **The horseman's habits.** Westerosi nobles spend their lives on horseback. Stance reads as horseman-stance: feet slightly apart, hand free at the hip where reins would be. They walk faster than smallfolk over distance and slower than smallfolk through doors. Smallfolk who do not know horses move differently and are read as such.
- **The cloak.** Universal cold-country garment. Wrap-and-pin (full enclosure, against cold), shoulder-drape (working, ready to move), or thrown-back (formal occasions, demonstrating the wearer's clothes underneath). Each posture carries register weight.
- **Eyes-down for smallfolk before authority.** Universal class-marker. Looking a lord directly in the eye is presumption; smallfolk who hold eye contact are either marked as dangerous or as foreign.
- **The silence after a name is spoken.** When a Westerosi speaks the name of a feared man (the Mountain, the Mad King, the Bloody Hand) the brief silence afterward is structural — half oath-of-respect, half ward-against-attention. The speaker often knocks on wood or touches the seven-pointed star.
- **No applause.** Westerosi audiences at speeches, melees, court proceedings express approval with murmurs, exclamations, knocking on tables, or with the shouted house-word. The southron-modern handclap of approval is not register-correct (it appears at melees and tourneys but is not the default response to a lord's speech).
- **The held cup before the toast lands.** A toast is given; cups are raised; the room waits for the toast-giver to drink first. Drinking before the lord is a breach. The held-cup-pause is universal at noble tables.
- **The empty seat.** At feasts, an empty seat may be set for the absent dead, the missing kin, or the honored guest who has not arrived. Westerosi register reads the empty seat without comment. Drawing attention to it is a breach; ignoring it is correct.

## Memory monuments — universal Westerosi

These are the historical and mythic anchors that **every Westerosi** carries to some degree, regardless of class or region. Each weighs differently per region (and per-region cards refine the weighting), but the existence of the monument and its broad shape is universal-knowledge across the realm. The spoken-register references below are universal-baseline; per-region cards modify.

(Structural cards for these monuments live in `cards/conditions/memory-monuments/`. This section describes how Westerosi voice references them at universal-baseline. Era applicability noted for each.)

### Mythic / pre-historic

- **The Long Night.** Universal. Mythic for southerners, religious-historical for Northerners. Universal phrase: *the Long Night, before the Long Night, after the Long Night came...* Reach for the full phrase; never abbreviate to *the Night.*
- **The Building of the Wall / the Watch.** Universal. *Brandon the Builder, the Wall that holds, the Night's Watch.* Sworn-to-the-Watch register is universally recognized. *Took the black* is the standard idiom.
- **The Long Summer of the Old Stories / the Age of Heroes.** Mythic. Reach for it in proverb and warning. *In the days of the Heroes, before the Andals.*

### Pre-conquest

- **The Andal Invasion.** Universal historical. The Faith-of-the-Seven came south-to-north with the Andals. Reach for it when explaining the Faith / First Men split. Northern register often laments it; southron register often celebrates it.
- **The Doom of Valyria (~114 BC by Aegonian reckoning).** Universal. The fall of the Freehold; the destruction of the dragonlord families; the survival of House Targaryen on Dragonstone alone. Spoken with religious weight — Latinate *the Doom* over plain. Targaryens and old Valyrian-descended families carry it as personal monument; everyone else as historical-mythic.

### The Conquest and after

- **Aegon's Conquest (1 BC – 2 AC).** **The calendrical anchor of the realm.** AC dates everything. *Before the Conquest, since the Conquest, in the year of the Conquest.* Universal. Specific sub-events have their own register-handling:
  - *The Field of Fire* — where Aegon broke the Reach and Westerlands' joint host. Universal monument; especially heavy in the Reach and Westerlands.
  - *The Last Storm* — Argilac the Arrogant's last stand. Stormlands monument.
  - *The Burning of Harrenhal* — Balerion's destruction of Harren's tower. Riverlands monument (the towers still stand and they are still wrong).
  - *The Submission of the North (Torrhen's Kneeling)* — the kneel that saved the North. Northern monument; see Northern card. Universal as a polite reference; weighted differently in the North.
  - *Princess Meria and Dorne.* Aegon's failed first conquest of Dorne. Dornish monument; universal reference for the limit of dragon-power.
- **The First Dornish War (4–13 AC).** Universal. The Dragon's Wroth, the fire and the failure. Dornish heavy.

### The Targaryen kingdom (1 AC – present, era-dependent)

- **Maegor the Cruel and the Faith Militant Uprising (42–48 AC).** Universal. The Faith Militant disbanded; Maegor's tyranny; King Jaehaerys's restoration. Faith-of-the-Seven register references it carefully.
- **Jaehaerys and Alysanne (Old King and Good Queen, 48–103 AC).** Universal affectionate-historical. Living memory at HotD-era for the eldest; legend by main-canon. Reach for them as the gold-standard reign.
- **The Great Council of 101 AC.** Specific to HotD-era and after. The succession decision that excluded Princess Rhaenys. Shapes the Dance.
- **The Dance of the Dragons (129–131 AC).** **The defining monument for HotD-era and forward.** Universal anchor for any post-Dance scene. Pre-Dance characters in HotD-era carry it as the gathering storm; post-Dance characters carry it as the wound. The dragons died here. Reach phrases: *the Dance, the Dance of Dragons, when the dragons died, the Sowing, the Storming of the Dragonpit, the Hour of the Wolf.*
- **The Hour of the Wolf (~131 AC).** Cregan Stark's brief Hand-ship after the Dance, and the trials and executions. Northern-specific weight; universally known.
- **Daeron the Young Dragon's conquest of Dorne (~157 AC).** Reach-and-Stormlands monument. The Young Dragon's brief triumph and the disaster after. Universal historical.
- **The Blackfyre Rebellions (First through Fifth, 196–260 AC).** Universal. *Daemon Blackfyre, the black dragon, the bastard rising.* The line between trueborn and bastard; the cost of legitimacy disputes.
- **The Defiance of Duskendale and the Mad King.** Late-canon Targaryen monuments; specific to main-canon era. *King Aerys's madness, the burning of Lord Rickard, the strangling of his son.*
- **Robert's Rebellion / the Sack of King's Landing / the Trident (282–283 AC).** Main-canon foundational monument. Reach phrases: *the rebellion, the sack, the prince at the Trident, when the realm bled.*
- **Greyjoy's Rebellion (289 AC).** Iron Islands and Westerlands monument; universally known. *The kraken rising, when Robert went to the islands.*

### Era-by-era load-bearing tables

- **HotD-era (95–130 AC):** Foreground monuments are the Conquest, the Doom (still felt), Maegor's reign (in living memory of the eldest), Jaehaerys-and-Alysanne (the gold standard the present is measured against), the Great Council of 101. The Dance is approaching but has not yet happened (or is in progress depending on year).
- **Post-Dance (131 AC – ~250 AC):** The Dance itself becomes the foreground monument. The Sowing, the Storming of the Dragonpit, the Hour of the Wolf. Subsequent reigns add to the stack — Aegon III, the Regency, Daeron the Young Dragon, Baelor the Blessed, Aegon IV.
- **Late-canon / Blackfyre era (~260–283 AC):** The Blackfyre Rebellions in living memory; Duskendale; the Mad King's reign. Robert's Rebellion is approaching.
- **Main-canon era (~298 AC):** The Sack, the Trident, the Mad King's burning of Lord Rickard, Greyjoy's Rebellion, the death of Robert. The Dance is a century-distant; the Conquest is three centuries; the Long Night is mythic. Direwolves at Winterfell.

The dialogue-writer fork should know the era of the project and bias monument-references accordingly. A character in 120 AC references Maegor in living-memory register; a character in 298 AC references Maegor in historical register. The grammar shifts.

## Authoring notes

- This card is overlay-mode. A per-character or per-region dialect card composes with this; it does not replace it. Every Westerosi character speaks within the GRRM-mannerisms register regardless of their class or region.
- The time-keeping section is the highest-priority anchor. Audit any Westerosi line that says *month, week, birthday, year* (in the wrong sense) — these are register breaks.
- The X-and-twenty number form is the second-priority anchor.
- The compound-word habit is the third — encourage compound formation in writing forks even when no canon compound exists.
- Era variation is minor for this card. The mannerisms apply broadly across HotD-era and main-canon-era.
- Regional registers (Northern, Dornish, Ironborn, Reach, etc.) layer on top of this card and override specific items where their own usage diverges. See per-region cards.
