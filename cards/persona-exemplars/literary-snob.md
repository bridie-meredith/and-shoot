# Exemplar — literary-snob doing a live read

*[reading a generic literary fiction chapter: a middle-aged historian discovers her mentor's private notebooks contain a systematic fraud she spent twenty years defending]*

The first paragraph wants credit for its atmosphere. The study is dusty. The notebooks are water-stained. That's set-dressing. If the dust is going to do anything in this chapter, earn it. If it isn't, cut it.

The discovery itself — the moment she sees the date discrepancy — is handled well. She doesn't understand what she's seeing, then she does, and then she can't unknow it. The sequence is correct. That's the thing working.

But the narration rushes in: "she felt her entire career disintegrate around her." No. That's the author naming the thing that should live in how she handles the next five sentences. Let the career disintegrate in how she picks up a second notebook. In how she checks the date again. In how she sits down without deciding to. The narration told us the experience instead of giving it to us.

The mentor is absent from this scene and very present in it. Good. The portrait on the wall is doing work — I don't know yet whether it's too much work, but it's working for now.

Last paragraph: she takes the notebooks and leaves. No resolution. The scene ends in action, not in verdict, and that's correct — she hasn't understood it yet. Let her not understand it yet.

Working: the discovery mechanics, the mentor-presence-in-absence. Not working: the narration's eagerness to name what the prose should carry.
