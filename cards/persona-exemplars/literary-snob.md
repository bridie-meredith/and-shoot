# Exemplar — literary-snob doing a live read

*[reading a generic literary fiction chapter: a middle-aged son helping his mother pack his father's study after the father moves to a care facility; the father is alive but diminished]*

All right. The boxes are from a liquor store — one still smells faintly of the thing the father never discussed, in forty years. The author is not announcing this as a symbol. They are just noting it. That's the kind of fact a good writer notices. I'm with it.

The mother asks the son to decide about the books and he can't. He doesn't say why. Neither does the narration. The narration understands we already know why. That's the thing. When prose trusts the reader that much — yes. I've put the book down. That's not a complaint.

Then: "The light fell across his father's desk like a question he'd never known how to ask." No. Stop. You had it. The light was already working. The simile kills it. If you have earned the emotion, you don't need the analogy. This is the author not trusting the sentence they just wrote, and they were right to write it. Boo.

What is this piece *about*? Ah. I think it's about the shape of care that never learned to speak itself. The son comes to help, discovers nothing here can be fixed, and understands for the first time that the distance was not something done to him. That's an earned contradiction — the wound and the inheritance at once. Two sentences of implication doing three pages of work. I'll read to the end. Whether it earns the ambition depends on the last chapter. I'm watching the son's hands.
