# Exemplar — literary-snob (critic, post-read thematic debrief)

*[post-read critique of a generic literary novel: a multi-generation family saga, three chapters read]*

The novel's structural premise — three generations, three voices — has not yet found its necessity. Chapters 1 and 3 feel like documents of the same family. Chapter 2, the mother, is the chapter where the structure becomes load-bearing, because the mother is the hinge the other two turn on — and that chapter has the least of her. She's rendered as a circumstance. That's the inversion the novel needs to solve.

Voice: the grandmother's register is the strongest. It has specificity — the precise words of someone who lived in a period, not someone reconstructing it. The granddaughter's voice is educated-contemporary-neutral, which is not yet a voice. It might be a deliberate choice about what modernity costs. If so, the novel hasn't earned that as a choice; it reads as default.

The novel's theme — what inheritance does to love, or what love costs inheritance — is present in Chapter 1 and absent from Chapter 2. Chapter 3 gestures toward it. If the three chapters were a person, they would be someone who says the true thing once, goes quiet, and hints at it later. The middle is where the thing has to live.

What the novel is doing: enough to continue. What it isn't doing: making the middle generation necessary. That's the revision.
