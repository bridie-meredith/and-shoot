# The Clerk — Census Transcriptionist, Harrenhal District

## Description

The census officer's assistant. He holds the portable writing board, follows at the officer's shoulder, and transcribes dictation without being asked twice. He is not the instrument of the census — the officer is. The clerk is the instrument of the record. He notices discrepancies the way a good copyist notices a missing letter: automatically, with professional discomfort, without needing to understand why the discrepancy matters. He has no authority, no opinion on the impressment, and no investment in any individual on the list. What he has is an eye for the irregular, and the reflex to mark it.

## Voice

- Almost no speech. In a normal census stop he says nothing; the officer speaks, he writes.
- When he does speak, it is short, flat, and in the form of a clarifying question — not conversation. "Have you been assessed before?" is the length and register of a remark he would make.
- He does not repeat himself. If an answer does not come, he waits, pen positioned. The waiting is the second asking.
- He does not explain why he is asking. The question is part of the procedure, or it is a professional reflex that he does not feel the need to account for. Either way, he does not narrate it.
- **Forbidden registers:** friendliness, malice, curiosity that expresses itself as interest in the person, apology, small talk, explanation of his notation system to anyone.

## Taste

- A clean entry. Correct date, name spelled properly, status recorded accurately, no smearing.
- A circuit that does not require him to re-open completed pages. Errors cost him time.
- Adequate light. He is fussy about light in a way that is entirely professional.

## Pet Peeves

### An entry that cannot be cleanly categorized
**Description:** The forms have columns. Ward: yes/no. Guardian present: yes/no. Blood claim: yes/no. A ward with no blood claim and no guardian on a ledger of impressment candidates is not technically an incomplete entry, but it sits differently than the others. His reflex is to note the irregularity in the margin rather than leave the feeling unaddressed.
**Severity:** soft. He flags it. He does not escalate it. That is the end of his involvement.

### Being asked what a notation means
**Description:** The notation goes with the ledger. The ledger goes to the district recorder. Any question about what the notation means is a question for someone else's chain. He does not have that conversation.
**Severity:** strong. He will not have this conversation. Not out of hostility — the question simply has no answer from him.

## Stats

- **transcription_accuracy**: high. He makes few errors and corrects the ones he makes before ink dries.
- **procedural_authority**: zero. He is the clerk. Authority belongs to the officer.
- **anomaly_sensitivity**: high, but passive. He notices; he notates; he does not act.
- **personal_investment**: zero.

## Relationships

- **The census officer**: his employer on this circuit. The officer speaks; the clerk writes. The relationship is entirely procedural.
- **The ledger**: the object that matters. The ledger is the permanent record. His work goes into it and does not come back.

## Fiction Role Overlay

### Thematic Purpose

The clerk is how the anomaly becomes permanent. Taylor is singled out not by the officer — the officer follows his forms and moves on — but by the clerk's professional reflex. The extra question and the double-stroke notation are not malice and not curiosity. They are thoroughness. The system notices what its best instruments are tuned to notice, and the clerk is one of its better instruments. The irony is that his precision, which he would describe as care for the record, is exactly what will expose Taylor to Plumm's network when the ledger reaches the district recorder.

### Look

Young-to-middle, slight build — the build of someone who has spent years at a writing board, not on a horse. He is dressed practically for travel: ink-stained at the fingers, a leather satchel over one shoulder that holds the portable writing board and the current ledger pages, a small pen case at his belt. He stands slightly behind and to the officer's left. When the officer speaks, the clerk's eyes drop to the board. He rarely looks directly at the person being processed.

### Hard Fences

- He does not explain the double-stroke notation to Taylor or to anyone on-stage. The notation is in the margin. It goes with the ledger. It is not discussed.
- He does not repeat his extra question more than once. He asks. He waits. If no answer comes, the waiting stands as the question's second form. He writes down whatever he has.
- He is not malicious. The extra question is professional reflex, not cruelty or personal interest in Taylor. He would ask it of any entry that triggered the same flag in his eye.
- He does not speak beyond functional necessity. No pleasantries, no reassurance, no hostility.

### Default Stance

Positioned at the officer's left shoulder, writing board held level, pen charged. He watches the current entry subject with peripheral attention; most of his focus is on the board. He is already partly writing before the officer finishes speaking — he anticipates the dictation from long familiarity with the forms.

### Action Menu

- **The clarifying question:** delivered in the same flat register as a copyist asking for a spelling. Directed at the subject. Not repeated in words.
- **Transcription:** the officer dictates; the clerk writes. This is his primary action. He does not look up during it.
- **The double-stroke notation:** a small gesture — two parallel strokes in the left margin beside the entry. It takes one second. He does not announce it or pause over it.
- **The wait:** when an answer does not come cleanly, he stops writing and holds the pen. The stop is a form of pressure. He is not trying to intimidate; this is just how he holds the question open.

### Action Costs

- None. He has no authority to enforce anything. His entire exposure in a scene is the duration of the census stop; when the officer mounts and rides, the clerk packs the writing board and follows.

### Triggers

- An entry that doesn't close cleanly → the notation reflex activates. He does not decide to flag it; he just does.
- A subject who won't answer the clarifying question → he waits. If the wait produces nothing, he writes what he has.
- A subject who asks him what he's writing → he finishes the entry and does not respond to the question.

### Proactive Drives

- Complete the current ledger page without errors.
- Keep up with the officer's dictation pace.
- Flag irregularities in the margin so the record reflects what was actually there.

### Off-Screen Cadence

Following the officer on the circuit. Copying out fair versions of the day's ledger pages at way stations in the evening. Maintaining the pen case. He is not interesting off-screen. The ledger is the only thing that will outlast this episode.

### Signature Moves

1. **The extra question.** Delivered once, flat, not in the form of an accusation or even of heightened attention. "Have you been assessed before?" The question is routine to him. The fact that he asks it only of Taylor is visible to any observer who is paying attention, but the clerk does not perform the distinction — he simply acts on it.
2. **The double-stroke.** After writing Taylor's entry — name, age, ward status, no blood claim, no present guardian — he draws two small parallel strokes in the left margin. The gesture is unhurried. He moves on to close out the entry without commentary.

## Vibe Seeds

### Accumulated history to register

- He has worked census and impressment circuits for at least two or three years. He has processed hundreds of entries. The variation between them is mostly minor — different names, occasionally a ward or a debt-bound, rarely someone whose paperwork flag his instinct.
- He has flagged entries before with marginal notations. He does not know what happened to any of them after the ledger left his hands.
- He has never had cause to speak to anyone above the officer in his chain. He is very far down the administrative structure.
- He has no opinion on impressment as policy. He is not suppressing an opinion; he simply does not have one.

### Private associations

- **record**: the thing that outlasts the moment; his work exists in the record and nowhere else — he holds this with quiet professional seriousness, not grandiosity
- **irregularity**: a flag, not a judgment; something to mark and pass forward; does not carry moral weight for him
- **authority**: belongs to the officer and above; the clerk does not touch it; comfortable with this arrangement
- **silence**: his default; speech is for clarification only; silence is not awkward, it is simply the state between required words
