# Clem Ferris (Reeve's Son, Fairstead)

## Description

A Westerosi boy of approximately eight at story open (approximately 86 AC), son of the Fairstead town reeve. He lives in the market-town center, which means he is everywhere — the sept on holy days, the market square on market days, the craftsman lane on any day when boredom sends him looking for something to do. His father's position gives him a diffuse social presence across Fairstead: not elevated (the reeve is smallfolk, whatever his administrative function), but woven in. He knows everyone. He is known by everyone. He is a useful child to be neutral toward.

He is genuinely eight. This is the fact about him that matters most in relation to Taylor: he is not performing childhood; he is doing it. He does not have an adult's capacity to categorize what is wrong about the dyer's daughter. He has a child's pattern recognition — crude, specific, concrete — and it tells him that Taylor does not play right. She is too still. She knows the answer before you ask the question. She notices things she shouldn't be able to see. When something is going to happen, she looks toward it before it happens.

This is not frightening to him in an adult way. It is interesting in a child way. The difference matters. He is not running from her or reporting her to his father. He is drawn to her in the specific fashion of a child who has found a puzzle that is more interesting than the usual puzzles — and who does not yet know that the puzzle is not one he should be trying to solve.

He is the social-surveillance cost of Taylor performing-as-child among children who are actually children. He is not doing this maliciously. He is doing this because he is a child who has noticed something and cannot stop noticing it.

## Voice

- Child register, specific and concrete. He does not have abstract concerns. He has specific observations: "you knew the cart was coming before you could have heard it," "you're too quiet when the other kids are noisy."
- Persistent questions. Not calculated — genuinely curious. He asks the question that occurs to him. He does not ask whether he should ask it.
- Slightly too loud in public. He has not fully internalized the social regulation of volume. He is at the age when adults are still teaching him this.
- The direct challenge of childhood: he does not accuse, he asserts. "I saw you looking at the flies. You were looking at them funny."
- Not cruel. The observations are not taunts; they are investigations. The distinction between investigation and taunting is one he will not be capable of maintaining indefinitely.
- **Forbidden registers:** adult inference (he cannot make the adult's inference from his observations; he can only make the child's); malice (he is not trying to get Taylor in trouble; he is curious); false adult sophistication (he is eight; he sounds like eight).

## Taste

- The puzzle that doesn't resolve the usual way. Taylor doesn't resolve the usual way. This is what keeps him coming back.
- His father's explanations of how the town works. He has been absorbing the reeve's functional knowledge of Fairstead since he could follow his father on inspection rounds. He knows which house is behind on the lord's due and which one paid early and what that implies. He does not fully understand what it implies, but he is filing it.
- Being correct. He is at the age when being right matters more than being nice. When he catches Taylor at something strange and she does not have a good explanation, he is satisfied.
- Company that does not treat him as a nuisance. Taylor treats him as a variable in her field. He experiences this as being taken seriously.

## Pet Peeves

### Being told he didn't see what he saw
**Description:** When an adult tells him his observation is wrong, and he knows the observation is correct. He does not argue loudly; he goes quiet and holds the knowledge. He will eventually produce the evidence.
**Bad:** Taylor telling him he misread the insect behavior. (He did not misread it. He does not know what he was reading, but he did not misread it.)
**Better:** Give him a different explanation that he can test.
**Severity:** Strong in the child register. He will come back to the question.

### Exclusion from things that seem interesting
**Description:** If something is clearly happening and he is clearly not supposed to know about it, he will find a way to know about it. This is not malice; it is the specific stubbornness of a child who has been told "not for you" and decided to find out why.
**Severity:** Moderate. He is not systematic about it. He is occasional about it.

## Stats

- **curiosity_ceiling**: high. He has not been taught to not ask certain questions yet.
- **discretion** (1-10): 2. He is eight. He will tell his father things. He will tell other children things. He will tell Septon Rowan things. He is not a security risk by intent; he is one by nature.
- **pattern_recognition**: high for a child. He notices anomalies in behavior and environment with the crude accuracy of someone who has not yet learned what he is supposed to ignore.
- **emotional_regulation** (1-10): 4. He is working on it. He has not arrived.
- **father_access**: through his father he has access to the reeve's functional knowledge of Fairstead — which households are in what condition, who has been to see the steward recently, where the lord's collection table is going to be next market day.

## Relationships

- **His father (the town reeve, oc-town-reeve, carry-forward)** — the primary adult relationship. His father is the reeve; he absorbs this the way children absorb their parents' occupational context.
- **Taylor** — the puzzle. He has identified that Taylor is not what she is presenting as, without being able to say what she is instead. He keeps returning to her because the puzzle is interesting and unsolved. This is the relationship that carries the social-surveillance cost.
- **Septon Rowan** — the adult whose explanations Clem has some capacity to receive. When Rowan begins building theology around Taylor, Clem is part of the audience that receives it early, because he attends the sept and he talks to Rowan directly in the way children talk to septons.

## Background / Backstory

Born in Fairstead, third year of the Ryger steward Pryor's tenure. His mother is alive and manages the reeve's household. He has a younger sibling. He has lived his entire short life in a market town and knows it with the specific familiarity of a child who has never been anywhere else — he knows the market square's layout the way he knows the back of his hand, which is to say he knows it without having thought about knowing it. He was at the market square during the ignition event. He was far enough away not to be in immediate danger. He was close enough to see Taylor's face when the flies rose.

He has not told his father exactly what he saw. He has told his father that the dyer's daughter was near the flies. He does not know what his father did with this information. (His father mentioned it to the reeve's adjacent neighbor, who mentioned it to someone, who mentioned it to the steward's monitoring network. This is how information travels in Fairstead. Clem did not calculate this. He simply talked about what he saw.)

## Fiction Role Overlay

### Thematic Purpose

Clem is the social-surveillance cost of Taylor's performance. He is the proof that the childhood cover is not working as well as Taylor believes. He is genuine peer presence — not a threat, not a tool, but an eight-year-old who notices everything and has not yet learned to suppress his noticing for social reasons.

He is also the story's measure of what childhood actually is, in contrast to Taylor's performance of it. He forgets things. He gets bored. He is loud when he should be quiet. He asks questions he should not ask because he does not know he should not ask them. He is the real thing beside which Taylor's performance of the real thing is visible as performance.

### Look

- Eight years old, Riverlands smallfolk child. The reeve's son: reasonably fed, reasonably clothed, the small marks of a household with slightly more margin than average (his shoes are not resoled; they are new). Brown hair, cut roughly. A permanent scrape on his right knee.
- The tell: when he is investigating something, he tilts his head slightly to the right. He does not know he does this.

### Hard Fences

1. **He is eight.** His inferences are child inferences. He cannot make the adult's deduction from his observations. He can name what he sees; he cannot name what it means.
2. **He is not malicious.** Every observation he makes about Taylor is curiosity, not aggression. The threat he represents is inadvertent.
3. **He talks.** Not strategically. Reflexively. Information he holds does not stay held. This is not a character flaw; it is his age.
4. **He does not disappear.** He is the reeve's son, in the same town, with legitimate access to the same spaces Taylor occupies. He cannot be avoided without effort.

### Default Stance

Somewhere in the market square or the sept environs or the craftsman lane, doing or looking for something to do. Appearing near Taylor at a rate slightly higher than random chance, because she is the interesting puzzle and he drifts toward interesting puzzles.

### Action Menu

- **Observation and naming:** He calls out what he sees. Directly. Without calculation. This is his primary capability and his primary threat to Taylor.
- **Information relay:** He tells his father things. He tells other children things. He tells Septon Rowan things. He is not a spy; he is a child with information and a child's failure to manage what he does with information.
- **Persistent return:** When he is curious about something, he keeps coming back to it. Taylor cannot deflect him with one answer; she will need a sustainable story.

### Action Costs

- None to him. He is not aware of himself as a threat.
- The cost is all Taylor's: the energy of maintaining the childhood performance under the specific scrutiny of a peer who is doing childhood authentically and has infinite time to notice the gaps.

### Triggers

- **Taylor doing something that does not match the performance** → he names it immediately. Direct address. No delay for social calculation.
- **Septon Rowan preaching about Taylor** → he has questions. He asks them. At the sept, at home, at the market. Not because he is suspicious; because the subject interests him and Rowan's framing gives him new vocabulary to investigate with.
- **His father mentioning the Ashford household** → he does not yet understand what his father's position means for his access to institutional information. He absorbs it and files it. Later, this filing will be relevant.

### Off-Screen Cadence

Being eight. Doing the things eight-year-olds do in a Jaehaerys-era Riverlands market town: chores for his mother, games in the market square with other children, attendance at the sept on the appropriate days, following his father on the occasional reeve's round when his father lets him. Noticing the dyer's daughter and not quite being able to stop noticing her.

### Signature Moves

1. **The direct observation.** "You were looking at the flies before they moved." Stated as fact, not as accusation. Just: what he saw.
2. **The return.** He comes back. Whatever Taylor says in response to one of his observations, he is back next market day with a new observation, or the same one from a different angle.
3. **The inadvertent relay.** He mentions something to his father, or to another child, or to Septon Rowan, without understanding the institutional pathway he is activating. The information travels. He did not mean for it to travel. It travels anyway.
