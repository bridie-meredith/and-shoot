# Wordplay and Pun

Mechanism card for humor that derives from the language itself — exploiting the slippage between sound, spelling, and meaning. This is the sixth card class (humor/reference), not a story-facing card. Consult the dialogue-writer fork; do not cast.

---

## Mechanism

The setup introduces a word or phrase carrying one clear meaning; the turn surfaces a second meaning (a homophone, a semantic overlap, an unexpected grammatical reading, an intentional misapplication); the payoff is the simultaneous perception of both. Laughter fires on the *click* of recognition — the listener realizes they were tracking the wrong meaning and now cannot un-see the right one. Secondary engine: **superiority** — the teller demonstrates linguistic cunning; the target is the gap between surface and depth. The mechanism is purely cognitive; it requires no emotional stakes, which makes it portable across tones but also easy to misplace in high-stakes scenes.

**Note on portability:** puns are the most language-locked humor mechanism in this library. The exact words never survive translation. Every Deploy note below therefore explains how to *rebuild* an equivalent wordplay in a target language or secondary-world idiom, not how to carry the English words across. Structure transfers; phonology does not.

---

## Facet axes

- **setting** — era + place + social register in which the joke is delivered. Wordplay is register-sensitive: the same mechanism sounds differently from a court wit and a tavern drunk.
- **teller** — social role, relationship to language, relationship to the listener. Wordplay rewards literacy but is not gated by it — oral puns run on sound alone.
- **target** — `self` / `other` / `power` / `the-situation` / `no-target` (pure play). Most wordplay is `no-target` or `other`; self-deprecating puns are a softer subclass.
- **delivery** — `spoken-aside` / `retort` / `bit` / `interior` / `written`. Wordplay's payload is often in the delivery beat — the pause before the turn, the faux-innocent face after.
- **literacy-gate** — card-specific axis. Rates whether the joke requires literacy (`written-form-dependent`, `oral-only`, `either`). Useful for period settings: an oral pun on a homophone works in any era; a written pun on spelling requires a reading audience.

## Exemplars

> EX-01 [setting: ancient-greece-symposium | teller: educated-wit | target: no-target | delivery: spoken-aside]
> "Why is a philosopher like a wine jar? Both appear deep — until you tip them."
> — Why it lands: double-meaning of "deep" (profound / a vessel's depth) works in oral form; "tip" activates physical comedy against intellectual pretension.
> — Deploy: identify a word in the target language / setting that means both a respectable intellectual quality and a physical property of a mundane object; build the comparison around that double-load. Oral form; no literacy required.

> EX-02 [setting: medieval-court-western-europe | teller: court-fool | target: power | delivery: performed]
> "The king asked me what the difference was between himself and the sun. I said: the sun rises in the east, Your Majesty. You merely reign."
> — Why it lands: homophone rain/reign (oral) collapses royal authority with weather; the fool's framing appears to praise while it diminishes, insulating the teller.
> — Deploy: find a homophone in the target language where one word is a royal/political term and the other is something banal or natural. The protective framing ("I merely observed") is separable structure — reuse it.

> EX-03 [setting: jacobean-england-tavern | teller: working-class-wit | target: the-situation | delivery: spoken-aside]
> A man about to be hanged: "This business will hang me yet — I always knew I'd make a name for myself, though I hoped the rope would be looser."
> — Why it lands: "hang" shifts from idiomatic threat to literal; "loose" activates the noose while working as the mundane colloquial of "easier." Gallows context licenses the dark register.
> — Deploy: the condemned/doomed character sub-type owns this move. Find a word in the setting's idiom that has both an everyday-figurative sense and a literal-fatal sense; let the character use the figurative while the context supplies the literal.

> EX-04 [setting: 18thc-london-coffee-house | teller: pamphleteer | target: power | delivery: written]
> A satirist notes that a minister has "settled the national debt — both his own and the country's."
> — Why it lands: "national debt" pivots from state finance to personal scandal; the double-load works because both senses are factually apt.
> — Deploy: identify a formal institutional term that has an exact analogue in personal behavior; write the sentence so both referents are activated at once. Literacy-dependent (requires the reader to know both senses); not suitable for illiterate-setting dialogue.

> EX-05 [setting: 19thc-english-drawing-room | teller: society-wit | target: other | delivery: retort]
> "I find that work agrees with me — I can look at it for hours."
> — Why it lands: "agrees with" conventionally means "suits me" (as in food or climate); the punchline reveals the speaker was using it to mean "I don't disagree with its existence — from a comfortable distance." The laziness is the confession.
> — Deploy: the "enthusiastic endorsement that is actually avoidance" shape. Find a phrase in the target idiom where strong agreement or approval can be re-read as passive non-engagement. Oral or written; no special literacy required.

> EX-06 [setting: secondary-world-court-fantasy | teller: court-herald | target: no-target | delivery: spoken-aside]
> The herald, announcing the newly-raised lord's full style: "Lord of Ashfield, Warden of the Pale Crossing, Keeper of the Four Ponds, and Finder of His Own Boot — the last title, my lord, being recently earned."
> — Why it lands: inflates the cadence of heraldic enumeration then punctures it with a mundane and slightly humiliating detail; "earned" does double work (titles are granted, not earned; this one was earned by losing the boot in the first place).
> — Deploy: any secondary-world setting with formulaic title/honorific lists can run this structure. Build the list at full pompous length; the final item in the list breaks the register. The joke is about the form as much as the content.

> EX-07 [setting: 20thc-british-military-mess | teller: officer-wit | target: the-situation | delivery: spoken-aside]
> "The general has decided to let the enemy advance — he says it's more efficient; we only have to shell them once when they're close, rather than twice when they're far."
> — Why it lands: "efficient" applies the language of administration to the language of killing; the casual inversion of the military logic reads as either satire or genuine bureaucratic derangement.
> — Deploy: pick the formal register of the setting's bureaucracy (accounting, church-administration, feudal law) and apply it to a physical/violent situation. The humor is the mechanism of wrong register, which is wordplay-adjacent (semantic incongruity by register collision). Works as double-entendre when the bureaucratic word also has a physical meaning.

> EX-08 [setting: modern-workplace | teller: office-drone | target: the-situation | delivery: interior]
> He had been told the job would "grow on him." After two years, he thought of mold.
> — Why it lands: "grow on you" (idiom for gradual appreciation) is literalized into biological unpleasantness; the subject's passive acceptance of this reframing is itself a character note.
> — Deploy: idiom-literalization is the simplest wordplay engine; it needs no special vocabulary. Find any idiom in the setting's speech that can be literalized into something physical, unpleasant, or absurd. Works in interior monologue; no delivery beat required.

> EX-09 [setting: renaissance-italy-market | teller: merchant-haggler | target: other | delivery: retort]
> The merchant, told his cloth was thin: "Thin, signore? Every thread in this bolt is honest Italian wool. I will not hear 'thin' from a man who waters his wine."
> — Why it lands: "thin" is redirected from a material flaw to a moral accusation via the "watered wine" vehicle; the counter-accusation is only loosely relevant but the speed of the pivot carries it.
> — Deploy: the defensive-redirect: when insulted with a quality word (thin, cheap, slow, crooked), find a context where that same word applies to the accuser's known behavior or status. The misdirection is the mechanism; the specific word is portable to any setting.

> EX-10 [setting: ancient-china-court | teller: remonstrating-official | target: power | delivery: written]
> A memorial to the throne: "The treasury reports that the rivers run full this year. The granaries also — full of records of last year's harvest."
> — Why it lands: "full" is used twice; the second use reveals the first as ironic — the granaries are full of paper, not grain. Written form; the parallel structure (rivers / granaries) sets the trap.
> — Deploy: parallel structure is the written-pun delivery vehicle across all settings. Statement A with word W; Statement B with word W where W means something worse. Literacy-dependent; builds through repeated word in a formal parallel structure.

## When it lands / When it fails

### Lands when:
- The audience is tracking the language closely enough to catch the second meaning — requires slight attention slack in the scene (low action, conversational pacing).
- The relationship between teller and target allows cleverness without aggression — a wit among equals, or a fool with license.
- The double meaning is genuinely apt to the situation, not just lexically possible; a pun that is technically valid but situationally irrelevant reads as showing off.
- The delivery timing lets the first meaning settle before the second arrives — the pause is structural.

### Fails / misfires when:
- The scene is at high emotional stakes (grief, mortal fear, confrontation). Wordplay in those moments reads as the author breaking register, not the character being clever.
- The teller does not have license for wit in the relationship — a prisoner making puns at a judge reads as deranged or suicidal; a servant punning at a lord reads as insolence (see `westeros-smallfolk` — smallfolk do not pun upward).
- The double meaning requires information the listener does not have (e.g. a pun on a private nickname; a written pun in an illiterate setting).
- Overuse: two wordplay beats close together reads as a comedian's set, not a character's mind. One per scene is the upper bound in most settings; one per chapter is safer in high-stakes fiction.
- The pun is imported from another language without rebuilding the structure — it lands as a non-joke because the phonological trigger does not exist.

## Anti-patterns / fences

**Surface-convention fence (non-negotiable).** Only the comedic mechanism and structure transfer into a dialogue-writer's output. The exemplar content — the specific words, jokes, and setups above — does not enter the prose. A dialogue-writer fork that pulls this card learns how wordplay is built (setup → double-load → click) and constructs a fresh line native to the speaker's voice, setting, and language. Importing an exemplar joke verbatim or with thin reskinning is a hard violation the auditor flags.

**Portability fence.** Never carry the English phonology of a pun into a secondary-world setting. The Deploy notes above explain how to rebuild; follow them, or flag the anchor as not suitable for wordplay at all rather than import a broken joke.

**Punching-down fence.** A pun whose payload lands on a powerless target (a physically disabled person, a character in grief, a child being humiliated) uses the register as a cruelty delivery vehicle. The mechanism is neutral; the application is not. Flag and refuse in sympathetic-POV scenes.

**Anachronism fence.** Modern English idioms used as the base for a pun in a pre-modern or secondary-world setting. The idiom must be native to the setting. An ancient-world character cannot literalize a "growth mindset."

**Overuse fence.** One wordplay beat per scene is a ceiling for high-stakes fiction. Above that, the author's cleverness overtakes the character's voice.

## Dialogue-generation hooks

**Retrieval keys.** Pull this card when: `family:wordplay`, `delivery:spoken-aside` + `teller:wit`, `register:light`, `target:no-target`, `target:power` + `setting:court-*`, `teller:court-fool`, `literacy-gate:oral-only` + `setting:medieval-*`, `literacy-gate:written-form-dependent` + `setting:*coffee-house* | *court* | *drawing-room*`.

**Applicability test.** A wordplay beat fits the anchor when: (1) the scene is below high-emotional-stakes; (2) the teller's behavior card does not fence wit or cleverness; (3) the setting has at least one word or phrase with a productive double-load in the target language/idiom; (4) no prior wordplay beat has fired in the same scene.

**Adaptation rule.** Identify the word or idiom in the speaker's native idiom that carries the double-load. Confirm the second meaning is accessible to the listener (shared knowledge, shared language, appropriate register). Build the setup so the first meaning is the expected one; deliver the payoff so the second meaning arrives one beat later. Re-skin all content to the speaker's voice and period. Obey the speaker's behavior-card fences (e.g. smallfolk do not wit upward; a grieving character does not pun their own loss unless characterization specifically supports gallows mode).
