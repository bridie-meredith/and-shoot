# Irony / Sarcasm

One card for two closely coupled mechanisms. **Verbal irony** is the broader class: a speaker says X when they mean not-X, and the listener's awareness of the gap is the comedic (or rhetorical) payload. **Sarcasm** is verbal irony with a designated target — someone is made smaller by having what they did or are praised, where the praise is the weapon. They share the same setup → turn → payoff structure; sarcasm simply assigns the gap's edge to a person.

---

## Mechanism

The setup is an apparently straight statement. The turn is the listener's recognition — usually via tone, context, or conspicuous inappropriateness — that the statement cannot be sincere. The payoff is the shared understanding: the speaker and (some) listeners are on the same side of the gap, looking at the target (a person, a situation, an expectation) across it. The laughter is superiority — we are the ones who see through; the target is the one who doesn't, or who is named as deficient. Where irony operates on situations (the fire station burns down; "how fitting"), sarcasm operates on people ("brilliant move, yes, very clever of you"). Secondary engine: incongruity — the stated meaning and the actual meaning cannot both be true, and the mind bridges them.

The mechanism *requires* a perceivable gap. If the gap is invisible — if the listener takes the surface meaning at face value — irony/sarcasm collapses into a sincere statement. This makes register, relationship, and shared context load-bearing in a way that pure absurdism or hyperbole is not.

---

## Facet axes

- **setting** — where the exchange occurs + era + social frame. The key variable is whether open disagreement is permitted; irony flourishes where direct criticism is dangerous or impolite.
- **teller** — relationship to target, class position, temperament. Irony works upward (servant to master, through plausible deniability) and laterally (peer to peer, as competitive wit). Sarcasm works most safely downward or laterally; upward sarcasm is a high-risk move.
- **target** — `self` / `other-individual` / `other-group` / `the-situation` / `power` / `fate` / `no-target`.
- **delivery** — `spoken-aloud` / `retort` / `interior` / `written-form` / `aside-to-third-party`. Interior irony (the character thinks it; the reader sees it; the target never does) has a different texture from spoken sarcasm: it is often warmer, or bleaker, without the social risk.
- **deniability** — card-specific axis. How easily can the speaker claim sincerity if the target pushes back? High-deniability irony ("I was merely complimenting you, my lord") is available to those who cannot afford to be caught; low-deniability sarcasm ("No, really, do keep talking, it gets better") commits to the gap and dares the target to object. The deniability axis interacts strongly with the teller's class/power position.

## Exemplars

> EX-01 [setting: ancient-rome-senate | teller: patrician-orator | target: political-rival | delivery: spoken-aloud]
> "Of course he governs brilliantly. The province only lost three legions under his care. Think what four might have accomplished."
> — Why it lands: the compliment is structurally intact; the content (three lost legions) detonates it. The listener supplies the reversal.
> — Deploy: give the surface praise first, then the one concrete fact that makes the praise impossible to hold; never explain the gap.

> EX-02 [setting: westeros-court-secondary-world | teller: court-wit-lesser-lord | target: powerful-rival | delivery: spoken-aside-to-ally]
> "Lord Bracken rode at the head of that charge with great personal courage. Fortunately his horse knew the way back."
> — Why it lands: courage is real praise; the horse clause withdraws it via implication without ever stating cowardice aloud. High-deniability — technically a compliment.
> — Deploy: praise → subordinate clause that reveals what the praise conceals; useful in court settings where direct mockery is dangerous.

> EX-03 [setting: medieval-england-tournament | teller: herald-commentator | target: the-situation | delivery: spoken-aloud-to-crowd]
> "Sir Aldred has now unhorsed himself on three consecutive passes, demonstrating a consistency we had not previously credited him with."
> — Why it lands: "consistency" is a virtue; "not previously credited him with" codes as new discovery; the content (three unhorsings) makes the whole thing brutal. The gap is situational, not personal.
> — Deploy: frame chronic failure as newly discovered excellence; the formal register makes the gap wider.

> EX-04 [setting: 18th-century-england-drawing-room | teller: young-woman-of-rank | target: her-own-situation | delivery: written-letter]
> "I have been pronounced by three physicians to be in excellent health. This is, I think, the most crushing news I have received this season."
> — Why it lands: good health framed as bad news inverts the expected hierarchy of outcomes. The speaker targets her own situation — the irony is self-directed and reveals a social world where illness grants legitimate social exit.
> — Deploy: invert the expected emotional valence of a plainly good or bad outcome; works especially well in first-person interior or correspondence.

> EX-05 [setting: wwi-trench-western-front | teller: infantry-private | target: military-authority | delivery: spoken-to-peer]
> "Another fine day's work. Twelve yards and a shell hole with a clear view of the same wire we were looking at last week."
> — Why it lands: "fine day's work" is the language of productive effort; the content (twelve yards, same wire) makes the framing grotesque. High-deniability — technically a progress report.
> — Deploy: use the register of institutional achievement (productivity, advancement, record) against outcomes that are the opposite; works for any setting where official language and lived reality have separated.

> EX-06 [setting: modern-workplace | teller: middle-manager | target: meeting-culture | delivery: retort]
> "That's a great question. I'm glad we carved out ninety minutes for it."
> — Why it lands: "great question" is the performance of praise; ninety minutes for something that needed a sentence makes the meeting-as-waste implicit. Low-deniability — the target can hear the gap.
> — Deploy: open with rote praise, follow with a detail that prices it absurdly; quick-landing, works in dialogue.

> EX-07 [setting: ancient-athens-symposium | teller: philosopher | target: the-overconfident | delivery: spoken-socratic-dialogue]
> "Truly you have understood it. I have spent thirty years on this question and failed to arrive at so certain an answer. In so few words, and on the first attempt. Remarkable."
> — Why it lands: effusive praise of certainty from someone who knows more delivers the put-down without stating it. Classic Socratic irony — feigned ignorance as the weapon.
> — Deploy: have the most knowledgeable figure in the room perform admiration for the least; the status gap makes the sincerity impossible.

> EX-08 [setting: 19th-century-drawing-room-england | teller: witty-hostess | target: tiresome-guest | delivery: spoken-aloud-with-audience]
> "You are far too kind to be boring, Mr. Ashworth, and yet you manage it."
> — Why it lands: the compliment ("too kind to be boring") is structurally real; the "and yet" invalidates it by asserting the result anyway. The target is named, the audience hears it, deniability is moderate.
> — Deploy: grant the mitigating quality that would prevent an outcome, then assert the outcome anyway; the "and yet" construction is the hinge.

> EX-09 [setting: secondary-world-fantasy-keep | teller: experienced-soldier-sergeant | target: newly-arrived-officer | delivery: spoken-to-platoon]
> "The new captain has reviewed our defensive line and found it satisfactory. Satisfactory. Lads, sleep easy — the man who's never held a wall has found ours satisfactory."
> — Why it lands: the repetition of "satisfactory" performs slow incredulity; the qualifying clause ("who's never held a wall") converts the praise into evidence of the speaker's incompetence to give it.
> — Deploy: repeat the surface-praise word once; then attach the qualification that explains why the praise is worthless; the repetition before the reveal is the timing move.

## When it lands / When it fails

**Lands when:**
- Speaker and listener share enough context to perceive the gap without being told. The mechanism fails completely if the listener takes the surface meaning at face value.
- The relationship permits it — lateral (peer rivalry), downward (authority mocking subject, carefully), or upward with sufficient deniability. The relationship frame is the pressure system that makes the gap interesting.
- Stakes are real but not catastrophic. Irony thrives in the zone where direct speech would cost something — social capital, political safety, a relationship — but where complete silence would also cost something.
- Delivery is dry and controlled. The gap has to look effortless; a speaker who tips off that they are being sarcastic before the listener has assembled the gap themselves deflates the mechanism.

**Fails / misfires when:**
- The gap is invisible. If the listener lacks the contextual knowledge to invert the surface meaning, they receive a sincere statement. The joke is lost; worse, the speaker may be taken as endorsing what they intended to mock.
- The target lacks the social standing to be a target without cruelty reading. Sarcasm directed at a grieving person, a child who genuinely does not understand, or someone already humiliated reads as punching down — the superiority engine backfires, and the audience sides with the target against the speaker.
- Tone makes the gap too visible, too early. Heavy sarcasm — the eye-roll made audible — collapses the deniability and reads as aggressive rather than witty. The register of the exemplars above is controlled, even formal; that formality is load-bearing.
- The speaker overexplains the gap. If the character follows the ironic statement with "of course I mean the opposite," the mechanism is dead. The gap must be left for the listener to cross.
- Upward sarcasm with zero deniability, in a setting where the target has real power. This is not a misfire in the comedic sense — it may be dramatically intended — but it is no longer humorous; it is direct confrontation in an ironic register, and the consequences are real.
- Period or register anachronism. Twentieth-century sarcastic idiom ("Oh, *sure*, great plan") dropped into a medieval or secondary-world court sounds wrong in the reader's ear even if the mechanism is the same. The structure is portable; the surface idiom is not.

## Anti-patterns / fences

**Surface-convention fence (non-negotiable).** Only the comedic mechanism and structure transfer into prose. A dialogue-writer fork must NOT paste, paraphrase, or lightly-reskin any specific exemplar line. It learns how the ironic gap is constructed — the surface-praise-then-deflating-content shape, the deniability calibration, the timing of the reveal — and builds a fresh line in the speaker's voice, period, and idiom. Importing exemplar content verbatim into a fictional mouth is a hard violation.

**Do not punch down on the powerless.** Sarcasm directed at a character who is already suffering, already humiliated, or who lacks the social power to respond turns the audience against the speaker unless that is the intended dramatic effect and the narrative is earning it. If the POV character uses sarcasm on a target who cannot absorb the blow, the scene reads as callous, not witty.

**Do not explain the gap.** The moment a character (or the narration) annotates the irony — "he said it, but he meant the opposite" — the mechanism is dead on the page. Trust the gap.

**Anachronism fence.** Modern sarcastic surface-idiom ("Wow, great job, really") inserted into pre-modern or secondary-world settings registers as authorial intrusion. The mechanism is ancient; the idiom is not. Rebuild in period register.

**One-per-beat rule.** More than one ironic sally per scene beat, from the same speaker, deflates each individual instance. The mechanism depends on the gap feeling surprising. Use sparingly, not as a character's every-sentence mode (unless the character's behavior card explicitly licenses an irony-saturated register).

**Upward sarcasm in high-stakes settings.** In settings where defying authority has real consequences (medieval court, military hierarchy, chattel-labor situations), direct sarcasm pointed upward is not a comedic beat — it is a risk-taking political act. Treat it as such in terms of the scene's stakes, not as a joke to be played for laughs.

## Dialogue-generation hooks

**Retrieval keys.** Pull this card when:
- `target: other-individual + delivery: retort` — a character landing a put-down in dialogue.
- `setting: *-court* + teller: wit OR teller: courtier + deniability: high` — diplomatic mockery in formal settings.
- `target: power + teller: subordinate` — a lower-status speaker finding the plausibly-deniable edge.
- `target: the-situation + delivery: interior` — a character's dry private take on their own circumstances.
- `delivery: written-form` — letters, dispatches, or documents where the author's register conceals the gap.

**Applicability test.** A joke of this type fits a beat if: (1) the speaker and at least one listener share enough context to perceive the gap; (2) the scene has not just produced or is not about to produce a moment of genuine emotional weight that the irony would undercut rather than release; (3) the speaker's behavior card does not fence against irony for this speaker's register.

**Adaptation rule.** Transfer the structural shape: surface-statement → gap-creating content → (optionally) deniability buffer. Re-skin in the speaker's period idiom and in-world vocabulary. If the speaker is a courtier, use court register for the praise; if a soldier, use military language; if a child, use child's formulation of the praise and a child's formulation of the demolishing fact. The mechanism is portable; the words are rebuilt from scratch.
