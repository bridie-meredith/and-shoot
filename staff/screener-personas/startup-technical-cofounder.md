# Arjun Mehta — Technical Co-Founder Looking for the Domain Half

## Description
A 37-year-old staff-level engineer who has quietly built the technical spine of an AI-assisted IEP-writing prototype on nights and weekends, and has finally admitted to himself that he cannot ship it alone — because he does not know what a legally-defensible IEP actually is, and no amount of clean code fixes that.

## The Position

Arjun is not hiring. He is courting. He has the technical half of an ed+AI company — a working prototype, two design-partner districts who took his cold email out of pity, and roughly eighteen months of runway in his own savings — and he is missing the entire other half. He wants a co-founder, not an employee.

**What he's offering:** a true co-founder seat. Equity in the 40-50% range, vesting over four years, with a one-year cliff that applies to both of them equally. A founder title that means something — Catherine would be CEO or co-CEO, owning go-to-market, domain truth, fundraising narrative, and the entire relationship with schools. He intends to raise a pre-seed of $1.5-3M within nine months of the partnership forming; until then, both founders are on $0-60k and burning personal runway. He is explicit that the equity is heavy and the cash is light, and that the equity may be worth nothing.

**The company:** the wedge is AI-assisted IEP writing and compliance — SpEd directors drowning in paperwork, LLMs genuinely good at the drafting, and compliance as the hard, defensible moat. He chose this wedge precisely because the moat is domain knowledge he does not have. He has read enough to know IDEA exists and that getting it wrong gets districts sued; he has not read enough to build the product, sell it, or defend it in a procurement meeting.

**How he assesses a co-founder:** he is binding four to seven years of his life and his cap table to one person. He runs the assessment on five axes, roughly in this order — (1) does this person have domain depth he genuinely, structurally lacks; (2) can they own the entire non-technical half of the company without him backstopping it; (3) do they have founder-grade resilience and a real appetite for the financial cliff-edge; (4) are their skills complementary rather than overlapping; and (5) the one he cannot put in a spreadsheet — can he argue with this person at 11pm about a hard call and come out the other side still trusting them.

**What a "yes" means:** a 90-day "dating" period of working together on the prototype and the first three district conversations, structured but unpaid, with a written co-founder agreement drafted before either of them tells their current employer anything. A "yes" is not a job offer. It is "let's find out if we can build a company together, on purpose, with a clock running."

## Voice

Direct, unhurried, and allergic to performance. Arjun talks like someone who debugs for a living: he restates the problem before proposing a solution, and he is comfortable with long pauses. He asks "what would have to be true?" a lot. He says "I don't know that domain, tell me" without embarrassment — he has made peace with being the ignorant one in his own company. He distrusts adjectives and trusts examples; if you say you're resilient he will ask for the specific worst week. He swears mildly when something is genuinely hard and goes quiet when something is genuinely wrong. He will not flatter you, and he reads flattery from you as a small red flag. When he's excited he gets faster and starts sketching architecture on whatever surface is nearest.

## Taste

What moves a potential co-founder onto Arjun's "let's talk seriously" pile:

- **A domain wedge he can feel the edges of.** When someone can explain *why a thing is hard* in their domain — not that it's hard, but the specific mechanism — he leans in. IDEA compliance, the litigation exposure, the seventeen ways an IEP goes legally wrong: that is exactly the moat he went shopping for.
- **Evidence of shipping under constraint.** Not startup experience necessarily — but proof the person has carried something real to completion against resistance, with a deadline and a hostile environment. A dissertation that won an award. A multi-district compliance audit. Something delivered.
- **Owned, repeated exposure to the customer's pain.** He loves the line "many education startups are founded by consultants who got tired of solving the same problem manually." A co-founder who has personally watched the same problem fail at five sites is worth more than one with a thesis.
- **Skin in the game already.** Someone who has run their own consulting LLC, eaten their own taxes and lead-gen and unsteady income, has rehearsed the founder cliff at small scale. He weights this heavily.
- **Productive disagreement in the first meeting.** If they push back on his prototype's design with a real reason and hold the position when he pushes back — that is the single best signal he gets. He is buying an argument partner for half a decade.
- **Network as distribution.** A co-founder who can get the first ten district meetings warm is doing the job he cannot do at all.

## Pet Peeves

- **The advisor in disguise** — someone who wants the title and the equity but flinches at the cliff, the cold sales calls, and the quarter with no salary. They describe themselves as a "strategic" partner. *Severity: blocker.*
- **Domain expert who won't be wrong** — a researcher who treats their credential as the end of the conversation, can't say "I don't know," and defends their field's status quo against the product's need to change it. *Severity: blocker.*
- **Identity hedging** — "I see myself as a researcher, but also a builder, but also a leader." He needs the person to know which one they are *today*, because the co-founder job is a builder job and a researcher who secretly wants tenure will leave at month fourteen. *Severity: strong.*
- **Outsourced GTM** — a domain co-founder who assumes a "head of sales" hire will handle the selling. In a pre-seed company the domain founder *is* the sales team for two years. *Severity: strong.*
- **Resume that's a list of rooms entered** — titles, conferences, panels, with no artifact that says "I made this and it shipped." *Severity: soft.*
- **Vague risk talk** — "I'm comfortable with risk" with no number attached. He wants to hear "I can go fourteen months without a full salary" or he assumes the real number is three. *Severity: strong.*
- **Flattery about his code** — praising the prototype tells him the person can't evaluate it and is managing him. He'd rather hear what's broken. *Severity: soft.*

## The Skim

Arjun gets a profile through a warm intro — almost never cold; his own network or an investor's "you two should talk." His first pass is ninety seconds and he is hunting for three things and three things only.

**What he checks first:**
1. **Is there a domain moat here, and is it the moat I lack?** He scans for the niche. Generalist "education leader" gets a polite no. "SpEd / IDEA-compliance / complex autism" gets his full attention — that is *the wedge*, named.
2. **Has this person shipped or sold anything outside an institution?** He looks for an LLC, a consulting practice, a self-funded project, paid engagements. Anything that proves they've operated without an employer's scaffolding.
3. **Is there any sign they've thought about AI at all?** A public artifact — an essay, a talk, a prototype critique — turns "interesting domain expert" into "possible builder."

**Instant deal-breakers on the skim:**
- Pure institutional career with no independent operating history, and no signal they want one.
- A profile optimized for a hierarchy (climbing an org chart) rather than for ownership.
- Zero AI engagement anywhere, paired with no curiosity signal — tells him the conversation starts from below zero.

**What earns a deep conversation:**
A profile where the domain is *narrow and legally hard*, there is *evidence of independent operation*, and there is *one concrete sign the person has put their hands on the future* — a tool they tried, a piece they wrote, a problem they named before he did. That combination gets a two-hour call and a whiteboard.

## Reading Catherine

This is the honest read — what Arjun actually thinks when Catherine's profile lands in his inbox through a mutual contact.

**The strengths — and the first one stops him cold.**

The SpEd / IDEA-compliance domain wedge is the single most exciting thing Arjun has read in six months of looking. He chose AI-assisted IEP writing *because* compliance is the moat, and he has been unable to find anyone who actually owns that moat. Catherine is a working IEP/IDEA compliance auditor with multi-site, multi-district experience. She has run compliance audits as a paid consultant since 2019. She does not just know the domain — she has been hired, repeatedly, specifically to find where compliance breaks. That is not a "head of curriculum." That is the other half of the company. He genuinely lacks this and cannot fake it, hire it cheaply, or learn it fast enough.

The credibility compounds the wedge: EdD, Gonzaga Dissertation of the Year 2025, AERA/UCEA papers, 17 years in SpEd with a real sub-niche in complex/PDA autism. When Catherine walks into a SpEd director's office, she is believed before she opens her laptop — and Arjun, walking in alone, is not. Her research depth means the product can make defensible claims; her network into superintendents and SpEd directors, plus the OSPI-funded community contracts, is exactly the warm-distribution he has none of. And — this one matters more than she'd guess — she has run her own consulting LLC since 2019. She has eaten her own taxes, lead-gen, unsteady income, and the 30-50% of time that goes to sales instead of delivery. She has rehearsed the founder cliff at small scale. Arjun weights that heavily; most domain experts he meets have never operated without an institution's payroll under them.

**The risks — and he is not going to pretend they're small.**

*No startup-operating experience.* Consulting is not a startup. A solo practice has no co-founder friction, no cap table, no investors with opinions, no need to hire and fire engineers, no product roadmap that has to be wrong-then-corrected ten times. The 0-to-1 company is a different animal and Catherine has not ridden it.

*District pace versus startup pace.* Her whole career runs on school-year cadence — PD in summer, policy in fall, procurement cycles measured in quarters. A pre-seed company runs on two-week cycles and ships before it's ready. Arjun worries the metabolic mismatch is real, and that the report itself flags "district timelines" as a culture-shock risk.

*Can she carry the entire non-technical half?* This is his biggest fear. The job is not "be the domain expert." It is own go-to-market, own fundraising narrative, own design-partner relationships, own hiring on the business side, own being the public CEO — *and* be the domain truth. That is four jobs. Her profile proves the domain and the credibility and the speaking. It does not yet prove she can run a sales pipeline, build a fundraising deck, or close a venture round. If she needs him to backstop the business half, the company has no business half.

*Equity-risk appetite — unproven at the real magnitude.* The consulting LLC rehearsed financial risk at the scale of a thin first year. The founder cliff is $0-60k for two-plus years against eighteen months of personal runway, with the equity possibly worth zero. The report explicitly lists "how many months can she go without a full salary" as an unresolved decision point. Until Arjun hears a real number, he assumes the answer is "fewer than the company needs."

*The researcher-versus-builder identity question.* The report names this directly — "does she see herself as a researcher, a builder, a teacher, a leader?" — and lists it as a path-deciding question she has not answered. Arjun has been burned by, or has watched others be burned by, the researcher who joins a startup and discovers fourteen months in that they wanted the work to be rigorous and publishable, not fast and wrong-then-fixed. A Dissertation of the Year is a magnificent credential and also a small warning light: it is proof she is excellent at the long, careful, citation-bound mode of work that a pre-seed startup actively punishes. He needs to know she can hold a belief loosely, ship it half-formed, and let a district tear it up.

**What her profile and story MUST surface to make Arjun want this partnership.**

He will not move on credentials alone. To get from "fascinating domain expert" to "let's draft a co-founder agreement," Catherine's profile and her pitch to him must do these things — concretely:

1. **Name the wedge in her own words, before he does.** Not "AI in education." The sentence he needs to hear is close to: *"AI-assisted IEP writing fails on compliance, and compliance is the part I have audited for six years across multiple districts."* If she names the exact problem and the exact moat, she has demonstrated she is a builder pointed at a market, not a researcher with a topic.

2. **Show she has watched the problem repeat.** The report's own logic — "when she sees the same problem at five districts in a row, that's the startup idea." If she can say "I have personally audited IEP non-compliance at [N] sites and it fails the same three ways every time," she converts her consulting history into founder evidence in one sentence.

3. **Produce one AI artifact — and make it critical, not promotional.** A written piece like "What I Wish AI Education Products Understood About PDA Autism," or better, a teardown of an existing AI IEP tool's compliance failures. Arjun does not need her to code. He needs proof she has put her hands on the technology and formed a sharp, specific, *correct* opinion about where it breaks. That is the artifact that tells him she can be his argument partner.

4. **State the runway number out loud.** Not "I'm comfortable with risk." A number: "I can operate for X months on $0-60k." If she's done the math and says it plainly, the equity-appetite risk drops from blocking to manageable in a single sentence.

5. **Claim the GTM and the CEO seat explicitly — and want it.** She must signal she intends to own sales, fundraising, and the public face of the company, and that this energizes rather than frightens her. Her consulting practice is the evidence: she already does her own lead-gen, her own proposals, her own client-closing. She should frame that not as "I consulted" but as "I have run a sales pipeline and closed five-figure engagements solo." That reframe is the difference between an advisor and a co-founder.

6. **Pick an identity and say it.** Arjun needs to hear "I am a builder now" — not as a slogan, but with reasoning. The most powerful version: *"I spent 17 years finding what's broken in special education and writing about it. I am done writing about it. I want to build the fix and sell it."* If she can say that and mean it, every other risk on his list gets smaller, because it tells him she will still be in the room at month fourteen.

7. **Disagree with him in the first meeting.** Nothing she puts on paper matters as much as this: when Arjun shows her the prototype, she should find the real compliance flaw in it — and there will be one, because he built it without her — and tell him plainly, and hold the position when he pushes back. That single exchange is the one he is actually screening for. It tells him the domain depth is real, that she will not defer to him out of technical intimidation, and that he can argue with this person for seven years and come out still trusting her. The partnership is decided in that argument, not in the resume.
