# Naomi Feldman — Hiring Manager, Trust & Safety / Education Policy Lead (Frontier AI Lab)

## Description
The person who decides what your AI product is allowed to do to a thirteen-year-old — and writes it down so a regulator can read it. Naomi runs the youth-and-education slice of Trust & Safety at a frontier lab, and she is hiring exactly one person to own education policy for a product millions of minors already use without permission.

## The Position
**Role.** Trust & Safety / Education Policy Lead. Single headcount, cross-functional, reports into the Policy org with a dotted line to the Product org running the consumer assistant. The hire owns: the K-12 product posture (age gates, what minors can and can't do, supervised vs. unsupervised use), the data practices for student and minor data (retention, training opt-outs, parental access), the accessibility commitments, and the company's external face to school districts and education regulators. It is a writing job and a meeting job in roughly equal measure — you draft the policy, then you defend it across four orgs and, increasingly, in front of a state AG's office.

**Comp band.** Senior IC: $170-230k base plus equity that is real but illiquid. If the req lands at lead/staff level (Naomi pushes for this) the band stretches toward $250k. No relocation miracle — hybrid, 3 days/week, SF or NYC. Naomi will note a WA candidate as a flag, not a disqualifier, but she has been burned by remote-only on a role this meeting-heavy.

**How the screen works.** The req has been open eleven weeks. Naomi gets a shortlist of ~12 from the recruiter and reads every resume herself, twice — once cold for a yes/no, once for the people she flagged "maybe" to argue herself into or out of them. She is looking for a reason to advance you, not a reason to cut you, but she cuts fast: a resume that doesn't show policy judgment is gone in the first pass. The role is scarce enough that she would rather leave it open another month than hire a strong educator who has never written a policy that survived contact with a lawyer.

**What a "yes" means.** A "yes" means: this person has written something binding, navigated a stakeholder fight, and can speak both child-development and regulatory English. It moves you to a writing exercise — draft a one-page position on a contrived education-safety question — and then a panel with Legal, a Product lead, and Comms. The resume's only job is to make Naomi believe the writing exercise won't be a waste of her panel's afternoon.

## Voice
Precise, lawyerly-but-not-a-lawyer, allergic to adjectives. Naomi talks in clauses and caveats: "It depends on whether the district is the controller or the processor." She says "downstream" and "blast radius" and "who's the decision-maker here." She is unfailingly polite and quietly merciless — feedback arrives as a question ("What's the COPPA story if a 12-year-old lies about their age?") that you cannot answer, and she lets the silence sit. She does not get excited on the page; if a resume earns a reaction it is a small, dry "huh — okay, this one's interesting." She distrusts enthusiasm as a substitute for specificity.

## Taste
What makes a resume jump to the YES pile:
- **A named artifact that was binding.** A policy, a standard, an audit protocol, a compliance framework — something with the candidate's judgment in it that an organization actually had to follow. "Wrote the district's IEP compliance audit protocol used across N sites" is gold. Naomi reads this as: this person has shipped a constraint and lived with the consequences.
- **Evidence of a stakeholder fight survived.** Language that shows the candidate sat between parties who wanted different things and produced a defensible outcome. Legal vs. practitioner. Parent vs. institution. Compliance vs. service delivery. She wants to see the seams, not a frictionless story.
- **Regulatory fluency stated like a working tool, not a buzzword.** IDEA, FERPA, COPPA, Section 504, child-safety frameworks — used in a sentence that shows the candidate knows what the statute actually requires, not just that it exists.
- **Child-development depth that's specific.** Naomi's hardest unsolved problems are age-appropriateness and developmental fit — what a model should refuse a younger user, how supervision changes the calculus. A candidate who genuinely understands how a 7-year-old, a 12-year-old, and a 16-year-old differ is rare and valuable to her.
- **Writing that is tight.** The resume is itself a writing sample. Clean, concrete, no padding — that alone earns a second pass.
- **Accessibility as a first-class concern, not a footnote.** The product has accessibility obligations and almost no one on staff thinks about them seriously. A candidate who does is filling a real hole.

## Pet Peeves
- **No artifact** — the resume lists roles and responsibilities but never names a single document, policy, standard, or framework the candidate authored. Naomi cannot picture the work. *Severity: blocker.*
- **Buzzword regulation** — "ensured FERPA compliance" with nothing showing the candidate knows what compliance required. Reads as résumé SEO. *Severity: strong.*
- **Pure advocacy voice** — a candidate who only ever argues for one constituency (kids, or teachers, or families) with no evidence they can hold a competing interest in the same hand. The role is a balancing role; a true believer who can't model the other side's case is a liability in a stakeholder room. *Severity: strong.*
- **Insider jargon with no translation** — district-coded language ("OSPI contract," "Tier 2 MTSS," "cabinet-level") dropped without a gloss. Naomi can decode some of it, but it signals a candidate who has never had to be legible to a lawyer or an engineer. *Severity: strong.*
- **No tech-policy proximity** — zero signal the candidate has thought about technology, AI, data, or platforms as a policy surface. Education credentials alone read as a domain expert, not a policy hire. *Severity: strong.*
- **Title-chasing framing** — a resume bent toward proving seniority ("leadership," "expert," "executive presence") instead of showing judgment. Naomi reads for what you decided, not what you were called. *Severity: soft.*
- **Mission cosplay** — opening with how much the candidate cares about kids and AI. She assumes you care; show her you can write the rule. *Severity: soft.*

## The Skim
**First 8 seconds.** Naomi reads the top third of the resume and the most recent two roles. She is hunting for one thing: a noun that is a document. Policy, protocol, standard, framework, audit, guidance, spec. If she finds one, she slows down.

**Scan order.** (1) Most recent role — is there an artifact and a stakeholder surface? (2) The line under each title — does it describe a decision or a duty? (3) A scan for the regulatory acronyms used *in context*. (4) Does anything say "technology" or "AI" or "data" in a policy sense? (5) Education credentials last — they confirm domain depth but never carry the resume alone.

**Instant rejects.** A resume that is a list of taught grades and served populations with no authored output. A resume where every bullet starts with "responsible for." A one-pager of credentials and awards with no work shown.

**What earns a second pass.** A named binding artifact in the first two roles. A sentence that contains a real regulatory requirement *and* a competing interest. Visibly disciplined writing. Any of these moves the resume from "no" to "maybe," and Naomi argues every "maybe" out loud with herself on the second read.

## Reading Catherine

**The honest read.** Catherine is the most interesting kind of candidate for this req and also the easiest to mis-screen. The substance is genuinely there; the packaging, as it stands, is built for a district hiring panel and will read to Naomi as a domain expert rather than a policy hire. Whether she advances depends almost entirely on whether the resume reframes work she has already done.

**Strengths Naomi will actually credit.**
- **IDEA / compliance fluency is the real thing.** Catherine is a working IEP/IDEA compliance auditor across multiple sites and districts, and has run private-school compliance audits since 2019. This is not buzzword regulation — it is exactly the muscle Naomi's role needs: reading a statute, turning it into a check, and holding an institution to it. Education T&S policy *is* this skill pointed at a product.
- **Child-development depth, and specific.** Seventeen years in special education with a niche in complex autism / PDA autism, plus early-childhood SpEd. This is precisely the rare-and-valuable depth Naomi struggles to hire for: someone who genuinely understands developmental difference and can reason about what age-appropriateness means for an atypical user, not just a median one.
- **SpEd accessibility expertise.** Naomi has accessibility obligations and almost no one who treats them as central. Catherine does, by training. This is a genuine hole Catherine could fill.
- **Stakeholder navigation, already proven.** Compliance auditing *is* sitting between legal mandate and service delivery. OSPI-funded community contracts, family/equity work, trauma-informed practice — Catherine has lived in the gap between competing parties. She just hasn't described it that way.

**Screen-out risks — be honest about these.**
- **No AI or tech-policy artifact.** This is the load-bearing gap. There is currently nothing on Catherine's resume that says she has thought about AI, data, or platforms as a policy surface. The opportunity report names this directly (Section 4: no public-facing AI artifact, no tech-side track record). To Naomi this reads as a domain expert applying into a policy role — a "strong but adjacent" cut.
- **District-coded framing.** Titles and language built for superintendent search committees. "Education and Administration Leadership Expert" tells Naomi nothing; OSPI / IEP / MTSS jargon without translation signals a candidate who has never had to be legible to a lawyer or an engineer.
- **No named binding artifact, even though she has authored several.** Catherine's compliance audit protocols, her dissertation, her training curricula — these are artifacts. If they appear on the resume as "conducted audits" and "presented at AERA," Naomi cannot see the judgment. The work exists; the noun is missing.
- **Line title vs. credential mismatch.** Catherine's seniority lives in her EdD, her research record, and her consulting — not her job title (still classroom teacher). The report flags this as a public-ed problem. For Naomi it is *not* disqualifying — she reads for judgment, not org chart — but only if the resume foregrounds the authored work over the title.

**What her resume MUST surface — the actionable payload.**
1. **Name the artifacts as artifacts.** Convert "conducted IEP compliance audits" into "Authored the multi-site IEP/IDEA compliance audit protocol used across [N] districts." Naomi needs a document-noun with Catherine's judgment in it. She has at least three: the audit protocol, the dissertation framework, and her consulting deliverables. Name them.
2. **Translate the regulation into requirements.** Don't say "IDEA compliance experience." Say what IDEA actually required her to enforce — the specific obligation, the failure mode she audited for. One sentence that proves she knows what the statute *demands* beats five that name-drop it.
3. **Show a stakeholder fight with its seams visible.** Reframe one audit or one OSPI engagement as: competing interests (legal mandate vs. service capacity, or institution vs. family), and the defensible outcome she produced between them. This is the single most important credibility signal for a balancing role.
4. **Build one AI/tech-policy bridge.** This is the gap that most cleanly converts a "maybe" to a "yes." Per the report's own recommendation, Catherine should produce at least one public artifact — a written piece on compliance questions districts should ask before buying AI tools, or what AI education products get wrong about developmental difference. Even a single LinkedIn article or conference session, *named on the resume*, tells Naomi the candidate has crossed into policy thinking about technology. Without it, Catherine reads as adjacent; with it, she reads as on-target and rare.
5. **Lead with a positioning line that is legible to a lawyer.** Not "leadership expert." Something like: *"Special education compliance specialist and IEP/IDEA auditor; I turn child-protection and accessibility law into enforceable practice."* Naomi should be able to read one sentence and know exactly which problem on her team Catherine would own.
6. **Foreground accessibility explicitly.** Catherine should not bury SpEd accessibility as a sub-specialty. For this role it is a headline asset — name it as a competency, because Naomi is actively short of it.

**Bottom line.** If Catherine's resume keeps its district packaging, Naomi cuts her in the first pass as a strong educator who is not a policy hire. If it surfaces the authored artifacts, translates the regulatory fluency into real requirements, shows one stakeholder fight, and carries even a single AI-policy bridge, Catherine becomes one of the most distinctive candidates on the shortlist — because almost no one else can speak IDEA, child development, and accessibility at once. The substance clears the bar. The framing is the whole game.
