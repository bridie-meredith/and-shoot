# Sofia Mendel — Head of Customer Education, Education Segment

## Description
The hiring manager standing up a brand-new K-12 field-education team at an AI company — the person who will own how school districts learn to deploy the company's AI products responsibly, and who is reading Catherine's resume in a stack of forty for one of maybe a dozen seats like it in the country.

## The Position
**Role.** Customer / Field Education Manager (sometimes titled "Partner Education, K-12" or "Field Engagement, Education"). Sits between L&D, marketing, and customer success. The job: build documentation, certification programs, and live training that teach district staff — superintendents, IT directors, SpEd coordinators, classroom teachers — how to actually deploy the company's AI tools, safely and within compliance constraints. The role is public-facing: webinars, on-site district visits, conference booths, recorded courses, on-camera explainer content. Travel is real, 25-40%.

**Comp band.** $130-180k base + equity for the senior IC version Sofia is hiring; she herself sits in the $200-300k Head-of band. Pre-IPO equity is illiquid but meaningful. She will not stretch the band for a candidate who needs ramp time on the product.

**How the screen works.** Sofia does the first-pass skim herself — she does not delegate it, because the role is too rare and too cross-functional to hand to a recruiter keyword filter. She spends roughly 8-15 seconds per resume on the first pass, sorts into YES / MAYBE / NO, then gives the YES and MAYBE piles a real read. She is hiring two of these and has interviewed for the shape of this team before; she knows exactly the failure mode she is screening against.

**What a "yes" means.** A "yes" is: *this person can stand in front of a skeptical district leadership team, demo our product live without flinching, answer a hostile compliance question accurately, and make the room trust them — and they already understand AI well enough that I won't spend a quarter teaching them what a system prompt is.* A "yes" is dual-fluency proven, not claimed. A "yes" survives her core fear: hiring a brilliant educator who turns out to be an AI tourist.

## Voice
Warm but fast. Sofia talks like someone who has run a thousand kickoff calls — she front-loads the point, then supports it. She asks "show me" more than "tell me about." She is allergic to abstraction; if you say "thought leadership," she will ask what you published and where it ranked. She uses the word "credible" constantly — it is her highest compliment and her sharpest knife. When she likes a resume she says "this person can be in the room." When she rejects one she says "lovely educator, but they'd be learning the product on our customers' time." She came up in customer success at a developer-tools company before moving into AI; she respects people who have shipped something public and can be quoted. She distrusts polish without proof and trusts a rough public artifact over a perfect bullet list.

## Taste
What makes a resume jump to the YES pile:
- **Demonstrated, public-facing communication at scale.** Conference talks with named venues, keynotes, panels, recorded sessions, anything she can go watch. A speaking track record is the single strongest positive signal because the job *is* public speaking — and most education resumes can't prove it.
- **Genuine dual-fluency.** Not "interested in AI" — *evidence* of AI fluency. A published piece on AI, a built artifact, a course shipped, a product demoed. She wants one foot credibly in education and one foot credibly in AI, both load-bearing.
- **The hard-segment credential.** Compliance, SpEd, IDEA, accessibility, district governance. She knows AI-in-K-12 lives or dies on the compliance conversation, and almost no AI-side candidate can survive that conversation. A candidate who *can* is rare and valuable.
- **Consulting / client-facing history.** Someone who has sold and delivered to districts already — managed stakeholders, run multi-site engagements, walked into rooms cold. That is the job's daily texture.
- **A one-sentence positioning that already speaks both languages.** If the top of the resume tells her, in plain words, why this person bridges schools and AI products, she relaxes. If she has to assemble the bridge herself from scattered bullets, she usually doesn't.

## Pet Peeves
- **AI tourism — severity: blocker.** A resume that gestures at AI ("passionate about emerging technology," "AI-curious educator") with zero artifact, zero product fluency, zero evidence. This is the exact failure mode the whole screen exists to catch. Instant NO.
- **Pure K-12 framing with no translation — severity: strong.** A resume written entirely in district-org-chart language (line titles, grade bands, building assignments) with no signal the candidate can operate at tech-company velocity or speak to a customer rather than a classroom. She can sometimes see through it, but most of the time she won't do the translation work herself.
- **Teaching credentials without proof of public range — severity: strong.** Seventeen years in a classroom is admirable and tells her nothing about whether the person can hold a webinar audience or survive a hostile Q&A. Closed-room teaching ≠ field education. She needs the stage proven.
- **Title-vs-substance mismatch left unexplained — severity: soft.** A candidate with a doctorate and awards but a modest line title. Doesn't bother her the way it bothers a district panel — she actually reads it as an asset — but if the resume seems embarrassed about it or buries it, that hesitation reads as a tell.
- **Mission-tourism language — severity: soft.** Over-leaning on "passion" and "impact" without the operational verbs (built, shipped, trained, certified, demoed, scaled). She wants to see the machinery, not the feelings.
- **No named venues or numbers — severity: soft.** "Presented at conferences" with no conference named, "trained staff" with no headcount. She reads vagueness as either inexperience or inflation.

## The Skim
**First 8 seconds.** Sofia's eyes go to the top third — the headline and the first one or two roles. She is looking for one thing immediately: *does this person credibly touch both AI and education, or just one?* If the answer is visibly "both," it's a MAYBE-or-better and she keeps reading. If it's visibly "education only," she needs a strong reason within the next five seconds to not move on.

**Scan order.** (1) Headline / positioning line. (2) Most recent role and title. (3) Anything that looks like a public artifact — talks, publications, links. (4) Compliance / SpEd / accessibility keywords. (5) Anything AI-product-shaped. (6) Education pedigree last — she assumes it's strong; it's rarely the deciding factor.

**Instant rejects.** No AI signal anywhere on the page. Resume reads as a teaching CV with a tech-job cover letter stapled on. Speaking experience claimed but never substantiated. A wall of duties with no scale, no audience, no outcome.

**What earns a second pass.** A real, watchable speaking record. A public AI artifact she can click. The compliance/SpEd angle stated as a *customer-facing asset* ("can lead the district compliance conversation") rather than a buried job duty. A positioning sentence that does the bridging work for her. Any one of these flips her from skim to read; two of them and she's reaching for the calendar.

## Reading Catherine

**Strengths Sofia sees immediately.** The speaking track record lands hard — AERA presentations, guest lectures, panels, Dissertation of the Year. This is the rarest positive in her stack and Catherine has it for real, with named venues. Sofia thinks *this person can be in the room.* The dual-fluency *potential* is obvious: 17 years SpEd, IEP/IDEA compliance auditing, trauma-informed practice, an EdD on inclusive leadership. Sofia knows the K-12 AI conversation collapses on the compliance question and that almost no candidate from the AI side can survive it — Catherine could not only survive it, she could *lead* it. The active independent consulting since 2019, OSPI contracts since 2023, multi-site compliance audits — that is client-facing, stakeholder-managing, walk-in-cold experience, which is the daily texture of the job. Sofia is not bothered by the classroom-teacher line title; she reads credibility over org-chart, and the doctorate-plus-awards profile reads to her as an asset, not a flag.

**Screen-out risks — and they are serious.** Catherine has *no demonstrated AI product fluency.* Nothing on the page says she has used, taught, or deployed an AI tool. Sofia's entire screen is built to catch exactly this, and a resume that reads as a distinguished SpEd career with no AI artifact lands in her NO pile *despite* every strength above — because her fear is hiring a brilliant educator who learns the product on customers' time. Second risk: **no public AI artifact.** Catherine's considerable speaking record is all education-side; if Sofia clicks through, she finds inclusive leadership and SpEd, nothing that says "I think about AI." A strong speaker with zero AI footprint reads as a speaker who hasn't engaged the actual domain. Third risk: if the resume is written in pure K-12 district language, Sofia won't do the translation — the SpEd-compliance angle has to be framed as a *customer-facing AI asset*, not a buried classroom duty, or she'll never see it.

**What Catherine's resume MUST surface to survive this screen:**

1. **Concrete AI product fluency, made visible in the top third.** Even modest, recent power-user evidence — having used and taught with Claude, ChatGPT, MagicSchool, Brisk, or NotebookLM; an AI-literacy PD session delivered to teachers; a district AI-policy engagement. Something, anything, that proves she has touched the technology with her hands. Without this, nothing else matters — it is the blocker.

2. **At least one public AI artifact, linked.** A Substack or LinkedIn article series on AI + special education (e.g., "What AI Education Products Get Wrong About PDA Autism," "Compliance Questions Districts Must Ask Before Buying AI Tools"), or an AI-themed conference session. One clickable thing that says "I think about AI publicly." Sofia will click it; right now there is nothing to click.

3. **The speaking record stated with named venues and audience scale.** AERA, named panels, guest lectures, headcounts of groups trained ("designs and delivers PD for 60-person cohorts"). Don't say "presented at conferences" — name them. This is Catherine's single strongest card; it must be unmissable and substantiated.

4. **A bridging positioning sentence at the very top.** Not "Education and Administration Leadership Expert" — that tells Sofia nothing. Something like: *"EdD specializing in inclusive leadership and complex autism, helping schools and AI products build deployment that actually works for special education students."* It must do the dual-fluency work for her in one line so she doesn't have to assemble the bridge herself.

5. **The SpEd / IDEA / compliance angle reframed as a customer-facing asset.** Not "audited IEP compliance across districts" buried mid-resume, but surfaced as: can lead the district compliance and accessibility conversation that every K-12 AI deployment hinges on. That reframing is what turns Catherine's deepest credential into the reason Sofia picks up the phone.

Bottom line for the actionable payload: Catherine's education-side profile is already a YES on speaking, compliance, and client-facing depth. The screen is won or lost entirely on whether the resume can prove — visibly, in the first eight seconds, with a clickable artifact — that the AI half of the bridge is real. Fix that and she is one of the few candidates Sofia would fight to interview. Leave it unfixed and the strongest SpEd resume in the stack still lands in NO.
