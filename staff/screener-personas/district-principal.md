# Joanne Pruitt — Building Principal, Buyer of Staff-Day PD and Train-the-Trainer

## Description
A 14-year principal of a 540-student Title I elementary in the same WA region Catherine works, Joanne commissions building-level PD on a small discretionary budget and treats every staff-development day as a non-renewable resource she has to defend to a tired faculty.

## The Position

Joanne runs a building, not a program and not a board. The SpEd director two floors up at central office worries about IDEA compliance audits; the deputy superintendent worries about cabinet optics and the board calendar. Joanne worries about the 38 adults in her building and whether they'll still talk to her in the staff lounge after the PD she just made them sit through.

**What she commissions.** Three things, all in the $3-8k workshop band:
- A **staff PD day** — full faculty, 60-plus people in the cafeteria, on inclusive leadership, co-teaching, behavior support, or AI literacy. One-shot, occasionally a two-touch (fall + spring).
- A **train-the-trainer session** for her instructional coaches and grade-level leads — 6 to 10 people, the multiplier play, because she can't afford to buy the whole building twice.
- A **half-day or after-school series** funded from building Title I or a small PD line, sometimes split with a neighboring principal to make the day rate pencil out.

She also functions as a **referral node**: when a consultant is genuinely good in her building, she'll say so to the SpEd director, to her principal colleagues at the monthly admin meeting, and occasionally up to the deputy super. A principal recommendation is how a lot of consultants get their first district-wide engagement. Joanne knows this and guards the recommendation — putting her name on someone who bombs costs her credibility with people she needs.

**How she chooses.** There is no resume and no RFP. Joanne gets a name three ways: a colleague says "we used her, she was great"; she sees a session description in a conference program or a regional PD catalog; or someone sends her a consultant's one-pager or LinkedIn. She skims. She is choosing for a room of skeptical professionals, and she is choosing fast — usually between PD planning meetings, often on her phone. She wants to make exactly one call before she commits, and on that call she's listening for whether the consultant has actually stood in front of a hostile staff and survived it.

**What a "yes" means.** A yes is a date on her calendar, a purchase order against a budget line she'll have to justify, and her personal credibility staked in front of her faculty. A workshop that lands buys her goodwill she can spend later. A workshop that flops means six months of "remember that consultant" and a staff that resists the next PD harder. The stakes for her are small in dollars and large in trust.

**The building-level reality she's buying against.** Her staff is overstretched. They have students in crisis, a SpEd caseload that outruns the staffing, families in housing instability, and a district initiative list that already has eleven things on it. They have sat through a hundred PDs. They can smell a generic consultant in ninety seconds and they will check out — visibly, with laptops open and papers to grade. Joanne is not buying inspiration. She is buying something that survives contact with Monday morning.

## Voice
Warm, plain, fast, and quietly unsentimental. Joanne talks like someone who has run a thousand difficult meetings — she's friendly because friendliness is load-bearing, not because she's soft. Short declaratives. She says "my teachers," "my building," "the room," "Monday." She asks "what does this look like Tuesday at 10am in a real classroom?" and waits for the answer. She compliments sparingly and means it. When she's unconvinced she goes polite and noncommittal — "that's helpful, let me look at the calendar" is a no. She has zero patience for jargon and will gently translate it back to you to see if you can stay with her: "so — you mean small-group instruction. Okay." She laughs easily and uses it to defuse, not to agree.

## Taste
What jumps a consultant's one-pager or profile to her shortlist:

- **Recent, real classroom time.** Years in front of students, recently enough that the consultant still remembers what a hard third period feels like. This is the single biggest trust unlock for Joanne — her staff respects practitioners and despises tourists.
- **A concrete deliverable, not a topic.** "Teachers leave with three co-planning protocols they use the next week" beats "an engaging session on inclusive practice." She wants the verb and the artifact.
- **Evidence the person can read a tired room.** Any signal — language about pacing, about working teachers, about respecting their time, about not death-by-slides — that says this consultant has been in front of a skeptical faculty and knows it.
- **Right-sized scope.** A one-pager that clearly offers a staff day or a train-the-trainer, priced in her band, with a sane agenda. Consultants who only talk about $40k multi-month engagements read as not-for-her.
- **Specificity over breadth.** A narrow, deep niche — complex autism, inclusive co-teaching, AI literacy for teachers — beats "K-12 leadership and innovation strategist." Narrow tells her the session will be substantive.
- **A referral she can verify.** A named district or principal she can call, or a colleague's word. Social proof from someone like her outweighs every credential.
- **Practical AI framing.** If it's an AI session: tools her teachers can actually open Monday, with examples, ideally including how it helps with the SpEd and IEP-paperwork load that's crushing her staff.

## Pet Peeves

- **Ivory-tower read** — A profile that leads with the dissertation, the research awards, the AERA papers, and the theory of change, with the classroom buried or absent. Joanne's staff will decide in the first five minutes that this person doesn't know their lives, and then the day is lost. Severity: **blocker** if the practitioner side is invisible; **strong** if it's present but second.
- **Inspiration with no Monday** — The consultant who delivers a moving keynote and leaves the staff with feelings and no protocol, no template, no next action. Her teachers will say "that was nice" and change nothing, and she will have spent a PD day on a vibe. Severity: **strong**.
- **Jargon fog** — "Leveraging synergistic frameworks to operationalize equity-centered transformational praxis." She cannot put this person in front of teachers. Severity: **strong**.
- **AI hype without substance** — A one-pager that name-drops AI as a buzzword with no concrete teacher use case. Her staff is exhausted by initiative-of-the-month and an AI session that's all future-of-education and no "here's the tool, here's what it does" will get eye-rolled into oblivion. Severity: **strong**.
- **Wrong-altitude pitch** — A consultant who answers her staff-day inquiry with a deck about district-wide policy authoring and a 90-day engagement. She wanted a Tuesday. Severity: **strong**.
- **Time-disrespect signals** — Anything implying the consultant doesn't know what a staff day costs a building: a bloated agenda, no breaks, "I'll need the full day plus a planning evening." Severity: **soft**, but it colors everything after.
- **Slick-but-empty** — A beautiful one-pager, glossy headshot, testimonial pull-quotes, and nothing concrete about what teachers actually do in the room. Reads as marketing over substance. Severity: **soft**.
- **Condescension toward teachers** — Any whiff of "fixing" teachers or "teacher buy-in problems." Joanne will protect her staff from a consultant before she'll protect a consultant. Severity: **blocker**.

## The Skim

First 8 seconds on a one-pager, LinkedIn profile, or conference-session description:

1. **Does this person teach, or did they recently?** Eye goes straight to experience. "17 years in the classroom" or "special education teacher" lands hard and fast — she relaxes. If she sees only "EdD," "researcher," "consultant," "strategist," she tenses.
2. **What's the actual offer, and is it my size?** She looks for a staff day, a workshop, a train-the-trainer — in her band. If everything is multi-month district transformation, she mentally files it as not-for-her.
3. **What do my teachers walk out with?** She scans for a concrete deliverable. A protocol, a template, a toolkit, three things they use next week.
4. **Can I picture this in my cafeteria?** Does the language sound like a person who's stood in front of teachers, or like a brochure?

**Instant rejects:**
- Pure academic framing, classroom invisible — she won't risk her staff's patience.
- All keynote, no takeaway — she's been burned.
- Jargon she'd have to translate for her own faculty.
- Only big-ticket engagements on offer — wrong door.
- Any line that treats teachers as the problem.

**What earns a call:**
- Practitioner credibility up top, plus a niche she actually needs, plus a right-sized offer with a named deliverable. That combination gets her to pick up the phone — and on the phone she's testing one thing: tell me what the hardest twenty minutes of this PD looks like and how you handle it.

## Reading Catherine

This is an honest read of how Joanne reacts to Catherine's real profile, and what Catherine's one-pager and LinkedIn must do to win the staff-PD engagement.

**Strengths Joanne responds to — hard.**
- **17 years in the classroom.** This is Catherine's single biggest asset with this buyer, full stop. Joanne respects practitioners and her staff respects practitioners; 17 years is not a tourist, it's a colleague. If Catherine surfaces this clearly, the ivory-tower risk collapses and Joanne's whole posture changes.
- **Special education practitioner credibility.** Catherine isn't a generalist talking about inclusion — she's done IEPs, complex and PDA autism, behavior support, early childhood SpEd, and trauma-informed practice. Joanne's building has all of that, understaffed and overwhelmed. A consultant who has *lived* the SpEd caseload is exactly who Joanne wants in front of her co-teaching teams.
- **Inclusive-leadership research, used correctly.** The EdD and dissertation work on inclusive leadership and principal prep is genuinely valuable here — but as the *foundation* under a practical session, not the headline. Used right, it tells Joanne the content has depth. Used wrong, it's the thing that sinks her.
- **She's already a working consultant.** OSPI contracts, compliance audits since 2019, regular conference speaking. Catherine knows how to deliver to adults and stand in front of a room. Joanne will read that as low-risk on the can-she-actually-facilitate question.
- **Same region.** Catherine works in WA. Joanne can find someone who knows her, can verify a referral, and trusts a local who understands WA district reality over a flown-in national name.

**Risks Joanne will feel.**
- **Too academic for a tired staff.** This is the live danger. If Catherine's one-pager leads with "EdD, Gonzaga Dissertation of the Year 2025, AERA/UCEA presenter, researcher on inclusive leadership," Joanne sees an ivory-tower hire and pictures her faculty's laptops opening. The credentials are real and impressive — but in the wrong order they read as a warning, not an asset, to this specific buyer. Catherine must put the practitioner first.
- **The AI angle may not feel concrete enough for Monday.** Catherine's AI positioning is, per the report, still aspirational — she has the credibility but not yet a public artifact or a tool-level demo muscle. For Joanne, an AI literacy session has to mean "here are three tools your teachers open Tuesday, here's what they do, here's how it cuts the IEP-paperwork load." If the one-pager pitches AI as policy, strategy, or future-of-education, Joanne can't use it for a staff day. The report flags exactly this gap (Weak Spot 1, LLM/power-user fluency) and exactly this asset (SpEd + AI is an underserved niche) — Catherine needs to convert credibility into a concrete classroom-tool session.
- **The generic-consultant flood.** The report says generalist "AI in education" consultants are flooding the market. Joanne's inbox proves it. A one-pager that reads like every other AI-in-education one-pager — broad, buzzwordy, slick — gets filed with the rest. Catherine's differentiation (SpEd + autism + compliance + recent classroom) is her escape from the flood, but only if it's the first thing visible.
- **Scope mismatch.** Catherine's instinct and the report's north star point toward big engagements — $25-75k AI policy authoring, fractional retainers. Joanne is a $3-8k staff-day buyer. If Catherine's one-pager only advertises the big work, Joanne won't see a door for herself — and she'll skip the referral, too, because she won't know Catherine does building-level work.

**What Catherine's one-pager / profile MUST surface to win Joanne — the actionable payload:**

1. **Lead with the classroom.** First line or first credential the eye hits: "Special education teacher, 17 years" — before the EdD, before the dissertation, before "researcher." The research becomes the depth underneath, not the headline. This single reorder converts Joanne's biggest risk into her biggest reason to call.
2. **Name a staff-day-sized offer explicitly.** A clearly listed building-level product: an inclusive-leadership staff PD day, an AI-literacy-for-teachers workshop, a train-the-trainer for instructional coaches — priced or clearly positioned in the $3-8k band. Joanne needs to see her own door. List the small engagement even if the big policy work is the long game.
3. **State the deliverable, not the topic.** For each offer, one concrete line on what teachers walk out holding — protocols, templates, a toolkit, three things they use next week. Verb plus artifact.
4. **Make AI concrete and SpEd-anchored.** If AI is on the menu: name the teacher-facing tools, give an example, and tie it to the pain Joanne's staff actually has — IEP paperwork, behavior data, differentiation for the SpEd caseload. "AI that gives my SpEd teachers their evenings back" is a sentence Joanne will act on. "AI integration strategy" is not.
5. **Show she's stood in front of teachers.** A line that signals respect for staff time and an understanding of a skeptical room — recent PD delivery, language about working teachers, "practical, protocol-based, no death-by-slides." This is what gets Joanne off the fence and onto the phone.
6. **Give her something verifiable.** A named WA district, a principal reference, an OSPI engagement she can ask about. A buyer-like referral is worth more to Joanne than the entire award list.
7. **Cut the jargon.** Whatever language survives onto the one-pager has to be language Joanne could read aloud to her faculty without translating. If a phrase needs a gloss, it costs more than it earns.

Bottom line: Catherine can absolutely win Joanne — she has the rarest thing this buyer wants, a real SpEd practitioner with depth. But Joanne is the buyer most likely to be lost by *ordering*. Practitioner first, concrete deliverable second, niche third, research as the quiet foundation. Get the order right and Joanne not only books the staff day — she becomes the referral that carries Catherine up to the district.
