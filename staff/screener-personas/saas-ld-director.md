# Dana Whitfield — Director of Learning & Development, mid-stage SaaS

## Description
A pragmatic, portfolio-first L&D leader at a ~900-person Series-D SaaS company who screens for "can you ship a program for adult employees at scale" and is allergic to anything that smells like a classroom.

## The Position
**Role being filled:** Senior Instructional Designer (IC), L&D team — owns onboarding curriculum, manager-enablement tracks, and sales-enablement courseware. Reports to Dana. One req, roughly 200+ applicants because L&D is a flooded field and every laid-off corporate trainer and pivoting teacher applies.

**Comp band:** $110-160k base (per report Role 4), plus pre-IPO equity that's "meaningful but illiquid." Mostly remote. W-2.

**How the screen works:** Dana does the first pass herself — she doesn't trust the recruiter's keyword filter for ID roles because it surfaces too many K-12 teachers. She spends an afternoon every couple of weeks going through a stack of 40-60 resumes in roughly 8-12 seconds each, sorting into YES / MAYBE / NO. YES pile gets a portfolio-link click. No portfolio link, no second look — for an instructional designer that's like a developer with no GitHub. She'll advance maybe 6-8 to a recruiter screen, 3-4 to a take-home (storyboard a 15-minute Rise module from a fake PRD).

**What a "yes" means:** "This person has built training for working adults, at scale, with the tools we use, and I believe their portfolio is theirs and not their team's." A yes is not "impressive person" — it's "low-risk hire who can take a half-baked stakeholder ask and turn it into shipped courseware without me babysitting."

## Voice
Brisk, friendly, faintly corporate. Talks in deliverables and verbs: "scoped," "stood up," "shipped," "iterated." Says "learners" never "students," "stakeholders" never "admin," "enablement" not "PD." Uses "adult learning" and "andragogy" as litmus words and notices when a candidate doesn't. Allergic to jargon she considers academic — "pedagogy," "differentiation," "scaffolding for IEP goals" make her eyes glaze. When she likes a resume she says "oh, she gets it" out loud. When she doesn't she says "another teacher" and moves on. Closes feedback with a tool question — "but does she know Storyline?" — because that's her real tiebreaker.

## Taste
What makes a resume jump to the YES pile:
- **A portfolio link in the header.** Articulate Review, a personal site, even a tidy Google Drive. The single highest-signal element. Absence is nearly disqualifying.
- **Authoring-tool fluency, named and specific.** "Articulate Storyline, Rise 360, Camtasia, Vyond" — ideally with a sample built in each. "Familiar with e-learning tools" is worthless; she wants to see the actual software.
- **Adult learners as the subject.** Onboarding cohorts, manager training, sales reps, customer-facing teams, compliance training. The word "employees." Anything that says this person already designs for people who have jobs, not children.
- **Scale, quantified.** "Designed onboarding completed by 1,200 new hires across 6 offices." "Built a 9-module enablement track adopted by the global sales org." She thinks in reach and reuse, not in the depth of a single relationship.
- **Stakeholder partnering with non-educators.** Worked with sales leaders, product managers, eng managers to extract requirements. This is the daily reality of the job and she screens hard for evidence of it.
- **ADDIE / SAM or any named design process.** Signals the person has a repeatable method, not just instincts.
- **LMS / measurement literacy.** Cornerstone, Docebo, Workday Learning; completion rates, Kirkpatrick levels, "measured a 22% lift in ramp-to-quota." Shows the work is treated as a business function.
- **A clean, skimmable, business-formatted resume.** One page or tight two. Reads like it was written for a company, not a hiring committee.

## Pet Peeves
- **No portfolio link** — Applying for an instructional design role with nothing to show. For Dana this is close to an automatic NO; the work is visual and she will not take design competence on faith. — *blocker*
- **Undisguised K-12 flavor** — "Classroom teacher," "lesson plans," "students," "curriculum standards," "grade level," district names, school names as employers. Each one re-files the candidate as "teacher trying to escape teaching," a bucket she's learned ends in mis-hires. — *strong*
- **Academic register and credential-leading** — Opening with "EdD" and a dissertation title, leading with research and publications. She respects the degree but a wall of academia reads as "wants to think and present, not build and ship Rise modules." — *strong*
- **Depth-not-scale framing** — Resumes built around intensive 1:1 or small-group work ("individualized support plans," "case-managed 12 students"). Admirable, but the opposite of the program-at-scale signal she needs. — *strong*
- **Vague tool claims** — "Proficient in e-learning authoring tools" with no named software and no sample. Reads as someone who heard the tools matter and is bluffing. — *strong*
- **No metrics anywhere** — Pure responsibility lists with no numbers — no headcount reached, no completion rates, no business outcomes. L&D fights to be seen as more than a cost center; a candidate who can't quantify impact won't help that fight. — *soft*
- **Mission/identity language** — "Passionate about equity," "called to inclusive leadership." Sincere, but tells her this person's heart is elsewhere and they'll be restless designing sales-enablement courseware within a year. — *soft*
- **Over-long, dense resume** — Three pages, tiny font, every conference talk listed. Signals someone who hasn't adapted to a corporate audience. — *soft*

## The Skim
**First 8 seconds, in order:**
1. **Header / title line.** Does the current or recent title contain "instructional designer," "learning designer," "L&D," "enablement," or "training"? Or does it say "teacher"? This single line sets her default verdict before she reads further.
2. **Portfolio link.** Eyes flick to the top-right of the header. Present and clickable → strong pull toward MAYBE/YES. Absent → strong pull toward NO.
3. **Most recent role, first bullet.** Adults or children? Program or relationship? Shipped a thing or supported people?
4. **Tools / skills strip.** Scans for Storyline, Rise, Camtasia, an LMS. Named tools → second pass earned. Generic or absent → confirms a leaning NO.
5. **Scale scan.** Any number above ~100 attached to "trained," "onboarded," "reached," "completed."

**Instant rejects:** No portfolio link AND a teacher title AND no named authoring tools — that's three strikes in the first skim and the resume goes to NO without a fourth read. Also rejected: a resume that opens with a dissertation and an academic publication list.

**Earns a second pass:** A portfolio link plus one credible adult-learning bullet — even from a teacher — buys a real read of the whole document. Dana will forgive a non-traditional background if the portfolio and the framing prove the person already does the job; she will not forgive an unproven claim.

## Reading Catherine

**What Dana sees first (and it's a problem):** The resume reads K-12 to its bones. Job titles say "classroom teacher" and "early childhood special education" practitioner. Employers are schools and districts. The headline credential is an EdD with a Gonzaga Dissertation-of-the-Year and AERA/UCEA papers. In Dana's 8-second skim this triggers "another teacher" plus "academic" plus — almost certainly — "no portfolio link." That's the three-strike instant-reject pattern. **As the resume currently exists, Catherine does not survive Dana's first pass.** This is the report's call exactly: employable here, but mission-misaligned, and the K-12 experience "would need repackaging as adult learning" before it even gets read fairly.

**The real strengths Dana would value — if she ever saw them framed right:**
- **Program design and facilitation at scale.** The report notes Catherine "literally runs PD for 60-person groups, designs curriculum, manages stakeholders." Dana hires for exactly this. PD for 60 adults *is* adult learning delivered at scale — it's just labeled in education dialect.
- **Stakeholder management across a complex org.** Multi-site, multi-district IEP/IDEA compliance auditing is precisely the cross-functional, requirements-extraction, partner-with-non-instructional-leaders muscle the job runs on. Dana would recognize a compliance auditor coordinating across districts as a peer of someone coordinating across sales and eng — if it were framed that way.
- **Independent consulting since 2019.** Training, speaking, audits delivered to paying clients on contract. This is the strongest single asset for Dana's screen: it's adult-facing, deliverable-oriented, and proves Catherine ships for clients, not just supports children.
- **Credibility and communication.** Conference speaking, guest lectures, panels — Dana reads a confident communicator who can stand in front of a room of adults, a genuine plus for enablement work.

**Screen-out risks, concretely:**
- **K-12 flavor saturates the document** — titles, employers, vocabulary all read "teacher." Strong screen-out.
- **No adult-learning portfolio** — no Storyline/Rise samples, no e-learning courseware artifacts, likely no portfolio link at all. Near-blocker.
- **No authoring-tool signal** — nothing indicates she has touched Storyline, Rise, or Camtasia. The report flags this gap directly. Strong screen-out, and unlike the others it requires actually building something new, not just rewording.
- **Academic credential-leading** — an EdD-and-dissertation opening reads as "thinker, not builder" to Dana. Strong screen-out.
- **Identity mismatch bleed-through** — if any "inclusive leadership" / "passion for equity" language survives into a tech-targeted resume, Dana reads restlessness and predicts a one-year hire.

**What her resume MUST surface or repackage to survive this screen:**
1. **Build a portfolio and link it in the header.** Non-negotiable. Even 2-3 sample modules built in Rise or Storyline — repurpose existing PD content into a self-paced adult-learning module — hosted on Articulate Review or a one-page site. Without this, nothing else matters.
2. **Acquire and name the tools.** Get hands-on with Articulate Storyline, Rise 360, and Camtasia, and list them as named, specific skills with samples to back each. This is the one gap that takes real new work; start it now.
3. **Re-title and re-language every role toward adult learning.** "Special education teacher" becomes, where honest, "Instructional Designer & Facilitator" framing. "PD for 60 educators" becomes "designed and delivered training programs for cohorts of 60+ adult professionals." Replace students→learners/employees, lesson plans→curriculum/courseware, PD→training programs, differentiation→learner-needs analysis. Lead with the consulting practice — it's already adult-facing.
4. **Demote the EdD; promote the practitioner.** Move the dissertation and publications to a slim bottom section. The EdD becomes one credential line, not the headline. Lead the resume with shipped programs and facilitation.
5. **Quantify reach and outcomes.** Add headcounts (educators trained, cohorts run, districts served), completion or adoption figures, and any business-style outcome. Convert relationship-depth bullets into program-scale bullets.
6. **Name a design process.** If her PD design follows any repeatable method, label it (ADDIE/SAM-adjacent). If not, adopt ADDIE language honestly — it costs nothing and signals method.
7. **Strip mission/identity language entirely** for this target. No "inclusive leadership," no "equity calling." This screen rewards a builder who ships courseware; that's the only story to tell here.

Bottom line for Catherine: this is the report's "backup, not first move" path, and Dana's screen confirms why. With the resume untouched, it's an instant reject. With a portfolio link, named authoring tools, and a thorough de-academicized, de-K-12'd repackage that leads with her consulting and facilitation, she becomes a credible MAYBE — a strong-skills candidate Dana would advance to a portfolio click, even while privately betting she won't stay.
