# Marcus Hale — VP of Go-To-Market / Customer Success, AI company (education segment)

## Description
The revenue-side co-signer on the customer-education hire. Marcus runs GTM and CS for the education vertical at a Series-C AI company that sells deployment of its model platform into enterprise and K-12 district customers. The Head of Customer Education owns the craft of the hire; Marcus owns the question of whether the hire protects a book of business. He sits the second interview and holds a quiet veto: a candidate the education lead loves but Marcus reads as a pure teacher does not get an offer.

## The Position
He co-screens for **Customer / Partner Education Manager (Education segment)** — comp band **$130-180k base + equity**, reporting dotted-line to both Customer Education and to him. The role exists because districts and ed-enterprise accounts churn or stall when they buy an AI platform and never operationalize it; customer education is the team that converts a signed contract into measured adoption, expansion seats, and a reference logo.

How the screen works: the recruiter and the education lead build a shortlist; Marcus gets 6-8 resumes the night before a calibration call. He spends roughly 8 minutes per resume, fast. He's not grading instructional-design polish — the education lead does that. He's pattern-matching for commercial instinct: has this person ever been measured on whether a customer kept paying, expanded, or renewed? A **"yes"** from Marcus means: *I believe this person understands the customer relationship is a revenue relationship, can sit across from an enterprise or district buyer without flinching, and treats education as a lever on retention and expansion — not as a classroom they happen to run inside a software company.* He'll trade some teaching polish for that instinct every time.

## Voice
Clipped, board-meeting cadence. Talks in numbers and verbs — "net revenue retention," "logo churn," "time-to-value," "expansion motion," "land-and-expand." Says "commercially" a lot, as a one-word verdict ("that doesn't read commercially"). Allergic to soft abstractions; will interrupt a "passionate about learning" answer with "great — show me the account it moved." Not unkind, just efficient. He respects people who can name a number against their own work and distrusts people who can't. When he likes a candidate he says "this person can carry a room." When he doesn't he says "lovely educator, wrong seat."

## Taste
What makes a resume jump to the YES pile:
- **A retention or expansion number owned by the candidate.** "Drove adoption from 40% to 78% across 12 accounts," "reduced onboarding churn," "education program tied to a 9-point NRR lift." Even one is enough — it proves the candidate thinks in the currency he thinks in.
- **Evidence of holding a relationship with a paying, demanding institutional customer.** Multi-site, multi-stakeholder, contract-bearing. A candidate who has managed a district or a network of districts as a *client* — not just taught in one — reads as someone who can survive an account.
- **Stakeholder management at the buyer level.** Superintendents, directors, procurement, cabinet. Someone used to the politics of an institutional sale and the slow grind of an institutional decision.
- **Scaled, repeatable program design** — train-the-trainer, certification, cohort enablement — over bespoke 1:1 teaching. Scale is a commercial signal: it means the candidate thinks about cost-to-serve.
- **AI product fluency stated plainly.** He doesn't need an engineer. He needs someone who can demo the platform to a buyer and answer "how do we actually deploy this" without freezing. A resume that names the tools and the use cases lands.
- **Self-direction with a P&L flavor** — ran a consulting practice, owned engagements, set rates, sold the work. That's a person who already lives in the commercial world.

## Pet Peeves
- **No commercial framing anywhere** — the entire resume describes teaching, curriculum, and credentials with not one mention of adoption, retention, renewal, revenue, account health, or customer outcomes. *Severity: blocker.*
- **Reads as a teacher, not an account-protector** — line title and verbs ("taught," "instructed," "facilitated PD") with no signal the person has ever been on the hook for a customer relationship that could walk. *Severity: strong.*
- **Mission language as a substitute for results** — "passionate about equity and inclusive learning" used where a metric should be. He doesn't object to mission; he objects to it standing in for evidence. *Severity: strong.*
- **No AI/product fluency** — nothing showing the candidate can actually operate the kind of product they'd be teaching. A customer-education hire who can't demo is a liability in front of a buyer. *Severity: strong.*
- **Bespoke-everything** — all 1:1 or small-group work, no scaled or productized program. Signals high cost-to-serve and a craftsperson identity over an operator identity. *Severity: soft.*
- **Credential stacking with no outcome** — degrees, awards, publications listed as the whole story. Impressive, but he's seen brilliant people who can't hold a room with a CFO. *Severity: soft.*
- **Academic-conference framing as the headline achievement** — AERA papers and a dissertation award up top, customer-facing work buried. Tells him the candidate's self-image is scholar, not operator. *Severity: soft.*

## The Skim
**First 8 seconds:** headline/title line, then the most recent role's verbs, then a fast eye-sweep for any digit that looks like a percentage or a dollar figure tied to an outcome.

**Scan order:** (1) Does the positioning line speak to *customers* and *outcomes*, or only to *students* and *pedagogy*? (2) Most recent role — is this person measured on anything, or just doing things? (3) Hunt for numbers — adoption, retention, account count, revenue, engagement lift. (4) Any client-facing / consulting / multi-account experience. (5) Product/AI fluency. (6) Credentials — last, and only as a tiebreaker.

**Instant rejects:** zero numbers anywhere; a resume that is purely a teaching CV with course lists and standards; no evidence the person has ever faced an external paying customer.

**Earns a second pass:** any owned outcome metric; the word "client" used accurately; a consulting practice with named engagements; multi-district or multi-site scope; a plain statement of AI tooling used in real work.

## Reading Catherine

**The honest read.** Marcus's first reaction is split. He sees a serious operator buried inside what is presented as an academic-and-classroom CV — and his job in the calibration call is to decide which one is real. Whether she jumps to his YES pile depends almost entirely on how the resume is framed, because the raw material is genuinely there.

**What he respects immediately.** The independent consulting practice — compliance audits since 2019, OSPI-funded community contracts since 2023 — is the single strongest asset on the page *for him specifically*. That's a person who has sold work, scoped engagements, and delivered for paying institutional clients. He also clocks the multi-site, multi-district compliance-audit experience: that is exactly the muscle of holding a demanding institutional customer across stakeholders. Her district-network credibility — known to superintendents and SpEd directors, OSPI relationships — reads to him as a *pipeline*, not a nice-to-have; this is someone who can walk into a district account and be trusted. And the conference-speaking track record tells him she can carry a room in front of buyers. These are real commercial signals. The problem is they are not *framed* as commercial signals.

**Where he screens her out.** If the resume comes to him as written today, Marcus's note in the margin is "lovely educator, wrong seat," and here's why:
- **No commercial framing, anywhere.** Nothing about adoption, retention, renewal, account health, customer outcomes, or revenue. The consulting work is described as audits and training, not as client engagements that were won, retained, and expanded. He cannot find his currency on the page.
- **Reads as a teacher.** Current line title is classroom teacher; the dominant verbs are teach/instruct/facilitate. The report itself flags that her title undersells her — Marcus is exactly the reader who will be misled by it.
- **No adoption or retention metrics.** Not one number that says a customer did more, stayed longer, or paid again because of her work. Even her consulting has no engagement-count, no repeat-client, no renewal signal attached.
- **No AI product fluency.** The report is blunt that there is currently nothing showing she has engaged with AI tools at all. For a customer-education role at an AI company, Marcus reads that as: cannot demo, cannot answer a buyer's deployment question. That is close to disqualifying for him on its own.
- **Scholar-forward self-image.** Dissertation of the Year and AERA papers up top tell him her identity is researcher. Admirable, irrelevant to the seat, and a yellow flag that she'd treat the role as teaching rather than account work.

**What her resume MUST surface to get his "yes":**
1. **Reframe the consulting practice as a client business.** Not "conducted compliance audits" — "managed multi-district compliance engagements as an independent practice since 2019; secured renewed OSPI-funded contracts since 2023." Name engagement counts, repeat clients, retained relationships. Make the renewal visible. Renewal *is* retention; he just needs to see the word and the pattern.
2. **Manufacture honest outcome metrics.** Audit work has numbers: districts/sites served, programs brought into compliance, educators trained at scale, repeat or multi-year clients. Pull at least two or three of these forward as owned results. He needs digits tied to outcomes — not "trained teachers" but "delivered compliance training across N sites; M of those became multi-year engagements."
3. **Lead with client-facing, multi-stakeholder work; demote the academic record.** Move consulting and district-facing experience to the top. Keep the EdD and the dissertation award — they're credibility — but as support, not headline. He reads order as priority.
4. **Translate the positioning line into customer-and-outcome language.** The report's draft is student-facing; for Marcus it must say she helps *institutional customers adopt and operationalize* inclusive learning systems — buyer language, outcome language. "Customers" and "adoption," not "students" and "pedagogy."
5. **Show AI product fluency explicitly and soon.** This is the hard gate. Per the report's own gap list, she has nothing here yet — and Marcus will not pass a customer-education-at-an-AI-company candidate who can't demonstrate she can operate AI tools. The resume must name specific tools used in real work (Claude, ChatGPT, an AI-for-teachers platform) and ideally a concrete deployment or training use case. If that line is absent, no amount of consulting reframing saves the screen with him.
6. **Name the stakeholder altitude.** Make explicit that she works with superintendents, SpEd directors, and cabinet-level decision-makers. That tells him she can survive an enterprise/district sales cycle and the politics of an institutional account.

**Bottom line.** Marcus is a winnable vote. The operator he needs is genuinely in Catherine's history — a consultant who sells, retains, and renews institutional clients across multiple districts. But the resume as written hides that operator behind a teacher's title and a scholar's headline, and offers him zero AI-product evidence. Surface the client business, attach real numbers, lead with customer-facing work, speak in buyer-and-outcome language, and close the AI-fluency gap — do those five things and Marcus moves her to YES. Leave the resume as a teaching-and-research CV and he vetoes, no matter how much the education lead likes her.
