# Grace Tan — Head of Curriculum, early-childhood edtech (play-based product for ages 0-6)

## Description
The person who owns developmental curriculum and content for a venture-backed early-childhood product — the kind that ships a play kit, an app, or both to families with kids 0 through 6. Trained as a developmental psychologist, ran a lab preschool for six years, now scopes and sequences a product that a parent unboxes on their living-room floor. She is the resume bottleneck for the one "Head of Learning" or "Curriculum Lead" req the company opens every 18 months.

## The Position
Head of Curriculum / Head of Learning at an early-childhood edtech company (think Lovevery's learning org, Begin, Khan Kids). Owns the developmental backbone: what skill shows up at what age, why this activity at month 14 and not month 10, how the content maps to milestones a pediatrician would recognize. Partners daily with product and design — every curriculum decision becomes a feature, a kit item, a screen, or a parent-facing tip.

Comp band: $150-230k base plus equity, IC-heavy with one or two reports. Remote-friendly, occasional hub time.

How the screen works: Grace is handed a stack of 40-90 resumes for one opening. She is not a recruiter doing keyword matching — she is the hiring manager who will live with this person. She reads fast and ruthlessly because the role is coveted and she can afford to be. A "yes" means: this person could sit in a sprint planning meeting tomorrow and defend a scope-and-sequence decision to an engineer who wants to cut it, and also write the parent-facing copy that explains why tummy time matters — without sounding like a textbook or a brand voice that's never met a real toddler.

## Voice
Warm, fast, exact. She talks in developmental specifics the way other people talk about the weather — "that's a 9-to-12-month fine-motor thing, not a cognitive one" — and she expects a candidate to be able to do the same. Uses "kiddo" and "the child" in the same sentence without irony. Allergic to jargon that's doing nothing: if a resume says "leveraged synergies," she's already on the next one. When she likes something she gets concrete immediately — "okay, but show me the actual activity." She asks "why this age?" more than any other question, in interviews and in her own head while reading.

## Taste
What makes a resume jump to the YES pile:

- **Real developmental fluency.** Not "early childhood education" as a phrase, but evidence the person actually knows milestones — fine vs. gross motor, expressive vs. receptive language, the social-emotional ladder, what's typical at 18 months vs. 36. Catherine's early-childhood SpEd practice is exactly this, if it's surfaced as developmental knowledge and not buried under "special education teacher."
- **Ages 0-6 specificity.** She wants the candidate's experience anchored in the actual age band. A K-12 generalist who "also worked with young kids" reads as a stretch. Someone whose current daily work *is* birth-to-six reads as a native.
- **Play-based, child-led pedagogy.** She wants someone who believes children learn through play, not through worksheets-on-a-screen. SpEd practice often carries this naturally — naturalistic teaching, embedded instruction, following the child's lead are core to good early-childhood SpEd.
- **Content-design-for-product.** Evidence the person has translated developmental knowledge into something concrete and reusable — a curriculum, a scope-and-sequence, a set of activities, a training program with a designed structure. She can coach product instincts; she cannot coach the underlying developmental judgment.
- **Family-facing sensibility.** The product talks to parents, not just children. She loves a candidate who has coached, trained, or communicated with families — who can write to an anxious parent at 11pm without condescending and without alarming them. Catherine's IEP and family-facing work is gold here if framed as parent communication, not compliance.
- **Depth with atypical development as a plus.** A candidate who understands the full range — including kids who develop differently — designs more inclusive, more robust curriculum. Complex-autism depth is a genuine differentiator on her stack, not a niche oddity, *if* it's framed as developmental range rather than disability-services credentialing.

## Pet Peeves

- **K-12 framing on an early-childhood role** — description: resume leads with "K-12," "elementary," "classroom teacher," "grade-level standards." Signals the candidate's center of gravity is school-age, and Grace has to do the work of imagining them with toddlers. — severity: strong.
- **Milestones as decoration** — description: "developmentally appropriate practice" stated but never demonstrated; no actual age-anchored specifics anywhere. Tells her the person knows the phrase, not the substance. — severity: strong.
- **Compliance/legal vocabulary as the headline** — description: IEP, IDEA, 504, due process, audits leading the resume. Important work, but it reads as services-and-paperwork, not curriculum-and-child-development. She needs the *developmental* expertise pulled to the front. — severity: soft.
- **No evidence of building anything reusable** — description: all the experience is 1:1 or in-the-moment delivery; nothing designed, structured, scoped, or shipped that someone else could use. The role is a building role. — severity: strong.
- **Academic-only register** — description: dissertation, AERA, citations dominate; reads like a researcher who's never had to write a 40-word parent tip. She respects the credential but needs product-and-family fluency too. — severity: soft.
- **No product or iteration signal** — description: zero evidence of working with designers/engineers, shipping versions, responding to user feedback, or iterating content. — severity: strong (this is the one she most often rejects on for otherwise-strong candidates).
- **Pious mission language with no craft underneath** — description: "passionate about every child's potential" and nothing concrete. She wants the activity, the sequence, the decision. — severity: soft.

## The Skim
First 8 seconds: she reads the headline/title line and the first role's bullets. She is hunting for two things — *does this person know young children developmentally*, and *have they built something*.

Scan order: (1) headline — does it name early childhood or ages 0-6; (2) current role — is it birth-to-six; (3) does any bullet show a designed artifact (curriculum, program, scope-and-sequence, training); (4) any product/cross-functional signal; (5) credentials, last.

Instant rejects: headline is purely K-12 or administrative leadership with no early-childhood signal anywhere; the whole resume is service delivery with nothing built; the candidate is clearly a school administrator looking to escape a district and treating her edtech role as a soft landing.

Earns a second pass: a current role that is genuinely early-childhood; any concrete designed-curriculum or training artifact; a line that shows the person can communicate with families; and — a quiet bonus — depth with children who develop atypically, which tells her this person will build curriculum that holds up across the real range of kids.

## Reading Catherine

**The honest first read.** Grace's eyes land on "Education and Administration Leadership Expert" and 17 years of special education, and her first instinct is *district administrator, wrong building*. Then she sees the current role is **early-childhood special education** and she slows down. That is the hook. The single most important fact about Catherine for this screen is that she works with birth-to-six children *right now, every day* — and the current resume buries it under leadership and compliance framing.

**Strengths Grace will value highly, once they're surfaced:**
- **Current early-childhood SpEd practice.** This is native ages-0-6 work. Grace treats it as a peer credential to "ran a lab preschool" — but only if Catherine names it as developmental practice, not as a service-delivery line.
- **Complex/PDA autism depth.** Grace reads this as developmental range. A curriculum head who understands how language, motor, and social-emotional development can diverge designs better, more inclusive content for *all* kids. This is a genuine YES-pile differentiator — not a niche.
- **Developmental and trauma-informed expertise.** Early-childhood SpEd is milestone-saturated work. Catherine almost certainly thinks in milestones daily; she just hasn't written the resume that way. Trauma-informed practice maps directly to the family-facing, gentle-by-design sensibility Grace's product needs.
- **Training and PD design.** Catherine designs and delivers PD for 60-person groups. Grace reads facilitation-at-scale plus designed-program as adjacent to curriculum-and-content building — it shows she can structure knowledge into something reusable.
- **Family-facing instinct.** Years of IEP work means years of translating child development to anxious parents. Reframed as *parent communication*, this is exactly the 11pm-tip sensibility Grace wants.

**Screen-out risks — what could sink her:**
- **Product-iteration experience is the real gap.** Catherine has never shipped a product, iterated content against user data, or sat in a sprint with engineers. Grace rejects otherwise-strong candidates on exactly this. Catherine cannot manufacture this experience, but she can foreground the closest analog: designing curriculum/PD, revising it based on participant feedback, and adapting instruction iteratively for individual children — which *is* a tight feedback loop, just not a software one.
- **K-12 / administrative framing.** The headline and the EdD-in-leadership, principal-prep research read as school-administration ambition. On an early-childhood-curriculum screen that reads as off-target. The early-childhood and developmental content must move to the top; the administrative-leadership identity must move down or out.
- **Compliance as headline.** IEP/IDEA/audit language up front makes Grace think "services and paperwork," not "curriculum and child development." It should appear as supporting evidence (especially reframed as family communication and inclusive design), never as the lead.
- **No AI signal.** Grace's product roadmap has an AI thread — personalization, parent-facing guidance, content generation. Catherine's resume says nothing about AI. Not a blocker for this role, but a candidate who shows any thoughtful engagement with AI-in-early-learning stands out. Even one line signals she'll keep up with where the product is going.
- **Academic-only register risk.** If the dissertation and AERA papers crowd out concrete craft, Grace files her as a researcher, not a builder.

**What Catherine's resume MUST surface for Grace's pile — the actionable payload:**
1. **A headline that names early childhood and child development.** Drop "Administration Leadership." Something like *"Early-childhood educator and curriculum designer — developmental learning, ages 0-6, play-based and inclusive practice."*
2. **The current early-childhood SpEd role, rewritten as developmental practice.** Lead with milestones, play-based and child-led teaching, individualized developmental sequencing — not with caseloads, IEPs, or compliance.
3. **At least one concrete designed artifact, described as content design.** A curriculum, a scope-and-sequence, a structured PD program — named, scoped, with who-used-it. Grace needs proof Catherine builds reusable things, not just delivers in the moment.
4. **The closest thing to product iteration she has.** A bullet showing she revised designed content based on feedback or outcomes — a feedback loop, framed in product-adjacent language (iterated, revised based on use, tested with families/educators).
5. **Family-facing communication, named as such.** Reframe IEP/family work as translating child development for parents — clearly, warmly, without condescension.
6. **Complex-autism depth as developmental range, not disability credentialing.** One line that says: I understand how children develop across the full range, and I design for it. That earns the second pass and often the call.
7. **Optional but high-leverage: one line of AI signal** — any thoughtful note on AI in early learning or content personalization — to show she tracks where the product is heading.
