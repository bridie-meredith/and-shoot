# P13 unspoken-word

LOGLINE: A war-witch who unmade an army with a single word now teaches grammar to children to ensure the word can never be spoken again — until one of her students is born already knowing it.

LENS: penitential

## Premise trajectory (5-component path)
- motivation: Sera teaches grammar in a village school not from calling but from sentence: the Word burned through her and she has spent seventeen years building a cage for it out of rules, curricula, and children who will never know what they are being taught to avoid.
- anchor: The child Pell — six years old, arrives in her classroom midyear, and on the third day writes a word in chalk that Sera has spent seventeen years keeping out of every classroom she has taught.
- escalation: Pell does not know the word is dangerous; cannot be made not to know it; and will speak it aloud in the ordinary course of a six-year-old's day unless Sera intervenes — but every intervention risks teaching Pell what the word does.
- trade: Sera can suppress the word in Pell's reach by destroying Pell's access to language entirely — cutting the child off from speech and writing — which is the same act as the war, performed at smaller scale on one child.
- irony: Sera's seventeen years of teaching grammar to prevent the word was itself a ritual act that attracted Pell, who was born in the resonance of Sera's containment-work; the cage called the thing it was built to hold.

## World rules (the magic-tech that makes the engine work)
The Word is a grammatical structure, not a phoneme — it is a syntactic pattern whose meaning is "unmake the present tense of a noun." Spoken correctly at sufficient volume and intent, it strips the referent from the world: the army Sera unmade did not die, they ceased to have been soldiers. Survivors remember them but the men are simply gone, their slots in the world empty. The Word cannot be spoken accidentally — it requires knowledge of what it does, intent to speak it as the Word, and a specific cadence that only emerges in the speech of someone with native grammatical fluency in the old tongue. Pell was conceived the night Sera finished her first grammar curriculum — in the village nearest to that school, from parents who speak only the common tongue. Pell has no known connection to the old tongue. But Pell writes the Word in chalk the way children write their own name: as something they already know.

## Substance signature
- state_axes: Sera: moral-framework under continuous stress (penitence-as-purpose vs. penitence-as-self-deception); legibility shifts as her cover identity as schoolteacher frays. Pell: capability axis as the live threat — a child who does not know she is a weapon is not a safe child.
- cost_ledger: Sera keeps Pell in school past dismissal for "extra lessons" — Pell's mother comes to the school angry; Sera's cover story thins. Sera removes all written materials from the classroom — the other students' parents demand explanation; Sera's school tenure is reviewed. Sera confronts what she knows she must do and cannot: silence Pell permanently without a second unmaking.
- antagonist_pressure: Round 1 — Pell writes the Word; Sera erases it, says nothing; Pell writes it again the next day. Round 2 — the regional grammar-warden arrives for a routine curriculum review; Pell's work is in the pile; Sera has twenty minutes to remove it before the warden opens the folder. Round 3 — Pell, frustrated by Sera's inexplicable restrictions, whispers the Word to another child during recess to teach them "the special word Ms. Sera won't let us write."
- dramatic_shape: Setup — Pell writes the Word; Sera sees it and erases it; the reader understands what Sera understands. Escalation — Pell keeps producing the Word spontaneously, in different exercises, as if it is the only word she knows. Midpoint reversal — Sera realizes the Word in Pell is not dormant knowledge — Pell is using it the way a child uses language, generatively, producing new instances; the cage has been breached from inside. Cost spike — the grammar-warden finds one instance; Sera destroys the folder and resigns her post in the same afternoon. Climax — Sera finds Pell on the road outside the village, about to demonstrate "the special word" to a group of older children. Sera steps between them and does the only thing she has not done in seventeen years: she speaks the Word herself, into the air, with no referent — using it up in a sentence about nothing, burning it empty, the way you fire a weapon into the ground to make it safe. Aftermath — Sera is deaf. Pell is fine. The older children heard a sound they cannot describe. Sera walks back toward the school.
- dramatic_shape (curve): Setup — the chalk word. Escalation — Pell generates new instances daily. Midpoint reversal — the Word is active in Pell, not dormant. Cost spike — the warden, the folder, the resignation. Climax — Sera speaks it empty on the road. Aftermath — the price of the solution and what Sera does with it.

## Protagonist arc
Sera begins as a woman who has organized her entire life around not using a thing she used once. She ends having used it again, deliberately, to neutralize it, sacrificing the last of her hearing — the sense most implicated in what the Word does — to make it safe in another person's mouth. The single decision: she steps in front of Pell on the road, opens her mouth, and speaks.

## Beat outline (4-6 beats)
1. Pell, third day of school, writes a word in chalk during a spelling exercise; Sera erases the board, her hand steady, and assigns a new exercise; she does not look at Pell's face.
2. Sera stays late and reviews six weeks of Pell's prior work from the village school — the Word appears in seventeen exercises, each time in a grammatically correct sentence about ordinary things: "The dog unmakes the yard." "My mother unmakes the bread."
3. The grammar-warden arrives; Sera removes Pell's folder from the review pile and destroys it in the school stove; the warden asks why she's burning paper in June; Sera says mold.
4. Pell, refused paper and chalk for two days, scratches the Word into her desk with a nail; Sera sees it at end of day, sits down at the desk, and puts her forehead against the wood.
5. Sera resigns, packs her room, and finds Pell on the road to the next village, walking with three older children, about to show them the word that gets teachers upset.
6. Sera speaks the Word into the air between Pell and the older children, aiming it at nothing — at a patch of empty road — and it fires; the road is unchanged; Sera's ears fill with silence; the children stare; Pell looks at Sera and says "that's my word."

## Concrete engine (DEC-0115 fence)
A six-year-old writes a grammatical construction in chalk on a school board, and the woman who taught herself to build seventeen years of lesson plans around its absence sees it and erases it with her sleeve. The child writes it again the next day. The woman resigns her post, walks out to a road, opens her mouth, and speaks the construction aloud at a patch of dirt — the same act that removed forty thousand men from the world, performed on nothing, to spend the bullet. Her ears stop working. The dirt is unchanged.

## Novelty claim
The magic-as-grammar conceit has precedent (Le Guin's True Names, others), but the specific engine — a syntactic pattern that unmakes the present tense, spent rather than wielded at the climax, by the person most trying to bury it — and the road-to-hell that the containment-work itself called the inheriting child, are a fresh configuration. The self-deception of penitence-as-prevention is the sharpest axis.
