# P19 life-vote-senate

LOGLINE: In a senate where votes are cast in years of remaining life, the youngest senator wins every motion by spending recklessly, and must choose between her cause and the months she has left to see it pass.

LENS: political

## Premise trajectory (5-component path)
- motivation: Elia, 24, was elected on a famine-relief bill that the older senators will not pass; she has thirty years of life ahead and she will spend them if that is what it takes.
- anchor: The famine itself — specific, ongoing, measurable in child deaths per week — not an abstraction but a count she updates every morning on a sheet she carries in her coat.
- escalation: Each vote she wins she survives, but the senate has procedural tools to force repeated votes on the same bill, forcing her to spend again; the opposing bloc has learned to force revotes rather than negotiate.
- trade: The bill passes — or will pass, incrementally, motion by motion — but Elia burns through her life doing it, and the bill's implementation requires an advocate still alive to administer it.
- irony: Elia's willingness to spend her life is the opposition's most effective weapon against her — they force spending on procedural motions that don't move the bill, knowing her cause requires her alive at the end.

## World rules (the magic-tech that makes the engine work)
Each senator carries a life-ledger: a notarized physician's estimate of remaining years, updated annually. To vote on a motion, a senator physically signs a withdrawal slip specifying a number of years, which are taken immediately — the senator ages visibly over the following hours, the years drawn from future capacity, not from memory or past. You do not lose time you have lived; you lose time you would have lived. The physical toll is proportional: a one-year vote costs a senator roughly what a year of hard labor and illness would cost the body. A five-year vote leaves a senator looking five years older by morning. Signing zero is abstention. The youngest senators have the most life to spend and are therefore the most powerful voters on contested motions — historically, senior senators held power because they held institutional knowledge, but the life-vote senate has systematically elevated youth as a military resource. Elia has understood this since she was sixteen.

## Substance signature
- state_axes: Elia: capability degrades visibly (aging, physical decline) as position and political power rise; moral-framework stress between self-expenditure-as-principle and self-preservation-as-strategic-necessity; social-tether to her original constituents (the famine districts) maintained or lost depending on whether she survives to administer. Position: moves from junior senator to the floor's most feared vote, then toward irrelevance as she runs low.
- cost_ledger: First major vote — Elia signs five years on the initial bill; she looks 29 by morning; her hair is different. Procedural counter-motion — the opposition forces a revote on the amendment definition; she signs three more; she is 32 in the mirror. Forced series of procedural votes over six weeks — Elia has aged fourteen years and is 38-in-body at 24-in-time; her physician visits the senate chambers. Final vote — the bill requires her last political capital; she has six months of ledger remaining when she signs.
- antagonist_pressure: Round 1 — senior senator Drothe simply moves to table; Elia outspends him; he sees the pattern. Round 2 — Drothe forms a coalition that will force three procedural revotes before any substantive motion; each revote costs Elia regardless of outcome; he does not need to win, only to drain. Round 3 — Drothe's faction bribes Elia's physician to revise her ledger estimate downward, reducing her legal voting capacity; Elia must dispute the estimate in a formal hearing, spending a year in legal process, before she can vote again.
- dramatic_shape: Setup — Elia signs her first five years on the famine bill; the motion carries; she sees the cost in the mirror and is satisfied. Escalation — Drothe's procedural campaign begins; Elia spends on every forced vote, aging week by week, and the famine sheet in her coat updates while the bill stalls. Midpoint reversal — Elia's physician pulls her aside: she has spent more than half her ledger in four months; she has perhaps fourteen years remaining; she will be an old woman before the bill is implemented, and the implementation requires a senator's continued presence. Cost spike — Drothe's ledger fraud; Elia cannot vote for a year; she ages no further but watches the famine sheet update eleven more times while she litigates. Climax — Elia's ledger is restored; she has six months remaining; the bill is one vote from passage; she signs the six months. Aftermath — the bill passes. Elia is 24-in-time and 70-in-body, legally alive, with nothing remaining on her ledger. She sees the bill's first implementation report from a wheelchair in the gallery.

## Protagonist arc
Elia begins as someone who understands her youth as leverage and has no fear of the cost because the cause is real and the children are dying. She ends having spent herself to the floor with the bill implemented and her body thirty years past itself — and the question the story insists on is whether she won or lost, and the story refuses to answer cleanly. The single decision: she signs the six months rather than wait for a coalition that Drothe can always block.

## Beat outline (4-6 beats)
1. Elia signs five years on the famine bill's first reading; the chamber votes; the motion carries 31-29; she studies her face in the bathroom mirror for forty seconds and goes back to her seat.
2. Drothe presents a motion to redefine the bill's distribution criteria — a procedural move, not substantive — and calls for a floor vote; Elia signs three years; the motion fails; Drothe notes the withdrawal total in his ledger.
3. Elia's physician visits the senate chamber after the sixth forced revote in three weeks; he puts a sheet in front of her with her estimated remaining years; she reads it, folds it, puts it in the same pocket as the famine sheet.
4. Drothe produces a revised physician estimate showing Elia's ledger at legally insufficient levels; the senate clerk suspends her voting rights pending hearing; Elia sits in the gallery for the next vote and watches Drothe's amendment pass.
5. Elia's ledger is restored after eleven months of hearings; she walks into the chamber with six months on her ledger and the famine sheet showing a number larger than when she started.
6. Elia signs the final motion. The bill passes. Her aide brings a wheelchair to the chamber floor because she cannot stand for the ceremonial signing; she signs from the chair; the chamber applauds; she cannot hear most of it.

## Concrete engine (DEC-0115 fence)
Elia picks up the withdrawal slip, writes a number, and signs her name. Over the next six hours she ages the number of years she wrote — her joints stiffen, her hair shifts, the lines around her eyes deepen — and the famine bill moves one procedural step toward passage. The opposition calls another vote. She picks up another slip. The famine sheet in her coat updates with the week's child deaths while she signs. Both things happen at the same time, in the same chamber, on the same morning.

## Novelty claim
The life-vote senate has strong precedent (political sacrifice as literal self-destruction, aging-as-cost mechanics). The novelty is the procedural-drain as the specific antagonist mechanism: the opposition does not need to defeat Elia on substance, only on endurance, forcing her to spend on motions that don't advance the cause. The enemy is procedure, not principle — and she knows it and spends anyway.
