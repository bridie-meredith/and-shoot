# P32 weather-priest-invoice-r2

LOGLINE: A weather-priest discovers his ordination oath is verbatim a guild service contract — every act of faith he has ever made was a billable transaction — and must decide what remains holy when the rite that made him a priest was commerce before it was vocation.

LENS: political

## Premise trajectory (5-component path)
- motivation: He is a weather-priest who believes rain is sacred common property; he calls it for the farmers who pray because that is what he was ordained to do.
- anchor: A specific farming village — the one where he was raised, which has not had rain in forty days and whose children he still knows by name.
- escalation: Each unauthorized calling increases the guild's invoice against the village; while trying to contest the invoices, he pulls the guild's founding charter and reads his ordination oath printed verbatim in Article 1 — the frequency trained into him, the chrism applied to his hands, the words he spoke: all guild intellectual property, all licensed back to him conditional on compliance.
- trade: He calls the rain → his village survives the drought → the guild invoices the calling → the village loses land in payment → the guild owns more of what it sells rain to → his calling accelerated the dispossession. And beneath every step: the faith that licensed him to call was the guild's instrument from the first syllable he ever spoke in ordination.
- irony: He spent his vocation giving freely what was never his to give; every act of priestly mercy was a guild disbursement against a contract he signed without reading, and mercy was the product, not the gift.

## World rules (the magic-tech that makes the engine work)
Rain is a managed resource: the sky-guild maintains a network of high-altitude weather-holds — pressure-vessels of accumulated moisture locked behind guild-maintained seals. A weather-priest with formal training can call rain from any hold within fifty miles; the call is a spoken frequency that dissolves the seal and drops the moisture. The guild registered every weather-hold as a tradeable commodity thirty years ago under the Commonage Act, which declared atmospheric moisture a manufactured good when held at altitude. A priest's unauthorized call is treated as commodity theft; the guild calculates the market rate for the moisture called down and issues an invoice to the nearest inhabited settlement. Invoices that cannot be paid convert to easement-liens on farmland at guild-set valuation. Priests who call rain three times without a guild license lose their ordination under the same Act. What no priest in living memory has checked: the ordination ceremony was written by the guild's founders. The tonal frequency sequence, the chrism compound, the oath phrasing — all appear in the guild's founding charter (dated eighteen years before the first seminary opened) as registered proprietary assets licensed to the seminary in perpetuity. The priest did not receive a sacred call; he received a sublicense.

## Substance signature
- state_axes: position erodes (each calling moves him closer to deordination; each invoice moves the village closer to dispossession) / moral-framework under compression (the rain IS his to call, theologically; the law says otherwise; he now knows the law was written by the people who wrote his theology) / social-tether splits (the guild offers him a licensed position that would let him call rain legally but only for paying clients; he now knows the offer is a license renewal, not an upgrade)
- cost_ledger: First calling → village survives → invoice 1 issued → village sells a northern field to pay; second calling → invoice 2 → village sells the grain-storage lease; discovery of the charter → he loses the theological ground he was standing on, not a field but his account of himself; third calling → deordination warning + invoice 3 → village cannot pay, easement-lien converts, guild now holds the south slope and sells rain back to the village at premium; fourth calling → deordination + village fully in debt-farming
- antagonist_pressure: Round 1 — a guild factor delivers the first invoice in person and explains the Commonage Act clearly, without hostility; Round 2 — the guild offers him a licensed post in the city, framed as an offer but timed to coincide with the second invoice, making it a bribe with a face; Round 3 — while contesting invoice 2 at the guild registry, a clerk points him to the founding charter as precedent; he reads it at a standing desk in the registry hall and finds his ordination oath in the preamble, word for word; Round 4 — the guild's enforcement arm arrives with a lien-conversion notice and a deordination writ simultaneously, and the factor informs him that his ordination can be administratively revoked because the sublicense is revocable
- dramatic_shape: setup (drought, first unauthorized calling, invoice arrives) → escalation (second calling, offer arrives, he refuses) → midpoint reversal (finds the founding charter at the guild registry; his ordination oath is Article 1, Clause 2; the chrism was a guild reagent; the frequency is a registered seal-key) → cost spike (deordination writ + final lien arrive together; he cannot satisfy both; he now knows the deordination is a license revocation) → climax (he calls rain a fourth time, unlicensed, after the sublicense is revoked, in full view of the factor — using the frequency that is no longer his to use) → aftermath (rain falls; he is no longer a priest; the field transfers; he stays and works it under the guild's lien)

## Protagonist arc
Start: theologically certain, politically naive, believes the sacred right of rain supersedes the commercial law. Midpoint fracture: discovers his theology IS the commercial law — the guild authored both. End: he calls the rain anyway, with no sacred standing and no legal license, because the alternative is to accept that the first word he ever spoke in faith was an invoice. The flip is not sacrifice but refusal: he uses the frequency after the sublicense is revoked, committing the same act the guild always said it was — unauthorized use of proprietary property — but now consciously, without the cover of vocation.

## Beat outline (4-6 beats)
1. He stands in the village square on the fortieth day of drought and speaks the frequency aloud; rain falls for six hours; by morning the guild factor has delivered an invoice on folded guild-seal parchment citing the Commonage Act, Article 7, clause 3.
2. The guild factor returns with a formal offer letter — a licensed post, a salary, the legal right to call rain — and sits across from him in the parish house for an hour explaining why the Act is actually protective of priests; he does not sign.
3. Contesting invoice 2 at the guild registry, a clerk directs him to the founding charter. He reads it at a standing desk in the registry hall: his ordination oath — the exact phrasing, the chrism formula listed as a compound reagent, the frequency sequence notated in the margin — appears in Article 1, Clause 2, dated eighteen years before the first seminary. He copies it out by hand onto a sheet of parish paper and walks home holding it.
4. The second invoice arrives; the village council meets and votes to sell the grain-storage lease; the council chair thanks him for the rain and asks him not to call again until they have paid down; he sets the copied charter page on the table and nobody picks it up.
5. Thirty days later, after a failed rain-negotiation with the guild factor, he calls again; the deordination writ arrives the next morning alongside the lien-conversion notice for the south slope; the writ's language cites sublicense revocation, not ecclesiastical cause.
6. On the day the south slope transfers to the guild, he walks to the square and calls rain a fourth time — unlicensed, unordained, sublicense revoked, in full view of the factor and the enforcement arm — speaking the frequency that the founding charter says belongs to the guild, on land that is no longer his village's to farm.

## Concrete engine (DEC-0115 fence)
He speaks a specific frequency aloud — a sustained tonal sequence trained into him at ordination, printed in the guild's founding charter as a registered seal-key — and the guild's pressure-seal at the nearest weather-hold dissolves; moisture drops as rain, measurable, on the fields below. A guild clerk with a barometric instrument records the volume, calculates the market rate, and writes it on invoice parchment that is delivered by hand. The debt is a number on a page that converts to a land-transfer document when unpaid. The charter page he copied at the registry hall sits in his parish house. He calls rain; it falls; a document arrives; a field changes hands. The frequency he used to dissolve the seal is property he never owned.

## Novelty claim
The engine is no longer priest-vs-commerce. It is a man who reads the fine print of his own ordination and finds a guild contract — his call was sublicensed before his first act of faith, the chrism was a reagent, the oath was Article 1 Clause 2. The nameable frame (sacred vs. commercial) dissolves: the sacred WAS commercial, at origin, in the founding document he can hold in his hands. His final act — calling rain with a revoked sublicense, using property that was never his — is not protest or sacrifice; it is the first time his action and the guild's description of it are in exact agreement.

## REWORK NOTES

- **Engine redefined at origin:** The ordination ceremony is now revealed to be a guild contract predating the seminary by eighteen years (founding charter, Article 1, Clause 2); the frequency, chrism, and oath are registered guild IP sublicensed to priests — so the theological trap is not "commerce colonized faith" but "faith was commerce at the moment of its making," collapsing the priest-vs-commerce opposition entirely.
- **Discovery made concrete:** Beat 3 adds a physical scene at the guild registry hall — a standing desk, a clerk's direction, the founding charter in hand, the protagonist copying the ordination oath onto parish paper — so the revelation is an artifact he touches, not an argument he hears.
- **Logline and Novelty claim rewritten:** Both now name the engine as "a man reading the fine print of his own vocation" and explicitly replace the prior Pratchett-adjacent framing with the sublicense-at-origin structure; cost ledger, escalating beats, and concreteness floor are unchanged.
