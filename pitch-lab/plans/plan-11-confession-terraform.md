# P11 confession-terraform

LOGLINE: The colony's terraforming engine runs on confessions — larger sins yield more fertile soil — and the new governor must decide whether to feed the dying crops with a crime she has not yet committed.

LENS: penitential

## Premise trajectory (5-component path)
- motivation: save the colony from crop failure before the next ship arrives and finds nothing left
- anchor: the confessional intake chamber where colonists speak into a soil-converter and the ground beneath it changes
- escalation: small sins produce marginal yield; the shortfall is measured and will not be closed by minor confessions
- trade: she commits the crime, confesses it, saves the crops — and the crime is real, with a victim
- irony: the act designed to save the community is the one thing that marks her unfit to lead it; confession publishes the crime

## World rules (the magic-tech that makes the engine work)
The terraforming engine is a bacterial culture keyed to the emotional chemistry of human stress hormones present during genuine confession — the colonist must mean it. A speaker stands in the intake chamber, presses both palms to a conducting plate, and speaks; the machine measures pulse, skin conductivity, and vocal-stress signatures against a verified-regret threshold. When the threshold is met, the spoken content is processed against a moral-severity index calibrated by the colony's founding charter: scale of harm caused, number of people affected, and degree of intent. The converted chemical signal is piped into the soil grid under the eastern fields. A colonist confessing a genuine petty theft enriches roughly a quarter-acre. The charter's severity index places premeditated harm to a person — violence with deliberate intent — at roughly forty acres per event. The fields need thirty-eight acres before the growing window closes. Confessions are public record: the intake chamber prints a receipt that goes into the colony ledger, readable by anyone.

## Substance signature
- state_axes: position ↓ (governor becomes a confessed criminal); moral-framework ↓ then ambiguous (she crosses a line she defines as uncrossable); social-tether ↑ temporarily then ↓ (community survives but cannot trust her with power)
- cost_ledger: thirty-eight acres of fertile ground = one premeditated act of violence against a named person who did not consent to be the instrument of the colony's survival; the receipt in the ledger = permanent public record of who she harmed and why
- antagonist_pressure: the colony charter enforces the ledger's transparency — no governor can seal a confession record; the person she harms has legal standing to challenge her position; the next supply ship carries auditors who will read the ledger
- dramatic_shape: setup (she inherits the governorship and the crop failure simultaneously) → escalation (she runs the math; small confessions from colonists do not close the gap) → midpoint reversal (she identifies the specific act that would close it; realizes she must do it first, then confess it) → cost spike (she commits the act; the victim's face is a person she knows) → climax (she stands at the intake chamber and speaks; the field blooms under her feet while the receipt prints) → aftermath (the auditors arrive; the ledger is open; the crops are alive; she steps down before they ask)

## Protagonist arc
Starts as a governor who believes her job is to protect people from harm. Ends as someone who harmed a specific person to protect the rest — and cannot distinguish that from a crime. The flip: the moment she steps into the intake chamber after committing the act, she is no longer deciding whether to do it. She is deciding whether to report it. She chooses publication over concealment; the only thing she can still control is the shape of her own record.

## Beat outline (4-6 beats)
1. She walks the eastern fields on her first day and watches the soil consultant press a handheld meter into the ground; it reads critically low; the consultant does not meet her eyes.
2. She calls every colonist into the intake chamber over three days and facilitates their confessions — minor debts, small thefts, a broken promise — and watches the field meter move by fractions.
3. She works through the charter's severity index with paper and a pen, doing arithmetic at her desk alone, until she arrives at the number that closes the gap and writes the category of act beside it.
4. She does it. The scene is one paragraph, present tense, entirely physical: what her hands do, what the other person says, the sound the door makes when she leaves.
5. She stands at the intake chamber, palms on the plate, and speaks for four minutes. The floor trembles as the culture activates. Through the chamber's window she watches the field darken with moisture.
6. The receipt prints. She takes it to the colony ledger herself, writes her own name at the top of the entry, signs the date, and leaves it open on the desk for the auditors.

## Concrete engine (DEC-0115 fence)
A person stands in a room, presses their hands to a metal plate, and talks. The machine under the floor responds to the body's chemistry — sweat, pulse, the catch in the voice. The soil in the fields outside changes color as the culture spreads: pale gray to dark brown, visible from the governor's window. Every transaction has a paper receipt in a physical ledger. The chain from act to field is biological, legible, and irreversible.

## Novelty claim
Confession-as-resource is known; what is new here is the premeditation trap — the engine requires genuine regret, but the governor commits the crime in order to confess it, making her regret both real (she is genuinely sorry) and structurally impossible (she chose to do it, which the machine also measures). The tension is whether a premeditated sin confessed immediately reads to the machine as sincere — and whether it matters if it does.
