# P32 weather-priest-invoice

LOGLINE: A weather-priest caught between farmers who pray for rain and a sky-guild that sells it must choose whose drought to end, knowing the rain he calls down anywhere will be invoiced everywhere.

LENS: political

## Premise trajectory (5-component path)
- motivation: He is a weather-priest who believes rain is sacred common property; he calls it for the farmers who pray because that is what he was ordained to do.
- anchor: A specific farming village — the one where he was raised, which has not had rain in forty days and whose children he still knows by name.
- escalation: Each unauthorized calling increases the guild's invoice against the village; the guild's enforcement arm arrives to collect; the village cannot pay; the debt is converted to a land-transfer, which the guild farms with rain it sells back at premium.
- trade: He calls the rain → his village survives the drought → the guild invoices the calling → the village loses land in payment → the guild owns more of what it sells rain to → his calling accelerated the dispossession.
- irony: Every act of priestly mercy that keeps his village alive adds to the debt instrument that will eventually transfer the village's farmland to the guild that caused the drought.

## World rules (the magic-tech that makes the engine work)
Rain is a managed resource: the sky-guild maintains a network of high-altitude weather-holds — pressure-vessels of accumulated moisture locked behind guild-maintained seals. A weather-priest with formal training can call rain from any hold within fifty miles; the call is a spoken frequency that dissolves the seal and drops the moisture. But the guild registered every weather-hold as a tradeable commodity thirty years ago under the Commonage Act, which declared atmospheric moisture a manufactured good when held at altitude. A priest's unauthorized call is treated as commodity theft; the guild calculates the market rate for the moisture called down and issues an invoice to the nearest inhabited settlement. Invoices that cannot be paid convert to easement-liens on farmland at guild-set valuation. Priests who call rain three times without a guild license lose their ordination under the same Act.

## Substance signature
- state_axes: position erodes (each calling moves him closer to deordination; each invoice moves the village closer to dispossession) / moral-framework under compression (the rain IS his to call, theologically; the law says otherwise; he knows the law was written to produce this exact compression) / social-tether splits (the guild offers him a licensed position that would let him call rain legally but only for paying clients)
- cost_ledger: First calling → village survives → invoice 1 issued → village sells a northern field to pay; second calling → invoice 2 → village sells the grain-storage lease; third calling → deordination warning + invoice 3 → village cannot pay, easement-lien converts, guild now holds the south slope and sells rain back to the village at premium; fourth calling → deordination + village fully in debt-farming
- antagonist_pressure: Round 1 — a guild factor delivers the first invoice in person and explains the Commonage Act clearly, without hostility; Round 2 — the guild offers him a licensed post in the city, framed as an offer but timed to coincide with the second invoice, making it a bribe with a face; Round 3 — the guild's enforcement arm arrives with a lien-conversion notice and a deordination writ simultaneously, forcing him to choose between his ordination and his village's last free field
- dramatic_shape: setup (drought, first unauthorized calling, invoice arrives) → escalation (second calling, offer arrives, he refuses) → midpoint reversal (discovers the guild factor is also a licensed priest, ordained the same year, who accepted the guild post and now calls rain only for clients who can pay) → cost spike (deordination writ + final lien arrive together; he cannot satisfy both) → climax (he calls rain a fourth time, unlicensed, over the village, fully deordinated, knowing the invoice will take the last field) → aftermath (rain falls; he is no longer a priest; the field transfers; he stays and works it under the guild's lien)

## Protagonist arc
Start: theologically certain, politically naive, believes the sacred right of rain supersedes the commercial law. End: he understands the law was written to use his theology against the people he serves, and he calls the rain anyway — fully aware, fully costing the village — because stopping would be the same as agreeing that the guild owns the sky. The flip: he calls the fourth time after deordination, when it can only cost and cannot save his standing.

## Beat outline (4-6 beats)
1. He stands in the village square on the fortieth day of drought and speaks the frequency aloud; rain falls for six hours; by morning the guild factor has delivered an invoice on folded guild-seal parchment citing the Commonage Act, Article 7, clause 3.
2. The guild factor returns with a formal offer letter — a licensed post, a salary, the legal right to call rain — and sits across from him in the parish house for an hour explaining why the Act is actually protective of priests; he does not sign.
3. He meets the licensed priest — his year-mate, ordained the same day — who calls rain for a coastal resort and a plantation district and who has not called unauthorized rain since the Act passed; they eat together and do not agree on anything.
4. The second invoice arrives; the village council meets and votes to sell the grain-storage lease; the council chair thanks him for the rain and asks him not to call again until they have paid down; he nods and does not promise.
5. Thirty days later, after a failed rain-negotiation with the guild factor, he calls again; the deordination writ arrives the next morning alongside the lien-conversion notice for the south slope.
6. On the day the south slope transfers to the guild, he walks to the square and calls rain a fourth time — unlicensed, unordained, in full view of the factor and the enforcement arm — and the rain falls on land that is no longer his village's to farm.

## Concrete engine (DEC-0115 fence)
He speaks a specific frequency aloud — a sustained tonal sequence trained into him at ordination — and the guild's pressure-seal at the nearest weather-hold dissolves; moisture drops as rain, measurable, on the fields below. A guild clerk with a barometric instrument records the volume, calculates the market rate, and writes it on invoice parchment that is delivered by hand. The debt is a number on a page that converts to a land-transfer document when unpaid. He calls rain; it falls; a document arrives; a field changes hands.

## Novelty claim
Commodified magic with invoice mechanics has close precedents (Pratchett adjacent, various secondary-world capitalism critiques). The specific load-bearing element here is the theological trap: the priest's ordained function (calling rain) is structurally identical to the commercial violation (unauthorized commodity extraction), and the law was written to make them indistinguishable. His only available protest is to keep doing what he was made to do until it destroys his standing — which is not a sacrifice but an insistence.
