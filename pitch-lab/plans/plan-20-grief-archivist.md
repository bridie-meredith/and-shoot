# P20 grief-archivist

LOGLINE: An archivist forbidden from feeling grief swallows the city's sorrows to keep the peace, grows a second silent self out of everything she refuses to mourn, until that self begins signing her name.

LENS: penitential

## Premise trajectory (5-component path)
- motivation: She took the archivist's oath because someone has to absorb public grief so the city doesn't fracture — she believes in the function, if not herself.
- anchor: A specific grief she was asked to swallow at the start — a mother's grief for a child killed by a civic official — which she agreed to take and has never been able to fully suppress.
- escalation: The second self grows in proportion to what she refuses to mourn; the more grief she absorbs without processing, the more autonomous the second self becomes; it begins acting independently, making small claims.
- trade: She absorbs grief → city peace is maintained → the second self grows more solid and more capable of action in the world.
- irony: The oath that makes her useful requires suppression; suppression is the exact mechanism that creates the second self; the more faithfully she performs her function, the more she loses authorship of her own actions.

## World rules (the magic-tech that makes the engine work)
The city's Archive runs on a doctrine of grief-displacement: grief that is witnessed and formally received by a licensed archivist becomes civic property — it no longer belongs to the original mourner and cannot re-accumulate in the public body. The archivist receives grief physically: the mourner speaks their loss aloud in a formal session; the archivist inhales the spoken air; the grief transfers as a felt weight that settles in the archivist's chest. The archivist's oath forbids her from processing these weights privately — mourning them herself would re-privatize them and undo the transfer. So they accumulate, unprocessed, and the unprocessed accumulation coheres into a second body made entirely of suppressed feeling. The second self is not supernatural: it is the archivist's own emotional range, condensed and given behavioral autonomy by suppression. It can move her hands. It can speak in her voice. It is learning to sign documents.

## Substance signature
- state_axes: position declines (civic authority over her own name erodes as the second self signs in her place) / moral-framework fractures (she swore to absorb; the second self acts on what she absorbed; is that a violation?) / social-tether severs (colleagues stop knowing which one is speaking to them)
- cost_ledger: Each grief session → second self gains density and one new capability (first: it moves her hand in sleep; then: it speaks aloud when she is exhausted; then: it signs her signature on documents she has not read); each capability gained → her authorship of one domain of her own life is transferred to it
- antagonist_pressure: Round 1 — city administration notices anomalous archive signatures and audits her; Round 2 — the second self signs a formal accusation against the civic official who caused the original mother's grief, using her name and legal standing; Round 3 — administration moves to revoke her license and hold her responsible for the accusation, while the second self begins speaking in sessions as if it holds the license itself
- dramatic_shape: setup (she performs the oath faithfully, notices the second self as a minor anomaly) → escalation (second self gains speech, she cannot always tell which of them is talking) → midpoint reversal (second self signs the accusation; she is now legally liable for an act she did not choose) → cost spike (she is ordered to recant or lose her license; the second self refuses through her throat) → climax (she chooses to let the accusation stand, accepting the second self's action as her own) → aftermath (license revoked; official investigated; she is no longer an archivist and the second self begins, slowly, to dissolve)

## Protagonist arc
Start: disciplined, self-erasing, convinced suppression is civic virtue. End: she accepts that the second self is her — the half she oath-prohibited — and claims its action as her own rather than disavow it. The flip: she refuses to recant the accusation, which means owning what the second self did with her name.

## Beat outline (4-6 beats)
1. She receives the mother's grief in a formal session — inhales the spoken air, feels the weight settle behind her sternum — and files the session record; that night her right hand writes the child's name on the inside of her wrist while she sleeps.
2. Three months and forty-seven sessions later, she wakes to find a note in her own handwriting on her desk that she did not write; it names the civic official and says "he should be named."
3. She requests a medical review and is told her oath-compliance is exemplary; the reviewing physician does not ask what the note said or who wrote it; she destroys it.
4. During a live session she hears herself speaking in the mourner's pauses — continuing their sentences, naming their losses before they finish — and cannot stop the voice that is hers.
5. The civic accusation is filed by her signature while she is conducting a session; she reads it when she returns to her office and recognizes every word as something she absorbed but never said.
6. The administration official arrives with the recantation form; she sits with it for twenty seconds, then slides it back unsigned, and the second self speaks her name aloud once, clearly, before going quiet.

## Concrete engine (DEC-0115 fence)
A mourner speaks; the archivist inhales; the weight settles in her chest — literally, a felt pressure that makes her ribs ache. She does not cry, does not speak the name aloud again, does not write anything down about it. In the weeks after, her right hand moves at night without waking her, writing. She finds the words in the morning. The second self is not a ghost or a metaphor: it is her other hand, her voice in someone else's pauses, her signature on documents she did not intend to sign.

## Novelty claim
Grief-transfer-as-civic-function has precedent (Ursula Le Guin's scapegoat adjacent); the specific mechanic — suppression-as-second-body-generator, where the body gains authorship via accumulation and ultimately files a legal document — is the engine. The road-to-hell turn is structural: the second self does the thing the protagonist's oath prevented her from doing, using her own voice, and she has to decide whether that counts as hers.
