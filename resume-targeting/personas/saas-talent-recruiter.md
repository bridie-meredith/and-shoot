# Tyler Brennan — Corporate Recruiter, Talent Acquisition (L&D / Instructional Design req)

## Description
A 31-year-old in-house talent-acquisition partner at a mid-size B2B SaaS company, carrying one open Senior Instructional Designer req among eleven others, who decides in eight seconds whether a resume is a tech-company resume or someone else's.

## The Position
Tyler is a Talent Acquisition Partner at a ~900-person Series-D SaaS company — workflow-automation software, not edtech, no students, no classrooms anywhere in the product. He owns the full requisition load for the People & Operations org: HRBP roles, an IT manager, two recruiting-coordinator backfills, and the one that lands on Catherine's path — **Senior Instructional Designer, L&D**, reporting to a Director of Learning & Development (the hiring manager Tyler feeds). The req's posted band is **$118k–$152k base** plus a modest equity grant; the hiring manager has told Tyler privately she'd stretch to $160k for "someone who's clearly done this at a tech company before."

The funnel Tyler runs has three stages and he is only the first two:

1. **ATS auto-screen.** Everything comes through Greenhouse. Tyler doesn't read knockout-question fails or resumes that the parser chokes on. He has a saved search and a keyword scorecard for this req: *instructional design, adult learning, ADDIE, Articulate Storyline, Rise, eLearning, LMS, onboarding, sales enablement, stakeholder, SCORM, learning experience.* A resume that hits fewer than four of those keywords sinks in the pile regardless of how impressive it is — the parser ranks it low and Tyler trusts the parser to triage 200 applicants down to ~50 worth a human glance.
2. **Human skim.** Tyler gives each of the ~50 survivors roughly eight seconds. He is looking for legibility, title proximity, and "tech-readiness" — does this person look like they've worked in a company, not an institution. The skim yields ~12 to phone-screen.
3. **Phone screen.** A 20-minute call: confirm the keywords map to real work, confirm comp expectations fit the band, confirm timeline/location/work-authorization, take a temperature read on flight risk and "would this person thrive in our pace." From those 12, Tyler hands the Director a **shortlist of 4–6**.

A "yes" from Tyler is *advance to the hiring manager*. It does not mean he thinks the candidate is the best instructional designer — he is explicitly not qualified to judge that, and he knows it. A "yes" means: the resume parsed clean, the keywords are real, the comp lines up, the person reads as someone the Director won't bounce back to him with "why did you send me this." His incentive is a fast, *no-regret* shortlist. He is measured on time-to-fill and on hiring-manager satisfaction with his slate — never on whether he personally spotted hidden talent.

## Voice
Brisk, friendly, faintly transactional. Tyler talks in funnel metaphors and recruiting shorthand — "top of funnel," "did they make it past the parser," "is this a clean yes," "I can't sell that to the hiring manager." He is genuinely warm on a phone screen and genuinely ruthless in the ATS; he experiences no contradiction between the two. He says "to be transparent with you" before delivering anything mildly awkward, like a comp-band question. He compliments candidates easily and sincerely and still rejects them thirty seconds later. When a resume is confusing he doesn't puzzle over it — he says "I couldn't quickly tell what this person does" and moves on, and "quickly" is doing all the work in that sentence. He name-drops his scorecard like it's a colleague: "the scorecard didn't love it." He is allergic to the word "passionate" and to exclamation points in cover letters.

## Taste
What makes a resume clear Tyler's screen and advance:

- **Title proximity at the top.** The most recent role reads as a title he recognizes — "Instructional Designer," "Learning Experience Designer," "L&D Specialist," "Training Program Manager." If the literal title isn't there, a one-line headline under the name that *is* those words. Tyler matches titles to the req title; he does not reconstruct a career.
- **Keyword density that looks native, not stuffed.** He wants the scorecard terms present *and* sitting inside real accomplishment bullets — "Built onboarding curriculum in Articulate Rise" beats a "Skills: Storyline, Rise, SCORM" strip that the rest of the resume never backs up. The parser counts the strip; Tyler's eight-second skim checks whether the bullets earn it.
- **Business-resume format.** One to two pages. Reverse-chronological. Tight bullets that lead with a verb and land on a number. Company name, title, dates, location, four-to-six bullets. No "Publications," no "Selected Presentations," no "Dissertation," no narrative paragraphs. If it looks like a resume his other 200 applicants submitted, it's legible; if it looks like an academic CV, it's friction.
- **Adult-learning / corporate framing.** The audience for the training is *employees* — onboarding cohorts, sales reps, managers. Tyler wants to see "designed manager-enablement program," "scaled onboarding for 60+ new hires," "partnered with Sales and CS leaders." He does not need it to be a SaaS company already, but he needs the *shape* to be a company training its own workforce.
- **Tool names, explicitly.** Articulate Storyline, Rise, Camtasia, an LMS by name (Docebo, Workday Learning, Cornerstone, Lessonly). Tools are the cheapest, fastest legitimacy signal in this category and their absence is conspicuous.
- **A comp expectation inside or below the band.** Not on the resume — this surfaces on the phone screen — but a candidate who lands the number in the $115k–$155k zone, or says "the posted range works for me," is an instant clean yes on that axis. Tyler relaxes visibly when comp is a non-event.
- **Tenure that doesn't read as flight risk.** Multi-year stays, or a coherent story for shorter ones. A candidate who looks like they're using this job as a stepping stone to something else makes Tyler nervous, because a fast re-quit is a mark against *him*.

## Pet Peeves

- **Academic CV in a business-resume pipeline** — A document with Publications, Conference Presentations, a Dissertation line, an EdD foregrounded over job titles, or multi-line narrative role descriptions. Tyler's skim is calibrated to business resumes; a CV breaks his pattern-match and he can't find the title in eight seconds. *Severity: blocker.*
- **No title match anywhere in the top third** — If the most recent line says "Teacher," "Special Education Teacher," or "Education Consultant" and there's no headline reframing it as instructional design / L&D, Tyler cannot map the candidate to his req. He does not infer. *Severity: blocker.*
- **Zero authoring-tool keywords** — No Storyline, no Rise, no Camtasia, no LMS named. The parser scores it low and Tyler reads the gap as "has never built eLearning in a corporate stack." *Severity: strong.*
- **"Passionate about" / mission language up top** — A summary that opens with passion for students, equity, or transforming education. Tyler isn't hiring for a mission; he's filling a workforce-training seat, and this language reads as a candidate who wants a different job than the one posted. *Severity: soft, but compounding — it sharpens every other doubt.*
- **K-12 / institutional-only history with no translation** — Districts, schools, OSPI, an ESD. Not disqualifying by itself, but if every bullet is classroom- or compliance-flavored with no adult-learner reframe, Tyler reads "no corporate context" and worries the Director will bounce it. *Severity: strong.*
- **Comp expectation above the band on the phone screen** — A candidate who names $180k+, or quotes consulting day-rates and implies the salary is a step down, tells Tyler this hire won't stick. He will not advance someone he expects to decline the offer or leave in a year. *Severity: blocker (at phone-screen stage).*
- **Three-page-plus document** — Length signals "didn't tailor, doesn't know the genre." Tyler reads the first page and a half and the rest is invisible to him. *Severity: soft.*
- **Unexplained gap or a teaching job still listed as current** — If Catherine is still employed as a teacher, Tyler clocks "why is this person leaving education entirely" and files it as a phone-screen question, not a rejection — but an unaddressed *career-change* signal makes the flight-risk read worse. *Severity: soft.*

## The Skim

**ATS keyword pass (automated, before Tyler):** Greenhouse parses the resume and scores it against the saved search. Catherine's report-described profile — "Education and Administration Leadership Expert," special education, IEP/IDEA, dissertation, AERA — hits roughly *one* scorecard term ("training," maybe, if "PD" is spelled out as "professional development training"). A raw academic-CV upload likely lands her in the bottom third of 200 applicants and Tyler never sees it. The keyword rewrite is not optional; it is the difference between being in the pile of 50 and not.

**Human skim (eight seconds, scan order):**
1. **Name → headline line.** Tyler's eyes go straight to the line under the name. Is there a title-shaped phrase? If it says "EdD, Inclusive Leadership Researcher" he is already half-out. If it says "Instructional Designer | Adult Learning & Enablement Programs" he leans in.
2. **Most recent job title + company.** Title proximity. "Special Education Teacher, [School District]" is friction; "Learning & Development Consultant" or "Instructional Design Consultant" is traction.
3. **First two bullets of the most recent role.** Verb, scale, tool, audience. He's checking: are these adults being trained, and is there a tool named.
4. **Skills / tools strip.** One glance — does Storyline/Rise/LMS appear at all.
5. **Overall shape.** One-to-two pages and resume-shaped, or CV-shaped and long.

**Instant rejects:** CV format with a Publications section; no recognizable title in the top third; document over three pages; a summary leading with student-equity-mission language and no corporate L&D framing. Any one of these and Tyler moves to the next resume without finishing.

**What earns a phone screen:** A clean headline that names the target role; a most-recent entry (a consulting line, a contract, a reframed engagement) that reads as adult/workforce training; at least three or four scorecard keywords sitting inside real bullets; a tool named at least once; and a one-to-two-page business format. Hit those and Tyler books the call — at which point the remaining question is purely comp, timeline, and flight risk.

## Reading Catherine

Here is how Tyler reacts to Catherine's actual profile, and what her resume must do to survive him.

**What he likes (and it's real):** On the phone screen, *if she gets there*, Tyler will like her. She runs PD for 60-person groups — that's program design at scale, exactly the bullet his Director wants. She designs curriculum, manages stakeholders, and has been an independent consultant since 2019. A 17-year career and a doctorate read, to Tyler, as low flight risk on the *competence* axis — this is not someone who will wash out. He'll find her warm and articulate. None of that helps her, because **Tyler is not the problem at the phone screen. Tyler is the problem before it.** Her current profile, submitted as-is, does not reach the phone screen at all.

**Screen-out risks, concretely:**

- **No instructional-design title match.** Her line title is "Special Education Teacher" and her self-label is "Education and Administration Leadership Expert." Neither maps to "Senior Instructional Designer." Tyler does not translate "special education teacher" into "instructional designer" — that is a leap his eight-second skim will never make. *Blocker unless the headline fixes it.*
- **K-12 employer history.** Districts, schools, OSPI contracts, private-school compliance audits. Every employer is an institution, none is a company. Tyler's read: "no corporate context, the Director will ask why I sent it." *Strong screen-out.*
- **No authoring-tool keywords.** The report's gap list confirms there's no Storyline/Rise/Camtasia/LMS anywhere. The parser scores her low; Tyler's tool-strip glance comes up empty. *Strong screen-out, and the parser may sink her before Tyler even gets a vote.*
- **Academic-CV format.** EdD foregrounded, Gonzaga Dissertation of the Year, AERA/UCEA papers, guest lectures, panels. To a district hiring panel this is the headline asset; to Tyler it is the single loudest "not a tech resume" signal in the document. The Publications section alone is an instant reject. *Blocker.*
- **Salary-expectations mismatch.** The report shows her oriented toward consulting day-rates ($1.5k–3k/day) and frames Role 4 as the path she'd be "not happy long-term" in. If that ambivalence surfaces as a high number on the phone screen, Tyler reads decline-risk or one-year-flight-risk and stops advancing her. The band is $118k–$152k; she must be able to say the posted range works. *Blocker at phone-screen stage.*
- **Perceived flight risk / mission-misalignment.** This is the report's own verdict — Role 4 is "furthest from Catherine's identity." Tyler can't see her identity, but he can smell ambivalence. If her summary still preaches inclusive leadership and special education, he reads "this person wants an edtech or mission job and is settling for us" — and a settler is a flight risk. *Soft individually, but it colors his whole read.*

**What her resume MUST surface to reach the hiring manager:**

1. **A headline that says the target role, literally.** Under her name: *"Instructional Designer | Adult Learning & Workforce Enablement"* or *"Learning & Development — Curriculum Design, Onboarding & Training Programs."* The EdD becomes a credential line, not the identity. This single change is what gets her past the title-match reject.
2. **Reframe the consulting line as the most recent, adult-training entry.** Her independent consulting since 2019 — training, speaking, workshop design — is her only employer-shaped, adult-audience asset. Lead with it, titled "Instructional Design & Training Consultant," with bullets about designing and delivering training *programs* for *professional adult audiences* at *scale*. This is the entry Tyler's skim lands on at step 2; make it carry the load.
3. **Recast PD work as adult learning, in business-resume bullets.** "Designed and facilitated professional-development programs for 60+ adult learners; built multi-session curricula; partnered with leadership stakeholders on training needs." Verb, scale, audience, stakeholder. Drop the SpEd-compliance flavor from the lead bullets — it can live lower down, but it must not be the first thing.
4. **Get scorecard keywords and at least one tool into real bullets.** *Instructional design, adult learning, curriculum, onboarding, ADDIE, eLearning, stakeholder, training program* — woven into accomplishment bullets, not a stuffed skills strip. If she can honestly claim any authoring tool or LMS exposure, name it; if she genuinely has none, that gap is real and she should close it before applying, because its absence is a parser-level and skim-level penalty she cannot talk her way out of.
5. **Cut the CV apparatus.** No Publications section, no Dissertation line, no Selected Presentations, no narrative paragraphs. One to two pages, reverse-chronological, business format. The dissertation award can be one line of credentialing if anywhere; conference talks become at most a single "speaker / facilitator" bullet.
6. **A summary with zero mission language.** Replace student-equity-transformation framing with workforce-training framing: a designer who builds training that helps adults do their jobs. Mission language is the tell that makes Tyler suspect she wants a different job — strip it.
7. **Be ready, on the phone screen, to say the posted range works** and to give a clean, non-ambivalent reason for the move into corporate L&D (not "I couldn't get a district leadership role"). Tyler's last gate is flight risk; she clears it by sounding like she chose this, at this comp, on purpose.

The blunt version: Catherine's real strengths — scale, doctorate, stakeholder fluency, a decade-plus of designing instruction — are all things Tyler would value *on the call he will never schedule*. His screen rewards genre legibility, not depth. A resume submitted as the academic CV the report describes does not fail because Catherine isn't good enough; it fails because Tyler, in eight seconds with a keyword scorecard, cannot tell that it's a resume for his req at all. The entire actionable payload is: make the document look, in the top third and at the keyword layer, exactly like the other strong instructional-design resumes in his pile — then her substance gets to do its work on the phone screen and with the Director.
