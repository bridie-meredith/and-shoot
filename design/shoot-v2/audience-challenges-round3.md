# Audience Challenges — Round 3 (Antagonistic Generative)

**Mode:** Adversarial pressure. Accepts stand as accepts. Every line pushed under load. The goal is to surface the strongest counter-argument the writer must be able to defend against — not to overturn round 2.

**Taylor weight note:** worm-canon-pedant challenges for TH-1 through TH-4 are pushed harder than any other line. The doubled-register contradiction is the primary attack surface and receives full hostile treatment.

### TH-1 (taylor-hebert-westeros, mask: SLIPPING-structural)
**Round 2 line:** "Goodwife Mira. If it please you. Three things, before he closes his board — that the letter was held out and named, that I called for the septon at the door, that the door did not open. Only that you saw it. I would not ask if there were time."
**Round 2 verdict:** ACCEPT

**Challenges (adversarial — the writer must defend or revise):**

- *dark-fantasy-reader:* The line earns on the rubric but consider what a careful reader at speed actually absorbs: "Goodwife Mira. If it please you." reads as a polished medieval-fiction courtesy beat, and the next three *that*-clauses read as the standard legal-witness formula that any quasi-medieval fantasy reader has seen in a dozen procedural scenes. The slippage the writer claims — the adult operator's engineering in a child's mouth — requires the reader to understand that eleven-year-olds don't normally structure asks as indexed parallel clauses. Does it? Or does the parallel structure read as GRRM-Westerosi common speech, since GRRM characters frequently speak in rhetorical parallels regardless of age or intelligence? If the reader attributes the parallel structure to the setting rather than to the character's displaced register, the entire slip disappears. The line would then be a well-formed Westerosi courtesy speech, nothing more. The doubled register is load-bearing for the story, but it is doing its work invisibly — which means a reader who doesn't already know Taylor's base card will never notice the seam at all. Atmosphere earns. The slip doesn't.

- *pulp-enthusiast:* "Goodwife Mira. If it please you." — that's two dead beats before the board opens. Yes, the enumeration that follows is tight, and yes, the closer is a cost-clause, but the opening four words function as apologetic preamble and they slow the line to a politeness-register crawl before the tactical structure kicks in. The intent says the *structure* of the ask is the slip, but the line opens by demonstrating the child-surface, not the adult underneath, and the line doesn't shift into tactical register until the third sentence. A line that front-loads its cover register and buries its payoff in the parallel clauses is a line that can be skimmed past the payoff. If the board-move is in the architecture, the architecture needs to arrive first, or at least simultaneously with the softener — not two full beats after it.

- *worm-canon-pedant:* Here is the hardest push on the doubled register: the leaf card describes Taylor's surface vocabulary as "uncontracted Westerosi-formal in adult-facing speech" and her engineering slip as present "in the structure of the ask, not in the vocabulary." The writer has delivered exactly that — but the exact issue is that canon-Taylor's tactical pre-calculation is *always preceded by the silent version*. In Worm, she runs the analysis in her head and *then* speaks the conclusion, not the analysis. What she says to people under time-pressure is compressed and already resolved, not the indexed bullet-list of the analysis itself. "Three things, before he closes his board — that the letter was held out and named, that I called for the septon at the door, that the door did not open" is Taylor presenting her enumeration *out loud*, as if the enumeration is the request. But canon-Taylor would have resolved this to the ask before opening her mouth: she would say something like "Did you see it?" and the three items would be *implicit* in the ask, recoverable only by the inspector. The explicit indexed enumeration is the planning stage, not the delivery stage — and canon-Taylor's slip should be visible in the *delivery*, not in the planning structure leaking into speech. The writer has given us the planning voice instead of the delivery voice with the planning shape underneath it. Those are different things, and this is the one place where the leaf card's doubled-register framework may have been applied at the wrong stage of Taylor's internal process.

**Strongest single attack (the seam):** The structural slip depends on a reader knowing that eleven-year-olds don't enumerate parallel indexed clauses — but in a GRRM-register story where characters routinely speak in rhetorical parallels, that knowledge is not guaranteed, and a reader who attributes the structure to the setting rather than the character will read through the entire doubled register without registering it at all.

### TH-2 (taylor-hebert-westeros, mask: ON)
**Round 2 line:** "Septon Osmynd, ser. He is in the alcove. He has not risen today."
**Round 2 verdict:** ACCEPT

**Challenges (adversarial — the writer must defend or revise):**

- *dark-fantasy-reader:* The line earns by restraint — what it doesn't do is the card move. But restraint is the hardest thing to make *visible* in static review, and on the page it produces a line indistinguishable from a line that is simply sparse because the author didn't know what else to write. "Septon Osmynd, ser. He is in the alcove. He has not risen today." is three declaratives. Any novice authoring this scene in a quasi-medieval mode would write something similar, because three declaratives is the naturalistic response to a direct question from an authority figure. The claim is that the three-declarative stacking pattern is the base card's fragment-signature operating under mask-ON conditions. The counter-claim is that three declaratives is just what people say when an inspector asks a direct question and you don't want to embellish. The card can't take credit for every sparse answer. What specific feature of this line would be *wrong* if it came from a character who was not Taylor — who was just an eleven-year-old sept ward? The answer is: nothing. Nothing in this line would be wrong in that character's mouth. If the line can't be shown to be wrong for a non-Taylor speaker in this position, the doubled register is not doing demonstrable work, and the mask-ON demonstration is indistinguishable from a character who simply has no doubled register at all.

- *pulp-enthusiast:* The board is live in this scene — inspector with a pen, sick or dead septon in the alcove, a ward who knows things she shouldn't. And the line gives three declaratives and stops. The round 2 review calls this a board-mover because the inspector is now looking at a sick septon and a ward who answered too precisely. But "He has not risen today" is not *too precisely* answered — it is the most natural possible answer to the question "where is the septon." The precision is not visible in the line; it's in the review's interpretation of the line. A reader at speed gets three declaratives and then the scene continues. If the board move is invisible at speed, it's not doing board-move work. The line is defensible as setup-in-a-live-scene, but the stopping-point — which the writer correctly identifies as the mask working — lands to the pulp reader as the writer not knowing what comes next. A line that demonstrates competence through what it refuses to add is a line that requires the reader to already trust the writer's competence. That trust has not been paid for yet.

- *worm-canon-pedant:* Push hardest here. The review says "the absence of the closing-clause-twist is the on-card move" and "the stopping-point is the mask working." Both claims are true. But here is the hard version of the challenge: canon-Taylor in mask-ON mode does not give three declaratives and stop because she is *performing naturalness*. She gives three declaratives and stops because that is the exact minimum needed to close the question without volunteering. The word *today* — "He has not risen today" — is the load-bearing precision marker the writer and review identify correctly. But in canon, Taylor's precision under interrogation is always *slightly too calibrated for a civilian*, and the calibration shows in *what she chose not to say*. What she chose not to say here: that Osmynd is dying, that the alcove is where the dying are put, that *today* is a word that implies a pattern of not-rising that precedes today. A reader fluent in Worm knows that Taylor's lacunae are where the information lives. But a reader not fluent in Worm — which is most of this story's potential readership, since it is positioned as a crossover — reads "He has not risen today" as a cooperative, complete answer. The line is correct for canon-Taylor. The question is whether it is correct *for the audience this story is targeting*, who may not know to read the lacunae. The doubled register requires the reader to perceive the doubled register. The mask-ON line is the one place the reader is least equipped to do that.

**Strongest single attack (the seam):** A line that demonstrates character competence entirely through what it refuses to add requires the reader to already know what competence in this register looks like — and the crossover readership this story is targeting may simply read three spare declaratives as a child giving a spare answer, not as a tactical operator giving the minimum cooperative response under active interrogation.

### TH-3 (taylor-hebert-westeros, mask: SLIPPING — patched from "Twelve." to "Twelve, septon.")
**Round 2 line (patched):** "Twelve, septon."
**Round 2 verdict:** REJECT on original; patch applied per round 2 tuning recommendation.

**Challenges (adversarial — the writer must defend or revise):**

- *dark-fantasy-reader:* The patch is supposed to carry the slip mechanic: cold number arrives first, then the child-register softener re-engages *one beat after*, demonstrating the mask catching up to itself. This is the right mechanical description of what the line wants to do. The problem is that "Twelve, septon" does not feel like a beat of re-engagement — it feels like a polite child addressing her guardian while correcting him. "Twelve, septon" is what a well-brought-up ward says when she disagrees with a cleric. It is the least alarming possible correction, and it reads as the child-surface holding, not slipping. The original "Twelve." was rejected because a one-word line can't demonstrate the card. "Twelve, septon." was recommended as the fix because the vocative arrives late. But "septon" at the end of a correction is not a late arrival of the mask re-engaging — it's the conventional placement of address in a GRRM-era subordinate-to-superior construction. Every smallfolk character addresses superiors at the tail of sentences. The placement of "septon" at the end demonstrates the smallfolk/ward-register, not the mask-slip. The seam the line is supposed to carry has been closed by making the correction look polite rather than abrupt.

- *pulp-enthusiast:* Two words. The board is live — Rowan has just named a number that is wrong, a clock that matters, a route that will cost someone something if miscounted. And the correction is two words. The pulp reader is waiting for the escalation TH-4 will deliver, and TH-3 is supposed to be the hinge — the moment the correction comes out before the mask can stop it, the moment Rowan hears something wrong. Two words can carry that if and only if the first word lands hard enough to stop Rowan cold. "Twelve." stops him cold. "Twelve, septon." — the vocative softens the landing. The hinge moment is blunted by politeness. A cold number that immediately apologizes for itself in the same breath is not a mask slipping; it's a mask that caught the slip before it escaped. If the line's job is to make Rowan realize the ward is not what she presents as, the line needs to sound wrong-for-her, and "Twelve, septon" sounds exactly right for her.

- *worm-canon-pedant:* Pushing hardest. The intent says "the number comes out cold before the child-register can soften it. The slip is the line." The leaf card says the slip happens when the mask thins under pressure, and that "the brevity has an adult's certainty behind it." Now look at what the patch does: it adds the child-register vocative at the tail. In the original "Twelve." the adult's certainty was the entire line, unmediated. In "Twelve, septon." the adult's certainty occupies the first word and the child-register re-engages in the second word of the same breath. That is a recovery, not a slip. In canon, Taylor's slips are *unrecovered* in the moment they occur — they are noted by the observer, not corrected by Taylor in real-time. She doesn't slip and immediately re-mask within the same utterance in canon; she slips, and then the chapter-close or the next line acknowledges what slipped. "Twelve, septon." compresses both the slip and the recovery into a two-word line, which means the slip lasts approximately half a second before it is cancelled. That is not the doubled register surfacing under pressure; that is the doubled register managing a micro-exposure and immediately containing it. The mask-SLIPPING state is supposed to show register-failure, not register-management. The patch may have turned a rejected mask-slip line into an accepted mask-ON line wearing SLIPPING's label.

**Strongest single attack (the seam):** The late vocative is supposed to carry the slip — cold number first, child-register catching up — but "Twelve, septon." reads as one polite correction delivered in the conventional subordinate-to-superior tail-address form, meaning the vocative placement demonstrates ward-register performing correctly rather than the mask re-engaging after a failure. The slip and the recovery are simultaneous in the patch, which means there is no observable slip at all.

### TH-4 (taylor-hebert-westeros, mask: OFF)
**Round 2 line:** "The Kingsroad is the count he is making, septon. The river road is the road. Off the Kingsroad past the second milestone, the fords above Harroway are still running high — the melt has not gone yet, and a rider on a tired horse fords or swims, and either way he loses the morning. Past Harroway the cut is ungraded; that is half a day in wet ground. The climb above Cox's Ford is another. A day for each, and the count comes to twelve. Nine is the count if a man does not have to dismount."
**Round 2 verdict:** ACCEPT

**Challenges (adversarial — the writer must defend or revise):**

- *dark-fantasy-reader:* The line earns strongly and the closing-clause-twist is the best single moment in the TH block. But here is the atmospheric crack: mask FULLY OFF is supposed to mean the adult operator is visible and the speaker knows it. The review says "the line knows it" and "the audience that flags it as wrong-in-the-mouth is reading the line correctly." But the line does not register its own wrongness anywhere in its surface. Taylor is explaining road mathematics to a septon on a walking path, in detailed operational terms, without a single moment of self-awareness that she is doing something a ward of a dying septon has no business doing. In Worm, Taylor's mask-off moments are almost always accompanied by either a micro-beat of internal recognition (*she heard herself and—*) or a tell that the observer catches and the narrator acknowledges. Here the line is delivered flat — not flat in register, flat in consequence. There is no tremor in the line from the speaker knowing she has over-exposed. The darker-atmospheric version of this scene would have the over-exposure land in the line itself: a word too precise, a phrase she walks back one beat too late, something that registers as wrong even to her. What the writer has given is an adult-operator monologue in a child's mouth with no crack where the child knows she has slipped. That is mask-OFF with no consequence logic. A dark-fantasy reader expects the over-exposure to cost something.

- *pulp-enthusiast:* The line pays off TH-3 completely and the "nine is the count if a man does not have to dismount" closer is a clean reversal that lands. No complaint on board-move. The complaint is on rhythm. "Off the Kingsroad past the second milestone, the fords above Harroway are still running high — the melt has not gone yet, and a rider on a tired horse fords or swims, and either way he loses the morning. Past Harroway the cut is ungraded; that is half a day in wet ground. The climb above Cox's Ford is another." That is forty-eight words before the counting clause arrives. Forty-eight words of subordinated road-geography that a reader at speed will skim for the number. The pulp reader is not interested in the fords above Harroway; the pulp reader is interested in *why the ward knows this and what Rowan is about to do with the information*. The line buries its most interesting content — the wrongness of a child knowing this — under the content itself. If the tactical monologue is the mask-slip, the mask-slip needs to land in three sentences or fewer, or the escalation cost is absorbed by the geography and the reader gets to the closer having received information rather than alarm.

- *worm-canon-pedant:* Pushing very hard on the doubled register here. The mask is FULLY OFF — the base card is running. The base card's signature is "long sentences when planning, comma-chained run-ons in tactical exposition, closing-clause-twist that reframes the interlocutor's claim." All three are present. But here is the crack in the foundation: canon-Taylor does not give unsolicited operational briefings. She gives operational analysis when asked a direct question, when defending a position, or when she has identified an error that will cost someone. Here, Rowan has said "nine days." Taylor has said "twelve." Rowan has not yet asked *why*. That question — the S-2 line, "how is it that an eleven-year-old ward of my sept comes to count it twelve" — is what TH-4 is answering. So TH-4 is a direct-question response, which is canon-correct. But the level of unsolicited operational detail — the second milestone, the ungraded cut, the half-day in wet ground, the climb above Cox's Ford — goes well beyond answering "how do you know it's twelve" and into proving her road-assessment methodology to a man who did not ask for methodology. Canon-Taylor answers questions; she does not justify her answers with full analytical breakdown unless she is under adversarial scrutiny. A septon walking a road is not adversarial scrutiny. The register of this response is the register she uses with Director Costa or with Faultline, not with Septon Rowan on a pastoral walk. The line may be correct for the card's base-register, but it may be wrong for the scene's pressure level — the mask goes too far off for the stakes present.

**Strongest single attack (the seam):** The mask-FULLY-OFF state delivers an adult-operator road-assessment monologue with no internal registration of wrongness — the speaker knows she has over-exposed (the intent requires this) but nothing in the line's surface carries that self-awareness, which means the reader gets tactical information without the affective cost that the dark-fantasy register requires to make the over-exposure land as dangerous rather than merely informative.

### NC-1 (census-officer, westeros-noble-courtly, rung: functionary-courtly)
**Round 2 line:** "By commission of the castellan, and under the lord's hand, you shall hear me. All wards within the bounds of this sept, all souls without a holding, and any man or woman that cannot name a guardian seated in the cure — shall come forward to the board, and shall be set down by name, before this yard is quit. The roll closes at midday. None shall be entered after."
**Round 2 verdict:** ACCEPT

**Challenges (adversarial — the writer must defend or revise):**

- *dark-fantasy-reader:* The commission-language is correct and the tricolon is earned. But "By commission of the castellan, and under the lord's hand, you shall hear me" opens with a doubling — commission of the castellan AND under the lord's hand — that is redundant in the world of the text. A commissioner invoking both the castellan and the lord's hand in the same breath is either hedging his authority (which implies the authority is uncertain) or citing it twice because he doesn't fully own it (which is an interesting character note that the line doesn't acknowledge). In a world where lord's-hand authority is absolute and the castellan is its agent, you cite one or the other, not both. The doubling reads as a scribe's over-citation formula — correct on the page of a legal document, potentially wrong in the mouth of a man carrying the commission itself. If this officer is sufficiently confident in his commission, he says "under the lord's hand, you shall hear me" and stops there. The additional citation of the castellan is either a character tell (he doesn't fully believe his own authority) or a formula pasted in from the legal-document register rather than the public-speech register. The line earns on the card, but a careful reader of the world may catch that the authority is being asserted twice where once would suffice.

- *pulp-enthusiast:* The opening works. "The roll closes at midday. None shall be entered after." — those two closers are the board-move, and they land clean. The complaint is the middle: "All wards within the bounds of this sept, all souls without a holding, and any man or woman that cannot name a guardian seated in the cure — shall come forward to the board, and shall be set down by name, before this yard is quit." That sentence runs sixty words before the directive arrives. In a public carry-distance proclamation, sixty words before the imperative is a proclamation you stop listening to by word thirty. The tricolon is the card's structural signature, but in a spoken register, three long parallel clauses before the command means the people in the yard have time to start drifting before they know what to do. The line earns on the written-sentence level and loses on the spoken-aloud level. A functionary working a yard would shorten the tricolon to land the imperative faster, even at the cost of some rhetorical architecture.

- *worm-canon-pedant:* No Taylor contamination risk from this line — it's not adjacent to Taylor's register and Taylor doesn't speak here. The challenge is narrower: "any man or woman that cannot name a guardian seated in the cure" — the phrase "seated in the cure" appears in Westerosi institutional language throughout the episode, including in NC-3 and NC-4, and its meaning is established there as a term of art requiring a specific procedural act. But in NC-1, which is the opening commission speech, the phrase "seated in the cure" is used as a defining condition for who must come forward, without any definition of what it means. A smallfolk audience in the yard has no working definition of "seated in the cure" — it's a Faith-and-recorder institutional term, not common speech. A census officer addressing a yard of smallfolk, wards, and unattached people would not use the institutional term of art without glossing it for the audience. The line is correct in the noble-courtly register, but it may be wrong in the public-speech register — which is the register the intent specifically calls for ("audible at carry-distance, formal commission-language... should sound like a man invoking the lord's right," not like a man drafting a document).

**Strongest single attack (the seam):** "Seated in the cure" is a Faith-and-recorder institutional term of art used in a public carry-distance proclamation to a yard of smallfolk, without definition, where it functions as the key condition for who must come forward — which means the people most at risk of the census have no reliable way to know whether they qualify as "having a guardian seated in the cure" or not, and the officer has either made a procedural error or revealed that the commission language is intentionally opaque.

### NC-2 (plumm's man, westeros-noble-courtly, rung: mid-functionary)
**Round 2 line:** "Loose her, child. The bird shall fly, and you shall stand back from the perch."
**Round 2 verdict:** ACCEPT

**Challenges (adversarial — the writer must defend or revise):**

- *dark-fantasy-reader:* "Loose her, child. The bird shall fly, and you shall stand back from the perch." — the line earns on the card, and it is more atmospherically coherent than round 1's three-word reject. But "the bird shall fly" is a strange construction. It's not a command — "fly the bird" or "loose her and stand back" would be commands. "The bird shall fly" is predictive — it states what will happen as a consequence of compliance, rather than commanding the action directly. In the noble-courtly register, *shall* can carry this predictive weight, but it reads slightly as if the functionary is narrating the future rather than issuing an order. A man who expects compliance does not describe the consequences of compliance — he commands the action and expects the description to be unnecessary. "Loose her, child. Stand back from the perch." — two imperatives — is colder and more authoritative than the *shall*-predictive construction. The two-*shall* parallel is the card signature, but in this specific instruction, the *shall* works better on the second clause ("you shall stand back") than on the first ("the bird shall fly"), where it softens the order into a prediction.

- *pulp-enthusiast:* Clean, fast, in register. No real complaint on board-move — the raven goes, the ward steps back, the scene moves. The line is short enough and functional enough that the pulp reader is satisfied. The soft push: "you shall stand back from the perch" — *from the perch* is a redundant specification. If the ward has just loosed the bird, standing back from the perch is the only possible action; there is no alternative position she might have been standing back *toward* or *away from* that requires the prepositional specification. "You shall stand back" is cleaner. "From the perch" takes three words to say nothing that isn't already implied by "stand back after releasing a bird." The functionary's register is supposed to say no more than needed; "from the perch" is more than needed.

- *worm-canon-pedant:* No Taylor in this line. The contamination risk to Taylor's adjacent lines is the challenge here. NC-2 immediately precedes or follows Taylor handling the raven — the intent puts Taylor in the scene with the bird on her arm. "Loose her, child" is addressed to Taylor. This is one of the few lines where a non-Taylor speaker addresses Taylor directly, and the address-by-station ("child") rather than by name is the noble-functionary register. The risk: Taylor's response to this instruction — whatever it is — must be in the correct register for an eleven-year-old ward receiving a curt order from a Plumm's man, mask state TBD. "Child" as address sets up an expectation for Taylor's response register. If Taylor's adjacent line is in her base-card's adult register (pre-calculation, tactical), the contrast between "child" and Taylor's response will surface the doubled register whether or not that is the intended mask state for that moment. NC-2 functions as a register-pressure test on Taylor's adjacent line. The writer needs to decide whether Taylor's response is mask-ON (cooperative, child-surface) or mask-SLIPPING (some tell leaks), and NC-2's blunt address-by-station makes that decision harder to fudge.

**Strongest single attack (the seam):** "The bird shall fly" functions as a prediction of the consequence of compliance rather than a direct command, which in the noble-courtly register reads as a man narrating his own orders rather than issuing them — and a functionary cold enough to drop all honorifics should also be cold enough to issue direct imperatives rather than *shall*-predictive consequence-statements.

### NC-3 (district-recorder, westeros-noble-courtly, rung: institutional-bureaucratic)
**Round 2 line:** "The claim is heard, septon, and the claim is refused. A provisional appointment does not seat a man in the cure; the protocol shall not recognize it, and I shall not enter it against the line. Bring the letter of permanent appointment, and the matter shall be heard again."
**Round 2 verdict:** ACCEPT

**Challenges (adversarial — the writer must defend or revise):**

- *dark-fantasy-reader:* The line earns the card well. The atmospheric crack is subtler: "The claim is heard, septon, and the claim is refused." — the repetition of "the claim" in the opening pair is a rhetorical formality, the recorder mirroring the claim back to mark it received and then dismissed. That is correct courtly procedure. But "the claim is refused" is a passive construction that distributes the refusal to an implied institutional agent rather than to the recorder himself. Immediately after, however, "I shall not enter it against the line" — the recorder owns the refusal in first person. The line moves from passive-distributed ("the claim is refused" — by the protocol? by the recorder? by the procedure?) to first-person-owned ("I shall not enter it") within the same sentence pair. That is a small inconsistency in the voice of a man who, according to the intent, "is" the protocol — if he is the protocol speaking, everything should be institutional-passive or everything should be first-person; the mix signals either a character who is hedging between institutional shelter and personal authority, or a writer who switched registers mid-line without noticing.

- *pulp-enthusiast:* The refusal is clean and the pathway back is named. Board moves — Rowan has a requirement and a road. One push: "Bring the letter of permanent appointment, and the matter shall be heard again." The conditional structure here — bring X, and Y will happen — is technically correct but it is also the most bureaucratically gentle version of a refusal. A recorder who has just said "I shall not enter it against the line" does not soften the ruling by promising re-hearing in the same breath; he closes the transaction and lets the re-hearing be implied. Naming the re-hearing explicitly — "the matter shall be heard again" — is a courtesy that a cold proceduralist would omit. The courtesy softens the finality the line is supposed to carry: "polite procedural finality" is the intent, but the final word of the line is a promise of access ("heard again"), not a closing of the door. The closing beat should close, not open an escape hatch the functionary is advertising.

- *worm-canon-pedant:* No Taylor in this line. The adjacent-register contamination concern: NC-3 defines "seated in the cure" as the institutional condition Rowan's provisional appointment fails to meet. The recorder's ruling becomes the vocabulary framework that NC-4 (Plumm) then redeploys and Taylor eventually internalizes as the legal mechanism she is navigating. If the recorder's definition of "seat a man in the cure" is subtly imprecise — and "a provisional appointment does not seat a man in the cure; the protocol shall not recognize it" conflates *not being seated* with *not being recognized*, which are potentially two separate procedural failures — then the institutional vocabulary the story is building is slightly incoherent at its foundation. NC-4 specifies that a letter names a man to a cure but does not seat him — which is a distinction between naming and seating. NC-3 says a provisional appointment does not seat, which is correct but doesn't address whether a provisional appointment *names* to the cure or not. If Rowan's appointment is provisional, it may not have named him to the cure at all, in which case the question is not whether he is seated but whether he was ever named. The procedural flaw may be more fundamental than the recorder is stating, which would make the recorder's stated ground for refusal the lesser of two possible grounds — the one that allows re-hearing — rather than the more terminal ground that might apply.

**Strongest single attack (the seam):** The line opens with passive-institutional ("the claim is refused") and closes with first-person-owned ("I shall not enter it"), mixing the two modes of recorder authority in a way that signals either a writer's register-drift or a character who is uncertain whether he is the protocol or the man executing it — and that uncertainty undercuts the "polite procedural finality" the intent specifically requires.

### NC-4 (ser-harwick-plumm, westeros-noble-courtly, rung: knight-administrator — calibration target)
**Round 2 line:** "The good septon mistakes the ground, and I would not have him stand upon it longer than is needful. The recorder did not speak of jurisdiction; the recorder spoke of seating — of a hand set against the line, in a recorder's session, before the term is run out — and a letter of appointment, however lawful, however solemn, however well-witnessed by the motherhouse at Stoney Sept, names a man to a cure but does not seat him in it. I find the protocol satisfied in its plain reading; I find the cure unseated; and I find the matter to lie, this morning, where the recorder set it down."
**Round 2 verdict:** ACCEPT

**Challenges (adversarial — the writer must defend or revise):**

- *dark-fantasy-reader:* The line is the strongest single piece in the round 2 set, and the *I find* tricolon is the highest register moment in any of the 17 lines. The challenge is the *however*-tricolon inside the periodic sentence: "however lawful, however solemn, however well-witnessed by the motherhouse at Stoney Sept." The three *however*-clauses are supposed to build the weight of the letter before striking it down. But "however well-witnessed by the motherhouse at Stoney Sept" is Plumm naming the letter's strongest endorsement — the motherhouse — and then dismissing it. That dismissal should be the coldest, hardest moment in the line. Instead it's tucked inside the conditional clause that precedes the main predicate, and the main predicate ("names a man to a cure but does not seat him in it") is the argument-killer, not the dismissal of the motherhouse. The motherhouse is invoked and then immediately rendered irrelevant — not by Plumm addressing why the motherhouse doesn't matter, but by Plumm subordinating the motherhouse's endorsement to the procedural fact that naming and seating are different acts. A careful reader will notice that Plumm has successfully dismissed Faith jurisdictional authority (the motherhouse endorsement) without ever engaging its substance. That is either a brilliant procedural move by a knight-administrator who knows exactly what he is doing, or it is a writer avoiding the harder argument by burying it in subordinated grammar. The line needs to know which one it is.

- *pulp-enthusiast:* The *I find* tricolon is the best closer in the set. No dispute. The complaint is the opening: "The good septon mistakes the ground, and I would not have him stand upon it longer than is needful." Forty-five characters of subjunctive courtesy before the argument begins. Yes, "I would not have him stand upon it longer than is needful" is elegant and it establishes Plumm's register immediately — but in a scene where Rowan has just made a public doctrinal challenge in a yard, Plumm taking a full courtly sentence to say "he's wrong and I'd prefer he knew it" before stating *why* he's wrong delays the escalation. The pulp reader wants the ruling to hit the yard. The opening of NC-4 is the wind-up before the punch, and the wind-up is long enough that a reader who is not already invested in the procedural architecture will have skimmed past "needful" before the argument begins. The line earns as a written composition. It loses half a beat as a performed ruling in a public yard.

- *worm-canon-pedant:* No Taylor contamination. The challenge is about the line's relationship to adjacent Taylor material. NC-4's ruling — "the matter to lie, this morning, where the recorder set it down" — closes the procedural question in Plumm's favor. This is the scene's climactic institutional moment. What follows, narratively, is Taylor's position: she is the ward in question, she has just heard Plumm close the procedural door, and whatever she does next is her move. The concern: NC-4 is written at such a high register — the *however*-tricolon, the periodic sentence, the *I find* anaphora — that it performs as a set-piece speech rather than a ruling delivered in a yard. A set-piece speech is a text-artifact; a ruling in a yard is a thing people in the yard hear and react to. If Plumm's line is conceived as a set-piece, the reader experiences it as theater. **Strongest single attack (the seam):** The *however*-tricolon dismisses the motherhouse at Stoney Sept's endorsement — which is Rowan's strongest argument for Faith jurisdiction — not by engaging why the motherhouse's authority doesn't apply but by grammatically subordinating it to the procedural distinction between naming and seating, which means Plumm has evaded the harder doctrinal question while appearing to have answered it, and the line does not acknowledge whether this evasion is intentional strategy or a structural gap in the argument.

Mira's warning, if Taylor hears it, would be immediately processed by the adult-operator-in-Taylor as: my guardian situation is equivalent to *them as han't a man to name*, my status is contested, I am in the same category as the people Mira is warning. The question for the adjacent Taylor lines: does Taylor process Mira's warning correctly and immediately, or does she process it incompletely? In Worm, Taylor processes threat-relevant information immediately and accurately. If she is present for SF-1, she should be running the assessment before Mira finishes the sentence. If she is not present for SF-1, the scene needs to establish when she receives the equivalent information. The SF-1/Taylor adjacency is not a line problem; it is a scene-architecture problem the line exposes.

**Strongest single attack (the seam):** "The Mother won't say nay to it" is ambiguous between "the Mother sanctions this" and "the Mother won't intervene to stop this" — and in a dark-fantasy register, the ambiguity reads as a theological statement about passive gods rather than as a casual Faith-conversational, which may be giving the line a darker atmospheric weight than Mira, as a character, intends to carry.

### SF-2 (mira-stonefield, westeros-smallfolk, face-to-face refusal)
**Round 2 line:** "Ah, child. Nay. Nay, m'lady — child, beggin' your pardon, I've not the standing for it, I've not. The Mother knows I'd see you right if I were the one as could, but I in't, and the Father willing you'll find a goodwife with kin enough behind her to put her name where mine in't fit to go. Not for me to be saying that to a lord's man. Not me. Hush now."
**Round 2 verdict:** ACCEPT

**Challenges (adversarial — the writer must defend or revise):**

- *dark-fantasy-reader:* The m'lady slip and self-correction is the line's best moment — the deference reflex on a girl who reads upper, the card's register-tell. But "Hush now" as the closer — the kin-warm closer, the within-class softener — is doing two things at once that are in tension. "Hush now" is comfort-register, the older woman settling a child who has asked for something distressing. But the comfort-register closer after a refusal implies Mira is absorbing Taylor's distress rather than acknowledging the weight of what she has declined to do. In a dark-fantasy register, a refusal should leave something in the air — the cost of the refusal should be visible in the closer, not smoothed over by warmth. "Hush now" closes the refusal with gentleness, which is character-appropriate for Mira but is also the register-equivalent of tidying up after yourself. The line should end on the weight of what Mira did not do, not on her comfort for having done it. The gentler the closer, the more the reader feels that Mira is managing Taylor's reaction to protect herself from having to sit with the refusal's cost.

- *pulp-enthusiast:* The refusal is warm and the board-move is clear — Taylor is alone, Mira has declined, the stakes are raised. The complaint is the length. "Ah, child. Nay. Nay, m'lady — child, beggin' your pardon, I've not the standing for it, I've not. The Mother knows I'd see you right if I were the one as could, but I in't, and the Father willing you'll find a goodwife with kin enough behind her to put her name where mine in't fit to go. Not for me to be saying that to a lord's man. Not me. Hush now." That is eleven clauses. A refusal that takes eleven clauses to land is a refusal that is covering over something — either Mira's guilt (she is spending eleven clauses apologizing for the *nay*) or the writer's reluctance to let the refusal be clean. Smallfolk refusals, per the card, use indirection and trailing-softener. This line uses indirection, trailing-softener, *and* extended self-exculpation. The self-exculpation ("The Mother knows I'd see you right if I were the one as could, but I in't") is the line's weakest section — it explains Mira's internal state in a way that the card-register should imply rather than state. The card says she does not say she would lose her holding; she says no in the register that says no without saying no. Spending three clauses on "I would help you if I could" is coming close to saying the thing the card says she doesn't say.

- *worm-canon-pedant:* The m'lady slip is the worm-canon-pedant's deepest concern here. "Nay, m'lady" — Mira slips from *child* to *m'lady* because Taylor reads upper. In Worm canon, Taylor does not read upper; she reads working-class, South-side Boston, public-school register. The doubled register on the leaf card makes her read upper in certain framings — her adult-operator-facing-authority mode — but Taylor's base register in Worm is not a register that elicits upper-class deference from observers. Is the leaf card's Westerosi-child surface upper enough to produce an m'lady deference reflex in a Riverlands smallfolk woman? The leaf card says Taylor uses formal address correctly ("if it please you, ser," "Goodwife Mira"), which would read as high-register in a smallfolk context. If that is what Mira is responding to — the formal address pattern — the m'lady slip is earned. But if Mira is responding to Taylor's physical presentation, bearing, or material circumstances (she is a ward of a dying septon in a struggling sept), none of those would produce an m'lady deference reflex. The slip requires Mira to have read something in Taylor that reads upper, and the adjacent lines need to establish what that something is, because in canon, Taylor doesn't generally read upper to people who are not already in a hierarchical relationship with her.

**Strongest single attack (the seam):** "Hush now" as the closer absorbs the weight of Mira's refusal in a comfort gesture rather than leaving the refusal to sit in the air between them — which is character-consistent for Mira but is also the register-equivalent of self-absolution, and a dark-fantasy scene that ends on the refuser's comfort rather than the refused party's position has arranged the scene's moral weight in favor of the person who said no.

### SF-3 (grain-keeper's wife, westeros-smallfolk, threshold testimony)
**Round 2 line:** "M'lord. Beggin' your pardon, m'lord. Aye — aye, I seen her, the septon's girl, the little one as goes up to Osmynd's door — three times I seen her on the lane, this moon's turn just gone, or it might be a sennight more, I'd not swear closer than that. Once at dusk and twice afore midday, m'lord, with a basket like, and a kerchief on her, and not a word out of her either way. The Seven keep, I don't know nothin' beyond that. I don't. Just what me own eyes seen, m'lord, on me own threshold, comin' and goin' as she done."
**Round 2 verdict:** ACCEPT

**Challenges (adversarial — the writer must defend or revise):**

- *dark-fantasy-reader:* The card signatures are all present and the line earns. The atmospheric challenge: "The Seven keep, I don't know nothin' beyond that. I don't. Just what me own eyes seen, m'lord, on me own threshold, comin' and goin' as she done." — the poor-woman-disclaimer section is the card's move, and it's correct. But it is also the longest section of self-exculpation in any smallfolk line in the set, and it runs three full clauses after the information has already been delivered. "I don't know nothin' beyond that. I don't. Just what me own eyes seen... on me own threshold, comin' and goin' as she done." This is a woman apologizing for having seen what she saw, for three clauses. In a dark-fantasy register, a woman who is half-helpful and half-uneasy about testifying to an inspector is more interesting when the uneasy part shows in what she offers *and* what she holds back, rather than in the length of her disclaimer. Three clauses of "I don't know more than this" is the disclaimer of a woman who knows she is saying too much and is trying to cover herself retroactively. The unease should be in the telling, not in the apology for the telling.

- *pulp-enthusiast:* The witness testimony lands — specifics named, times of day given, three sightings confirmed. Board updated: Taylor is being placed by sight in a pattern. The complaint is the "aye — aye" opening with em-dash. Same issue as SF-1: the em-dash pivot here is structural — it separates the aye-reflex from the testimony. In SF-3, the em-dash after "Aye — aye" is functioning as a chassis pivot, not as a sensory specifier. "Aye, aye, I seen her" with comma-coordination would be smallfolk-correct and em-dash-free. The em-dash appears again in "the septon's girl, the little one as goes up to Osmynd's door —" as a pivot from the identification to the location. Two em-dashes in one line, and one of them is structural. The writing-bar rule is under pressure.

- *worm-canon-pedant:* The line places Taylor on the lane three times this past moon's turn. This is testimony that could be used to establish Taylor's movement pattern — in Worm terms, it is intelligence about a subject's behavioral routines. The inspector is gathering this intelligence for Plumm, which means Plumm is developing a picture of Taylor's activities. In Worm canon, Taylor's threat-assessment capability would register the intelligence-gathering risk long before it reaches this point. If she has been passing this woman's threshold with a basket, the adult operator inside her should have considered whether that creates a recognizable pattern. "Comin' and goin' as she done" — the witness is describing behavior that Taylor, in Worm-register, should have been aware was observable. If Taylor has been moving in a recognizable pattern without considering the surveillance risk, that is a character error for canon-Taylor, whose situational awareness in Worm extends to identifying observation posts and pattern-recognition vulnerabilities. The line's testimony reveals not just Taylor's movements but a potential hole in Taylor's operational thinking that the story needs to acknowledge or explain.

**Strongest single attack (the seam):** The grain-keeper's wife's testimony — three sightings, specific times, specific route — describes a behavioral pattern that canon-Taylor's operational situational awareness should have identified as an intelligence-gathering vulnerability before it was ever observable; if Taylor has been establishing a visible routine without considering the surveillance risk, that is a character departure the story needs to address, not a testimony-scene problem.
