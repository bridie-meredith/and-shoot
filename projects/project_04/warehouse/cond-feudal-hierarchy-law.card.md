# Feudal Hierarchy Law — Westerosi Legal Structure at 84–101 AC (Jaehaerys I)

## Description

The underlying legal structure of the Westerosi feudal hierarchy as it applies in the Riverlands at 84–101 AC. Covers: what each level of the hierarchy can demand from those below it, nominal Faith-provided protections for smallfolk, what recourse (essentially none) smallfolk have in practice, and how the hierarchy reads a smallfolk who acts as though she has agency she does not legally possess.

**Distinct from `cond-westerosi-customary-authority-jaehaerys`**, which governs the in-the-moment behavioral physics of deference. This card governs the underlying legal structure — what the law actually permits and prohibits, and what Taylor is doing wrong, legally, when she organizes.

## Sensory Impact

The law has no ambient sensory expression. Its expression is felt in moments of institutional contact: the summons that arrives with the lord's seal, the record that Pryor reads aloud in a formal setting, the administrative document Taylor is required to sign.

## Duration

Persistent. The feudal hierarchy law does not change within 84–101 AC.

## The Hierarchy

**Iron Throne (King Jaehaerys I):** Ultimate source of law and authority. Can demand any form of tribute, service, or levy; the person of any subject for trial; override of any lord's ruling. Felt in Fairstead through layers. Direct royal involvement in Taylor's situation would require her to have reached a level of significance that brings her to Tully's attention and from there to the king's court.

**Great House Tully (Riverrun):** The paramount lord of the Riverlands. All Riverlands lords hold their lands from Tully. Tully can demand: military levies from bannermen, administrative compliance, attendance at Riverrun for important occasions, resolution of inter-lord disputes.

**House Ryger of Willow Wood (minor Tully bannerman):** Legal powers over Fairstead and its inhabitants include: tax collection, labor corvée, military levy (in wartime), market-day authority, and low justice (try and punish smallfolk for offenses below capital severity) and high justice (try for capital crimes, with nominal appeal possible to Tully).

**Smallfolk (Fairstead residents including the Ashford family):**
- No right to organize collectively without lord's permission
- No right to refuse lord's administrative demands without formal legal grounds
- No right to leave the lord's territory without permission
- No right to bear arms without lord's permission
- No right to conduct legal proceedings against a lord or the lord's designated agents

**What smallfolk do have in law:**
- The Faith's nominal protection of the most basic human dignities
- The right to petition (nominally — requires getting the petition past the lord's administrative apparatus)
- Informal custom: certain practices have customary protection even without legal backing

## What Taylor Is Doing Wrong, Legally

**Unlawful collective action:** Smallfolk organizing against the lord's administrative apparatus is breach of the peace under the lord's low-justice authority — actionable without specific enumeration.

**Unauthorized practice of knowledge:** Using literacy in an adversarial way against the lord's interests redefines Taylor from "educated child" to "instrument of resistance."

**Unlawful assembly (if it gets that far):** The lord's authority to disperse unlawful assembly is clear and well-established. "Unlawful" does not require advance prohibition — the lord decides in the moment.

**The power that the law never anticipated:** Available categories: sorcery (undefined, handled ad hoc), witchcraft (handled under Faith authority, but Faith cannot enforce without lord's cooperation), or simply "the lord decides this is a problem." The lord's high-justice authority extends to cases that don't fit established categories.

## Faith-Provided Nominal Protections (and Their Practical Limits)

The Faith claims to protect the welfare of the poor as a general religious obligation. Without the Faith Militant, enforcement is entirely social and political. The lord can read a septon's letter, acknowledge it respectfully, and continue doing whatever he was doing. The Faith's protective function is a social pressure mechanism, not a legal one.

## Smallfolk Recourse (Essentially None)

**Formal recourse:**
- Petition to the lord: he adjudicates cases involving his own household. Success rate for smallfolk petitions against lord's men: approximately zero.
- Petition to Tully: requires the petition to travel through the lord's administrative channels. The lord's channels will not forward a petition against the lord's interests.
- Appeal to the Faith: the septon can advocate. The lord can ignore the septon.

**Informal recourse:**
- Flight: most effective recourse and the one the law is least equipped to prevent if executed carefully.
- Collective noncompliance: slow work, hidden stores, unreported production. Invisible and thus not prosecutable unless caught.
- The whisper chain as political pressure: reputational damage to the lord. Real informal pressure; not legally cognizable.

## How the Hierarchy Reads Taylor

1. **Mentally affected (touched):** Not legally accountable. Protective (handled by septon, not lord's court) and limiting (has no legal standing).

2. **Agent of a higher power:** Being directed by someone with more standing. Makes her legally dangerous — the lord will want to know who the patron is.

3. **An actual legal problem:** Smallfolk acting outside legal constraints without obvious impairment or obvious patronage. The lord decides how to handle a case the law did not anticipate. His discretion is wide.

Taylor's organizing success gradually moves her from category 1 toward category 3 in Pryor's assessment. The Stage 3 threshold in `cond-suppression-policy-progression` is the point where the lord has decided she is category 3 and is taking appropriate action.
