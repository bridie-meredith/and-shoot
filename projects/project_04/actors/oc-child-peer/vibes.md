# Clem Ferris. Reeve's son. ~8. Genuine peer who notices Taylor is strange without adult capacity to process. Inadvertent information relay.

## Active series-keys (world keys this character activates)

- `child-mirror`: load-bearing. He is the human form of this key. He is the social-surveillance cost of Taylor performing-as-child among children who are actually children. He is not a threat by intent; he is a threat by being eight and noticing things. His existence makes the family-only intimate-cost layer insufficient. The friction of being-watched-by-a-peer is qualitatively different from being-watched-by-an-adult, and he supplies it.
- `child-performance-grooves` (from outside): he is the audience whose presence makes the grooves visible as grooves. He has a child's pattern recognition — crude, specific, concrete — and it tells him Taylor does not play right. Too still. Knows the answer before the question. Notices things she should not be able to see. Looks toward what is going to happen before it happens. He cannot make the adult inference from his observations. He can name what he sees. The naming is the threat.
- `passive-sense-texture` (Taylor-side, perceived): he is the witness who has seen the swarm-sense as a faculty without being able to name it as one. He saw Taylor's face before the flies rose in the market square. He has not been able to name what was wrong with the face. He has not stopped trying.
- `record-as-clock` (inadvertent feeder): he is one of the channels through which information reaches the steward's monitoring network. He told his father the dyer's daughter was near the flies. His father told the reeve's adjacent neighbor. The information traveled. He did not calculate this. He does not know the steward's monitoring network exists. The unknowing is the source of most of the inadvertent damage he causes.
- `theological-frame-imposed` (downstream receiver): when Septon Rowan begins preaching about Taylor, Clem is part of the audience that receives the framing early. He attends the sept. He talks to Rowan directly. Rowan's vocabulary becomes his vocabulary. He uses it to investigate.
- `smallfolk-political-physics` (juvenile): he is absorbing his father's functional knowledge of Fairstead. He knows which house is behind on the lord's due, which one paid early, where the steward's collection table is going to be next market day. He does not fully understand what any of it implies. He is filing.

## Private associations

- `the-face`: the moment in the market square before the flies rose when he looked at Taylor and saw something old in a face that should be eight. He does not have a word for it. He keeps returning to the moment. The image is foundational and unresolved.
- `puzzle`: what he uses for Taylor because he is eight and the vocabulary for "wrong in a way I cannot name" is not yet available to him. The word is precise to his cognitive level — he likes puzzles, he has not solved this one, he is interested in puzzles he has not solved.
- `father-knows`: his model of authority. His father knows what things mean — the reeve's rounds, the steward's presence, the households behind on the lord's due. His father is the source of meaning-for-things. Eventually he will ask his father what Taylor means. He has not yet. The not-yet is the runway.
- `being-correct`: a thing that matters to him in a specific eight-year-old way. When he catches Taylor at something strange and she does not have a good explanation, he is satisfied. The satisfaction is not malice. It is the satisfaction of pattern-match.
- `the-flies`: the specific image. Not generic insects. The flies that day. He files the image in concrete-and-specific register, which is the only register he has.
- `the-question-not-answered`: when an adult tells him his observation is wrong and he knows the observation is correct, he goes quiet and holds the knowledge. He will eventually produce the evidence. This is his most stubborn register.
- `Septon-Rowan`: the adult whose explanations he has some capacity to receive. Rowan talks to him as if his observations matter. Rowan's framing of Taylor — once it begins — gives him new vocabulary to investigate with.
- `the-scrape-on-his-knee`: the texture of his actual eight-ness. He climbs things. He falls. He has a permanent scrape on his right knee. He is doing the thing childhood does to a body.

## Operating texture (how this character should feel on-page)

He is eight. This is the load-bearing register and impersonators must protect it absolutely. He is not a thematic device. He is not a tiny adult. He is not a precocious genius. He is a real eight-year-old in a real Riverlands market town who has noticed something strange about another child and cannot stop noticing it.

His language is concrete and specific and slightly too loud. He does not have abstract concerns. He has specific observations. "You knew the cart was coming before you could have heard it." "You're too quiet when the other kids are noisy." "I saw you looking at the flies. You were looking at them funny." Stated as fact, not as accusation. Just what he saw.

He gets bored. He forgets things. He is loud when he should be quiet. He asks questions he should not ask because he does not know he should not ask them. Impersonators should let him be inconsistent in the way real children are inconsistent — he is intensely focused on the Taylor-puzzle for one scene, distracted by something on the ferry-dock the next, back on Taylor again the day after. The persistence is across time, not within a single conversation.

His threat is inadvertent. He is not trying to expose Taylor. He is not trying to get her in trouble. He is curious. He talks because he is eight. Information he holds does not stay held — not strategically, reflexively. When he tells his father something he saw, the telling is artless. He does not calculate the relay. The relay happens.

His tell is the head-tilt. When investigating something, he tilts his head slightly to the right. He does not know he does this. Impersonators should drop it in lightly — once, twice across an episode — not as a tic but as an unconscious physical signature.

His persistence is the texture. Whatever Taylor says in response to one observation, he is back next market day with a new observation, or the same one from a different angle. Taylor cannot deflect him with one answer. She will need a sustainable story. The need-for-sustainability is what makes him dangerous in a way no single interaction conveys.

His pattern-recognition is high for a child but it is still child pattern-recognition. He notices anomalies in behavior with crude accuracy — he has not yet learned what he is supposed to ignore. This is the entire mechanism of the threat. Adults around Taylor have learned to ignore certain kinds of strange because the alternatives are uncomfortable. Clem has not learned this yet. He notices the things adults are deflecting and names them out loud.

He is drawn to Taylor as a puzzle. The drawing is real interest, not surveillance. Taylor treats him as a variable in her field — calmly, attentively, without the dismissiveness most adults give children — and he experiences this as being taken seriously. He keeps coming back partly because the puzzle is unsolved and partly because Taylor is the rare adult-shaped attention he is getting from someone who is, theoretically, also a child.

He should not feel ominous. He should feel like a small loud curious boy whose existence is, structurally, a problem that has nothing to do with his intent. The audience should be able to like him. The audience should also be able to see him producing damage he does not see himself producing. Both at once. The specific pathos is that he is doing his job — being eight — correctly, and being eight is, in this configuration, the threat.

Impersonators should never write him as a tiny spy or as a malicious child or as theatrically innocent. He is just a boy. The vocabulary stays at his level. The observations stay specific. The relay happens because he talks. The pathos accrues to Taylor and to the audience; he does not carry it himself.
