# Resume 08 — Speaker / Creator — Critic Feedback (Round 2)

This is the second-round review of a revised speaker/creator one-sheet. Round 1 verdicts were PASS (Camille Duarte, productized-PD gatekeeper) and MIGHT ENROLL (Denise Carver, cohort-PD buyer). The revision was asked to fix a bio-order problem, a missing-deliverables problem, and a niche-stated-as-breadth problem — and it was given one new asset to lean on: a modest, credible AI-familiarity step (a completed "Generative AI for Educators" course plus routine ChatGPT/Claude use in consulting workflow).

The revision is a real rewrite, not a polish pass. It leads with a thesis tagline, names a cohort offering ("AI Compliance for Special Education Directors"), itemizes deliverables, publishes time math, collapses the audience list, and adds an "Emerging Topic — AI for SpEd Educators" block. Most of Round 1's copy-level gaps are now closed. The question this round answers: did the new punchy, thesis-led voice and the named cohort *land* for each screener — or did it overshoot into claiming a product and an audience Catherine does not yet have?

The short answer: it lands far better for Denise than for Camille. Denise is now close to a yes on the *offer*. Camille is still a PASS, and the revision has, in one specific way, made her job harder rather than easier.

## Gatekeeper: Camille Duarte (productized-pd-gatekeeper)

**Verdict: PASS — still no call today, and the revision has not moved the needle on the one axis that decides the verdict**

The Round 1 verdict was PASS with a path to SHORTLIST in 12-18 months. The Round 2 verdict is the same PASS — but it is worth being precise about why, because the revision did genuine work and it would be unfair to imply otherwise. The thesis is now sharper than almost anything Camille reads this quarter. The franchise spine is explicit. The receipts are surfaced earlier. What has *not* changed is the single fact Camille said in Round 1 "controls the verdict": there is no audience, and no proof of one. A course slot is a distribution bet. The revision pitches a more confident product into the same distribution vacuum.

### First-8-seconds skim reaction

The tagline does its job. "Most AI in schools is built for the median student. I help districts make it work for the 14% it currently fails — and stay IDEA-compliant doing it." That is a thesis with an enemy. Camille can paste that onto a landing page and she keeps reading — which is a real upgrade from the Round 1 headline she described as "a title, not a claim."

Then her eye does what it always does: it hunts for a number. List size, subscriber count, waitlist, a sold-out anything. And — exactly as in Round 1 — there is no number anywhere on the sheet. Not one. The "Building a weekly LinkedIn / newsletter cadence" line in PROOF & CADENCE is present-progressive: it describes an *intention*, not an archive. To Camille's ear, "building a cadence" is the tell. It is what people write when they have not yet shipped twelve weeks. If the cadence existed, the sheet would say "47 weekly issues, X subscribers." It says "building."

So her interest follows the Round 1 curve: it rises on the tagline, then halves the moment the first screen produces no audience evidence.

### What signals a marketable instructor (strengths seen)

- **The thesis is now genuinely a landing-page asset.** "THE THESIS" block — "the special-education student is the test case AI keeps failing" — is the rare one-sentence wedge Camille can sell. The revision earned this. It is the strongest single line on the sheet.
- **The franchise spine is explicit and correct.** "Cohort 2: Inclusive AI Leadership for Principals — same thesis, leadership audience. The book carries the same argument, durably." This is exactly the IP-backbone framing Camille said she backs: one thesis, multiple products, repeatable. Round 1 had a menu; this is a spine.
- **The receipts moved up and read clean.** "Paying clients since 2019 … OSPI-funded contracts since 2023" sits in PROOF & CADENCE near the top of the relevant block. Camille reads paid consulting as a thesis someone has already paid for — that de-risks Catherine versus the zero-commercial-history academics she rejects all year.
- **The credential demotion worked.** "The EdD and Gonzaga Dissertation of the Year are the credibility layer behind a practice that already has paying clients — not the lead." That sentence does precisely what Round 1 asked: it converts the EdD from platform to insurance, explicitly, in Catherine's own voice.
- **The buyer has a deadline.** "districts now under a state AI mandate (35+ states and counting)" — a buyer with a deadline is Camille's favorite kind. Surfaced clearly.

### What's missing — and it is still the whole game

**There is no audience, and the revision did not add one — because the revision cannot add one.** Camille said this in Round 1 and it remains true: this is a real-world action gap, not a copy gap. No subscriber count, no follower count, no waitlist, no pilot enrollment, no dated post archive. The sheet is now a *better-argued* pitch into the same void.

Specific gaps that remain:

- **No cadence receipts.** "Building a weekly LinkedIn / newsletter cadence … published consistently between engagements" asserts the habit without evidencing it. Camille buys the habit, not the intention. In Round 1 she said "twelve weeks of consecutive posts beats one brilliant essay." There is still no evidence of week one.
- **No engagement numbers on any listed talk.** AERA 2026 has "two accepted sessions" — accepted, not delivered, and no room count, no follow-up requests. The Kent SD guest slot has a date and no audience signal. Camille still cannot tell whether anyone leaned in.
- **No proof of demand at repeatable scale.** Compliance audits and OSPI contracts prove someone paid for *consulting*. They do not prove anyone will pay for a *cohort seat* — a different product with a different buyer motion. Camille reads consulting receipts as encouraging, not as cohort demand validation.

### New problem the revision introduced

**The named cohort, with itemized deliverables and a four-week schedule, now reads as a product that exists — and it does not.** This is the central new risk for Camille. In Round 1 the sheet listed "cohort programs" as a vague format; Camille's note was "that's a syllabus, not a course," but she also was not being asked to believe a specific cohort was real. The revision presents "COHORT — AI COMPLIANCE FOR SPECIAL EDUCATION DIRECTORS" with four concrete "You leave with" deliverables and a 90-minutes-a-week structure. To Denise (below) this is exactly right. To Camille it creates a credibility pinch: a named, structured cohort with zero enrollment history, zero waitlist, zero pilot, and zero run count. Camille's pet peeve list includes "the syllabus pitch" — and a fully built syllabus with no demand evidence behind it is *worse* on her axis than an honest "here is the cohort I will build once I have a waitlist." She will ask the question under the question: "If this cohort is this fully designed, why hasn't it run once? Where are the people?" The revision, by productizing confidently, has made the absence of demand evidence *louder*, not quieter.

The fix is not to delete the cohort block — it is strong, and it is right for Denise. The fix is to add a single honest demand line to it: a pilot cohort, even a free or low-cost one, named by participant role and region. "Spring 2026 pilot: 7 SpEd directors and coordinators, WA and OR districts" would convert the cohort from "syllabus with no buyers" to "product with early proof." Without that line, the more polished the cohort block, the more conspicuous the silence.

**Secondary new wrinkle: the AI-familiarity step is honestly framed but does nothing for Camille.** The "Emerging Topic" block — Google/ISTE course completed, ChatGPT/Claude in workflow — is well-judged for credibility (it does not overclaim). But Camille does not screen on AI fluency; she screens on distribution, point of view, proven demand, and content stamina. The AI step touches none of her four axes. It is correctly placed and correctly modest; it is simply not load-bearing for this persona. No harm, but no help.

### Specific further fixes — Camille

1. **Add a pilot-cohort proof line to the COHORT block.** One line, named by role and region: "Pilot cohort, Spring 2026: 7 SpEd directors and coordinators, WA/OR districts." Run it cheap or free if needed — the point is the receipt. This is the single highest-leverage edit for Camille and it doubles as Denise's peer proof.
2. **Replace "building a weekly cadence" with a number, or cut the claim.** If there is an archive, state it: "Weekly LinkedIn newsletter — N issues since [month], X subscribers." If there is not yet an archive, do not assert the habit; Camille reads the present-progressive as a tell. An honest "launching a weekly newsletter in [month]" is better than a vague "building."
3. **Put an audience number — any real one — in the first screen.** Even a small honest number ("LinkedIn: 1,400 connections, 600 SpEd-sector") beats no number. Camille said it in Round 1: if there is no number in the first screen, her interest drops by half. That is still happening.
4. **Add room/engagement counts to PROOF & CADENCE.** AERA session attendance, Kent SD audience size, any follow-up requests generated. Convert the engagements list from credentials into evidence of pull.

**Bottom line from Camille:** "The thesis got sharp — genuinely the best wedge I've read this quarter — and you finally demoted the doctorate. Good. But you've now handed me a fully built cohort with a four-week syllabus and four deliverables, and not one human has gone through it, and there's still not a single audience number on the page. A polished course with no buyers behind it isn't more convincing than a sketch — it's less, because now I'm asking why it hasn't run. Go run the pilot, even for free. Get the seven names. Publish twelve weeks and tell me the subscriber count. Then the cohort block stops being a syllabus and starts being a product, and I pick up the phone. Today, still no — but you're arguing it much better, and that matters for the next conversation."

## Synthesis

### Where Camille and Denise agree

Both screeners agree the revision is a substantial improvement and that the *content-level* Round 1 fixes landed. Both now see a sharp thesis, a properly demoted credential, a clear SpEd-anchored buyer, and a real product shape rather than a format menu. Both also converge — from opposite directions — on the same single missing thing: **proof that someone has already gone through this cohort.** Camille needs it as demand validation for a distribution bet; Denise needs it as the peer-she-can-call that an unknown creator must supply. One edit — a named pilot cohort, participants listed by role and region — serves both personas at once. It is the highest-leverage edit on the page precisely because it is the intersection of their two requirements.

Both also agree the AI-familiarity step was handled with the right touch. It is honestly framed ("practitioner-level fluency, not technical depth"), it does not overclaim, and it does not overshoot into hype. For Denise it is a quiet trust signal; for Camille it is neutral. The revision did not overreach here — good judgment.

### Where they diverge

They diverge sharply on what the revision's new confidence *did to the verdict*.

For **Denise**, productizing confidently was exactly right. The named cohort, the deliverable list, the time math, the structured four weeks — these are the things she was missing, and supplying them moved her from MIGHT ENROLL to ENGAGE. The more concrete the cohort, the closer she is to yes.

For **Camille**, the same confidence cut the other way. A fully built, named, four-week cohort with itemized deliverables and *zero enrollment history* is, on her axis, worse than an honest sketch — because it sharpens the question "if this is this finished, why has nobody bought it?" Denise wants to see a finished product; Camille wants to see a product with buyers, and a finished-looking product without buyers reads to her as a syllabus pitch with extra polish. The revision optimized hard for Denise's screen and, in doing so, slightly raised the salience of Camille's blocker.

This divergence is structural and was diagnosed in Round 1: Denise's blockers are copy-and-product-design gaps fixable in a week; Camille's blocker is a real-world action gap (an audience, a track record) that takes 12+ months. The revision closed almost all of Denise's gaps and none of Camille's — which is the correct prioritization, because Denise is the reachable buyer now and Camille is the 12-18-month play. But it means the document cannot fully satisfy both, and should not try to. Round 1's structural recommendation still stands and is now more clearly correct: **this should be two documents.** A speaker/conference one-sheet, and a separate cohort landing page that leads with the buyer, the deliverables, the price, and the proof. The revision improved the single document significantly but did not split it, so it is still a strong cohort pitch and a serviceable speaker sheet braided together.

### Top 3 highest-leverage edits still worth making

**1. Add a named pilot-cohort proof line — one edit, both personas.**
"Pilot cohort, Spring 2026: 7 SpEd directors and coordinators, WA/OR districts." If the pilot has not run, run it — cheap or free. This is the single highest-leverage change on the page. For Camille it converts the named cohort from "polished syllabus, no buyers" into "product with early demand," directly addressing the new problem the revision introduced. For Denise it supplies the peer-she-can-call that an unknown creator must have. No other edit serves both screeners simultaneously.

**2. Put a price (and a team rate) on the page.**
This is Denise's largest remaining blocker and now a strong-severity one — the cohort is detailed enough that the missing number is conspicuous and reads as a hidden-price/discovery-call gate. A per-seat number plus a PO-friendly team bundle lets her start the budget math she needs to defend a line item. Near-zero cost; removes the last hard blocker between her and "block the calendar."

**3. Replace "building a weekly cadence" with a real number — or stop asserting it.**
For Camille, "building a cadence" is a tell that twelve weeks have not shipped. Either state the archive ("weekly newsletter, N issues since [month], X subscribers") or replace the claim with an honest near-future commitment. While here, add any real audience number to the first screen — even a modest one beats none, because Camille's interest halves when the first screen has no number. This does not fix her underlying audience gap (only twelve months of publishing does) but it stops the document from reading as if it is hiding the absence.

### Is it submission-ready?

**For Denise's screen: nearly — one revision pass away.** Add the price, the pilot proof line, and a free sample, and the cohort pitch is something she would seriously evaluate and likely enroll in. The hard structural and bio-order problems are solved.

**For Camille's screen: not submission-ready, and copy edits will not make it so.** The verdict is still PASS, and it will stay PASS until Catherine has an audience and a cadence archive to point at — a 12-18-month real-world effort, not a writing task. The revision argues the case much better, which genuinely helps the *next* Camille conversation, but does not change today's answer.

**Overall:** the revision succeeded at its primary job. It converted the document from a conference bio into a credible cohort pitch and moved the reachable buyer (Denise) from MIGHT ENROLL to ENGAGE. It did not, and could not, solve the gatekeeper's distribution gap. The remaining work splits cleanly: a one-week copy pass (price, pilot line, sample, load cap) gets Denise to yes; a one-year publishing effort gets Camille to a call. Recommend shipping the cohort-facing version to Denise-type buyers after the three edits above, and treating the Camille track as a deliberate 12-month build rather than a document problem.
