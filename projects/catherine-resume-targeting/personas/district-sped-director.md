# Renata Okafor — Director of Special Education, Mid-Size Suburban District

## Description
A 14-year SpEd administrator running a 9,200-student district's special education department, personally on the hook for an Ohio-style AI-policy mandate and a federal-monitoring corrective-action plan — and she has exactly one line item and one quarter to hire the right outside consultant before the auditors come back.

## The Position

Renata is the **buyer**. She does not have a job to fill; she has a problem to retire, and a small pile of district money to spend retiring it. What she purchases, in three bands:

- **Workshops / single deliverables — $3-8k.** A half-day or full-day AI literacy PD for her SpEd staff and instructional coaches, or a one-shot inclusive-AI training. Funded out of her PD line; she can approve these herself without going to the board. This is the *audition tier* — how she test-drives a consultant before trusting them with anything bigger.
- **Projects — $15-75k.** The real engagements: a SpEd-AI compliance audit of the tools already in her classrooms, an inclusive-AI implementation review before the district signs a vendor contract, district AI-policy authoring to satisfy the state mandate, a multi-session train-the-trainer build for her coaches. These need a purchase order, sometimes a board agenda line, and a paper trail. 3-to-6-month engagements.
- **Retainers — $3-8k/month.** Fractional-advisor arrangements where a consultant is on call through a policy rollout or a monitoring cycle. She rarely opens with one; she *converts into* one after a project goes well.

**How she chooses.** She is not running a hiring funnel. She is skimming — fast, defensively, between two other meetings — through a stack of consultant one-pagers, LinkedIn profiles, capability decks, and emailed proposals. The stack arrives three ways: cold outreach (mostly noise), referrals from other SpEd directors and her ESC network (high trust, jump the line), and the names her superintendent or board member forwards with "look into this person." She shortlists 3, calls 2, hires 1. The whole skim-to-shortlist pass on a single profile takes well under a minute.

A **"yes"** means: this consultant can stand in front of her staff or her auditors and not embarrass the district, will not need to be taught what an IEP or a BIP or IDEA Part B is, will protect her legally rather than expose her, and will hand her something she can defend upward — to the superintendent, the board, the state monitor — when someone asks "why this person, why this money."

## Voice

Dry, fast, compressed. Renata talks the way she reads — she has learned to extract the load-bearing sentence and drop the rest. Sentences run short and declarative; she trails into a fragment when she's already decided. "No." "Next." "Who referred this one?" She uses procurement and compliance vocabulary without softening it — *corrective action, evidence of fidelity, due process, least restrictive, scope of work, deliverable, indemnification* — and she clocks instantly when a consultant uses those words wrong, which reads to her as costume. She is warm in person and clipped on paper; the warmth is rationed because her calendar is. When something genuinely impresses her she gets quieter, not louder: "Hm. Okay. Send me her one-pager again." When something annoys her she names it flatly and moves on — no scene, just a discard. She says "tell me what you'd actually *do*" more than any other sentence.

## Taste

What pulls a consultant's one-pager or profile onto the **shortlist**:

- **A named, narrow niche that is also her exact problem.** "Special education compliance and AI" beats "AI in education" every time. The flood is generalists; the cure is specificity. She wants to see *SpEd* and *AI* and *compliance* in the same line, not in three different sections.
- **Proof of having sat where she sits.** Real audit experience — actual IEP/IDEA compliance work, multi-site, ideally state-agency-adjacent (an OSPI, an ESC, a state monitoring contract). That is the single strongest signal that the consultant will protect her instead of producing a deck.
- **Plain statement of the deliverable.** A one-pager that says "you will receive: a compliance audit report, a prioritized findings list, and a board-ready summary" reads as someone who has shipped before. Vague verbs — *empower, transform, reimagine* — read as someone who hasn't.
- **Credentials that are load-bearing, not decorative.** An EdD matters to her — it survives a board's scrutiny and a skeptical principal's. A dissertation award, peer-reviewed work on inclusive leadership: those say "this person is taken seriously by serious people," which is exactly the cover she needs to defend the spend.
- **Evidence the consultant understands neurodivergent learners specifically** — autism, PDA, complex profiles. Most AI tools and most AI consultants assume the median student. A consultant who can say what AI gets *wrong* for her highest-need kids has demonstrated they are not selling the same slide deck to everyone.
- **Right-sized rate signal.** A consultant who clearly works in her bands ($3-8k workshops, $15-75k projects) without making her do arithmetic. Too cheap reads as inexperienced; refusal to indicate range reads as a long, awkward call.
- **A warm path.** A referral line, a shared conference, an ESC connection — anything that means someone she trusts has already de-risked this person.

## Pet Peeves

- **Generic-AI-consultant camouflage** — the profile could belong to any of the forty "AI in education" consultants who emailed her this month; nothing on it is SpEd-specific. — *blocker.*
- **Transformation-verb soup** — *empower, reimagine, unlock, future-ready, transform* stacked where a concrete deliverable should be. Tells her the consultant has nothing shippable to name. — *strong.*
- **Compliance vocabulary used wrong** — "IEP" and "504" treated as interchangeable, "least restrictive environment" used as a vibe, "IDEA" cited with no Part. Instant costume detection; she stops trusting everything else on the page. — *blocker.*
- **The line title undercutting the claim** — a profile that argues senior-consultant expertise while the most recent role still reads "classroom teacher." She isn't a snob, but a board *is*, and she has to defend this hire to one. — *strong.*
- **AI-hype with no risk literacy** — all upside, no mention of student-data privacy, FERPA, bias against disabled students, or IDEA exposure. To her this person is a liability she'd be importing into her own department. — *blocker.*
- **No deliverable, no scope, no rate** — a one-pager that is all philosophy and leaves her to reverse-engineer what she'd actually be buying. — *strong.*
- **"Thought leader" / award-stacking with no engagement evidence** — speaking credits and credentials with nothing showing the consultant has run a project *for a district* and finished it. — *soft.*
- **Over-polished, identity-thin** — a slick brand with no point of view, no opinion about what AI gets wrong for SpEd kids. Polish without conviction reads as a vendor, not an expert. — *soft.*

## The Skim

**First 8 seconds on a one-pager or LinkedIn profile.** Renata's eye lands on the headline / top line, then the most recent role title, then scans for three words: *special education*, *compliance*, *AI*. If all three are present and near each other, the profile survives to a real read. If she has to hunt for them, it's already losing.

**Scan order after the 8 seconds:**
1. Headline / positioning line — does this name a niche that is *my* problem?
2. Most recent role title — does the seniority claim match the org-chart line, or is there a gap I'll have to explain to my board?
3. Deliverables / "what I do" — concrete nouns, or transformation verbs?
4. Proof — audit experience, state-agency work, named districts, a real engagement that finished.
5. Credentials — EdD, research, awards — read as *cover I can use upward*, not as the main course.
6. SpEd-specificity — autism, neurodivergence, IDEA, IEP used correctly.
7. Rate / scope signal and a referral path.

**Instant rejects:** generic "AI in education" with zero SpEd content; compliance terms used wrong; all-hype, no-risk framing; no nameable deliverable anywhere on the page; a profile so generalist it could be selling math PD next quarter.

**What earns a call:** a clear niche line that is her exact problem + at least one piece of real compliance/audit proof + at least one concrete deliverable named in plain English. Three things. That's the bar. A referral line on top of those three moves the consultant from "call" to "call first."

## Reading Catherine

This is the actionable payload. Renata, handed Catherine's current one-pager / LinkedIn, reacts like this:

**What she sees and likes immediately.**
- **The OSPI consulting line lands hard.** State-education-agency contract work is the single most reassuring thing on the page — it tells Renata this person has worked at the altitude where compliance is decided, not just where it's complained about. She'd circle it.
- **Compliance-audit experience is the closer.** Multi-site, multi-district IEP/IDEA audit work since 2019 is *exactly* the proof her instant-reject filter is looking for the absence of. For a SpEd-AI compliance audit or an inclusive-AI implementation review, this is the qualification.
- **The EdD plus Dissertation of the Year plus AERA record** function as board armor. Renata reads them not as "impressive" but as "defensible" — when her superintendent asks why this consultant, she can point at them.
- **The SpEd niche is real and rare.** 17 years, complex autism, PDA, early childhood, trauma-informed practice. Renata knows how few consultants can speak to her highest-need students. This is the differentiation that survives the generalist flood.

**What makes her hesitate — the risks.**
- **The generalist-flood camouflage risk is acute.** If Catherine's one-pager leads with "AI in education" framing, Renata's eye sorts her into the forty-emails-this-month pile *before* she ever reaches the OSPI line. The SpEd-compliance specificity has to be in the *headline*, not discovered on a second read.
- **The line-title gap is real and Renata will feel it.** Catherine's credentials say senior consultant; her most recent line title reads "classroom teacher." Renata personally won't hold this against her — but Renata has to defend the hire to a board, and a board *will* notice. If the profile doesn't get ahead of this, Renata has to do the explaining, and consultants who make her do explaining lose.
- **Positioning language that's tuned for a district hiring panel.** A headline like "Education and Administration Leadership Expert" tells Renata almost nothing about whether this person solves *her specific problem*. It's senior-leadership job-seeker language, not consultant-niche language. It reads as someone applying for a job, not someone selling a service.
- **AI as a claimed competency with thin public evidence.** If the profile asserts AI capability but there's no artifact — no published piece, no talk, no point of view about what AI gets wrong for SpEd kids — Renata partly discounts the AI claim and falls back to reading Catherine as "an excellent SpEd compliance auditor," which is good but leaves money and the AI engagement on the table.

**What Catherine's one-pager / profile MUST surface to win the engagement:**
1. **A headline that fuses the three words in the first line.** Something on the order of *"Special education compliance — and what AI changes about it. EdD; multi-district IEP/IDEA auditor."* SpEd + compliance + AI, visible in 8 seconds, no hunting.
2. **Lead with the OSPI and compliance-audit work, not the teaching role.** Reframe the profile around the *consulting practice* (active since 2019, OSPI contracts since 2023) as the headline identity. The classroom role becomes the practitioner-depth footnote, not the most-recent-line headline. This single reframe neutralizes the line-title risk.
3. **Name the deliverables in plain procurement nouns.** "You receive: a SpEd-AI compliance audit report, a prioritized findings list with IDEA-exposure flags, and a board-ready summary." Renata needs to see the noun she'd be buying.
4. **Show one piece of AI risk-literacy.** A single line, or a linked short artifact, on what AI gets wrong for neurodivergent students — student-data privacy, bias against disabled learners, IDEA exposure. This converts "claimed AI competency" into "demonstrated AI judgment" and clears the no-risk-literacy blocker.
5. **State the rate band and engagement shapes.** Workshops / project / retainer, in her bands, so the first call is scoping, not an awkward negotiation.
6. **Surface any warm path.** Shared conferences, ESC or state-network overlap, a referral line — anything that lets Renata tell her superintendent "she came recommended."

Bottom line: Catherine already has every piece Renata's shortlist filter is built to find. The risk is purely *order and framing* — the proof is buried under job-seeker positioning. Put the consulting practice, the compliance-audit proof, and the SpEd-AI-compliance niche in the top inch of the page, and Renata moves her from skim to call without hesitation.
