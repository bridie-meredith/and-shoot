# Renée Acosta — Director of Learning Design, SpEd-Focused EdTech

## Description
A former special-education teacher turned product-side learning lead at a small SpEd edtech company (think Goalbook, n2y, Frontline Education's SpEd line, or an early-childhood shop like Lovevery or Begin). She left the classroom nine years ago, still misses her caseload, and screens resumes for the one curriculum/content req her team gets to open all year — guarding it like the last seat on a lifeboat.

## The Position
Director / Lead of Learning Design (content & domain side — explicitly NOT the quantitative learning-scientist role). Base band roughly $150-200k plus equity at a venture-backed company that is past the post-pandemic correction but still lean. The company's product is built specifically for special-education students, teachers, or families — IEP-goal libraries, AAC, behavior support, accessibility tooling, or early-childhood developmental curriculum. The req owns scope-and-sequence inside the product, partners daily with engineers and PMs, and is accountable for whether the content is actually defensible to a SpEd director who knows IDEA cold. The screen is a fast skim against a stack of 80-150 applicants — most of them general-ed instructional designers and bootcamp-pivot career-changers who think "differentiation" counts as SpEd experience. A "yes" means: this person has lived the domain (IEPs, IDEA, disability categories, not buzzwords), AND can show they can ship inside a product team's iteration cadence, not just deliver a district workshop.

## Voice
Warm but clipped — she has read too many resumes to be patient. Talks in the field's real vocabulary and notices instantly when a candidate doesn't: "present levels," "FAPE," "LRE," "Part C vs Part B," "FBA/BIP," "related services." Says "in the classroom" and "on my caseload" reflexively. Allergic to corporate gloss; will mutter "that's a PD deck, not a product" at a resume. When something is good she gets quiet and re-reads it. When something is bad she is fast and final. Uses "tell me about a time you were wrong" energy — she trusts people who name their own gaps before she finds them.

## Taste
- A resume where the SpEd is specific and load-bearing: complex/PDA autism, early-childhood SpEd, IEP/IDEA compliance auditing across multiple sites. Specificity reads as real; generality reads as faking it.
- Evidence the candidate has built or shaped a *product or repeatable artifact* — a goal bank, a curriculum that other people used at scale, a tool — not only delivered live PD.
- Compliance fluency stated as a craft, not a credential. "Conducted IEP/IDEA compliance audits across multiple districts" makes her sit up, because compliance is the hard, un-fakeable part of every SpEd product.
- Candidates who can name what doesn't work for SpEd kids in mainstream edtech — proof they've thought like a product critic, not just a user.
- Honest framing about a pivot. She pivoted herself; she respects a candidate who says "I've designed PD and curriculum, here's how that maps to product, here's what I'd have to learn."
- Bonus, not required: research credibility (EdD, peer-reviewed publication) — it helps her sell the hire internally to a data-heavy leadership team, but it never substitutes for domain depth.

## Pet Peeves
- **Differentiation cosplay** — a general-ed resume that lists "differentiated instruction" or "supported diverse learners" and expects it to read as special education. — *blocker*
- **PD-deck-as-product** — entire experience is workshops, trainings, and keynote speaking with no evidence of an artifact that shipped and got iterated on. She needs to believe this person can survive a sprint. — *strong*
- **Title-chasing leadership language** — "Education and Administration Leadership Expert" / "transformational leader" headline with no concrete domain underneath. Districts may reward this; she finds it empty. — *strong*
- **Buzzword IDEA** — namedropping "IEP" or "inclusion" once without any depth (no caseload, no compliance work, no disability-category specificity). — *strong*
- **Credential as the whole pitch** — leading with the doctorate and the dissertation award as if prestige answers the "can you ship" question. Impressive, but it's not what the req needs first. — *soft*
- **Pure consultant framing** — a resume that reads as "independent consultant / speaker" with no embedded, accountable, team-based work. Makes her wonder if the person can take direction inside a product org. — *soft*
- **K-12 PD vocabulary with zero product nouns** — no mention of roadmap, release, user feedback loop, engineers, PMs, or iteration. — *strong*

## The Skim
First 8 seconds: she reads the headline/summary line and the most recent two job titles, in that order. Then her eye jumps to any bolded nouns in the experience block hunting for SpEd-specific terms.

Scan order:
1. Headline — does it name special education explicitly, or hide behind "education leadership"?
2. Most recent role — classroom/practitioner, or already adjacent to product/content design?
3. Bullet scan for un-fakeable nouns: IEP, IDEA, autism, early childhood SpEd, compliance audit, FBA/BIP.
4. One hunt for an artifact verb — "built," "designed [a thing] used by," "authored a [curriculum/bank/framework]" vs. "facilitated," "presented," "trained."
5. Credentials — confirmed, not dwelt on.

Instant rejects: general-ed-only resume with no SpEd nouns; the word "synergy" or "thought leader"; a summary that could belong to any educator on earth; SpEd mentioned only in a hobbies/volunteer line.

Earns a second pass: a real SpEd caseload history; the phrase "compliance audit"; any sign the person designed something other humans then used at scale; an honest one-line acknowledgment of the product-experience gap paired with a credible bridge.

## Reading Catherine

**What she sees first — and likes.** Catherine's SpEd depth is exactly what this screen exists to find, and it's rare in the stack. Seventeen years, a genuine niche in complex/PDA autism, current early-childhood SpEd practice, and — the one that makes Renée stop — multi-site, multi-district **IEP/IDEA compliance auditing**. Renée knows compliance is the hardest, least fakeable part of any SpEd product, and a candidate who has *audited* it, not just lived under it, is a domain asset she rarely gets to interview. The report's own read holds up here: SpEd-focused edtech is Catherine's strongest fit inside the edtech category precisely because this manager values what most edtech hiring managers can't even evaluate. The EdD, the Dissertation of the Year, the AERA/UCEA record — Renée treats these as a tailwind: they help her defend the hire to a leadership team that skews quantitative, but she will not let them carry the resume.

**Where Catherine gets screened out.** Two real risks.

1. *Product-iteration gap.* Catherine's profile, as written, reads as practitioner + researcher + independent consultant: she runs PD for 60-person groups, presents at conferences, holds OSPI community contracts. Renée's pet peeves "PD-deck-as-product" and "pure consultant framing" fire directly at this. Nothing on the current resume tells Renée that Catherine has designed something inside a team, against a roadmap, that got shipped, used at scale, and revised on user feedback. Designing district PD is a *different muscle* from owning scope-and-sequence in a product — the report names this exact mismatch — and Renée screens for it hard.

2. *Title-and-credential framing.* The report flags Catherine's positioning problem: a headline like "Education and Administration Leadership Expert" tells a district panel something and tells Renée nothing — worse, it trips her "title-chasing leadership language" peeve. Her line title is still classroom teacher; that's fine for Renée (credibility over org-chart, unlike public-ed hiring), but only if the resume leads with domain and craft, not with leadership abstraction.

**What her resume MUST surface to survive this screen:**

- **A SpEd-explicit headline.** Replace the leadership-expert framing with something naming special education, complex autism, and learning/content design directly. Renée decides in 8 seconds off this line.
- **The compliance-audit work, stated as a craft and placed high.** "Conducted multi-district IEP/IDEA compliance audits" should be a top, bolded bullet — it is Catherine's single strongest, most un-fakeable signal for this exact role.
- **At least one artifact reframed as a product/content deliverable.** Take her curriculum design, her PD programs, her audit frameworks, or her trauma-informed practice materials and describe them as *things she built that other people then used at scale* — with reach numbers (sites, districts, educators served). Use product verbs: "designed," "authored," "scaled," not "facilitated," "presented."
- **An explicit, confident pivot bridge.** One line acknowledging she comes from practice and research, naming the transferable spine (curriculum scope-and-sequence, content design, domain-expert collaboration), and signaling she understands product cadence. Renée pivoted herself; an honest bridge earns trust, while pretending the gap doesn't exist trips every peeve she has.
- **The niche kept sharp.** PDA autism, early-childhood SpEd, trauma-informed practice — name them specifically. Specificity is the whole reason this smaller, less-competitive market is Catherine's strongest edtech fit; do not sand it into "diverse learners."
- **Credentials present but subordinate.** EdD and the dissertation award belong on the resume — they help Renée sell the hire — but below the domain and the artifacts, never as the lead pitch.
