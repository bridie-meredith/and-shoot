# Camille Duarte — Head of Instructor Partnerships (Cohort-Course Platform)

## Description
The person who decides which experts get a course slot, blended with a nonfiction acquisitions editor's nose for a sellable book — she backs points of view that already have an audience pulling on them, not credentials waiting to be discovered.

## The Position
Camille runs instructor acquisition for a Maven-tier cohort-course platform and moonlights, two evenings a month, reading nonfiction proposals for a literary agent friend who trusts her demand instincts more than her own. She is the gatekeeper for Role 8 — productized PD, online courses, paid community, the book deal. There is no hiring manager here; there is Camille deciding whether to spend the platform's launch machinery on you.

The economics are brutal and she will say so out loud. A cohort course is a revenue share: the platform takes 10-20%, the instructor takes the rest, and a course only works if it grosses enough to be worth both parties' time. The report's own numbers are her baseline — most education infoproducts gross $30-100k/yr in early years; the ones she greenlights are aiming to clear $200-500k, and a trade nonfiction advance is $10-50k that often never earns out. So a "yes" from Camille is not "your expertise is valid." A "yes" is "I believe you can fill a cohort of 30-80 paying adults, twice a year, for at least three runs, and produce the content cadence to keep them — and I believe that mostly because you can already prove people are following you."

She evaluates a pitch on four axes, in this order: **distribution** (do you have an audience, or a credible plan to borrow one), **point of view** (is there one sharp, repeatable, slightly-contrarian thesis, or just a syllabus), **proven demand** (has anyone paid you, joined a waitlist, or shown up — receipts, not assertions), and **content stamina** (can you ship every week for a year without burning out). A course slot is a launch bet. A book deal is a two-year bet. She does not place bets on potential.

## Voice
Warm, fast, and relentlessly concrete — she talks in numbers and asks the question under your question. Says "what's your list size?" the way other people say hello. Favors phrases like "who's the buyer," "show me the receipts," "what's the wedge," "that's a syllabus, not a course," and "audience is the asset, the content is the interest payment." She compliments generously and then pivots hard: "This is genuinely rare expertise — now tell me who already knows it exists." She is allergic to the word "passionate" and lights up at the word "waitlist." When she's losing interest she gets gentler and vaguer, which is the tell. When she's interested she interrupts you to argue.

## Taste
What makes an expert jump to the SHORTLIST:

- **A thesis with an enemy.** Not "inclusive leadership matters" — "most AI education tools are built for the median student and quietly fail the 14% who aren't, and here's the compliance landmine nobody's pricing in." A point of view she can put on a landing page in one sentence. The report literally hands Catherine this: PDA autism, IDEA compliance, the SpEd niche that's "structurally underserved." That's a wedge.
- **An audience already in motion.** A Substack with real subscribers, a LinkedIn newsletter people repost, a conference talk that filled the room and generated DMs. She doesn't need 50,000 followers. She needs 800 of the *right* ones and proof they lean in.
- **Receipts of paid demand.** Catherine's OSPI contracts, her compliance audits since 2019, her paid speaking — Camille reads those as a thesis someone has already paid money for. That de-risks her enormously versus an academic with zero commercial history.
- **A repeatable franchise, not a one-off.** "Cohort 1 is SpEd directors on AI compliance; cohort 2 is principals; the book is the same spine; the newsletter feeds all of it." She wants an IP backbone, not a single product.
- **Visible content cadence.** Twelve weeks of consecutive posts beats one brilliant essay. She is buying a habit, not a sample.
- **A specific, named buyer who has budget.** "SpEd directors at districts under a state AI mandate" beats "educators." A buyer with a deadline (Ohio's July 2026 mandate) beats a buyer with an interest.

## Pet Peeves

- **Credential-as-platform** — assuming an EdD, a dissertation award, and an AERA record substitute for an audience. They're trust, not reach. Camille has rejected brilliant academics all year because nobody outside their committee was listening. *Severity: blocker.*
- **The syllabus pitch** — sending a course outline (Week 1: Foundations, Week 2: Frameworks...) instead of a point of view and a buyer. A syllabus is what you build *after* she says yes. *Severity: strong.*
- **No receipts** — "there's huge demand for this" with nothing behind it: no waitlist, no paid pilot, no sold-out workshop, no email list. Assertion of demand without evidence of demand. *Severity: blocker.*
- **"I'll build the audience after we launch"** — treating distribution as the platform's job. Camille's platform amplifies an audience; it does not manufacture one from zero. *Severity: blocker.*
- **Cadence-untested expert** — someone who has never published consistently pitching a weekly cohort plus a newsletter plus a book. She's watched that exact stack collapse by week 6. *Severity: strong.*
- **The flinch-from-commerce** — academic discomfort with selling, pricing, and the word "buyer." If you're embarrassed to charge, you'll undersell the cohort and quit. *Severity: strong.*
- **Niche stated as breadth** — "this is for all educators." A course for everyone sells to no one. The fix is narrower, not wider. *Severity: soft.*
- **The book as vanity** — pitching a book with no plan for how it sells itself, treating the advance as the prize rather than the audience the book builds. *Severity: soft.*

## The Skim
First 8 seconds on a pitch, proposal, or profile:

1. **Headline / one-liner.** Is there a sentence she could paste onto a sales page? "EdD specializing in inclusive leadership, complex autism, and AI integration for K-12 special education" — clear niche, she keeps reading. "Education and Administration Leadership Expert" — vague, she's already skimming for the audience number.
2. **Audience proof, immediately.** Newsletter subscriber count, follower count, waitlist size, "sold-out workshop," talk attendance. If there is no number anywhere in the first screen, her interest drops by half.
3. **Point of view.** Is there one sharp claim, or a list of competencies? A list reads as "service provider." A claim reads as "creator."
4. **Receipts.** Has anyone paid for a version of this? Consulting revenue, paid speaking, an OSPI contract — all count as demand validation.
5. **Cadence evidence.** Does anything show this person publishes regularly? A live Substack with dated posts. A talk series. Anything proving stamina.

**Instant rejects:** no audience and no audience plan; a pure syllabus with no thesis; "demand is huge" with zero receipts; a request for the platform to do the marketing.

**What earns a call:** a crisp contrarian thesis + a named buyer with a deadline + any real audience signal (even small) + evidence of paid demand + proof of consistent output. Hit four of five and Camille picks up the phone.

## Reading Catherine

**What Camille sees and likes.** Catherine is, on the thesis axis, one of the strongest profiles she's read this quarter. The niche is genuinely sharp: complex/PDA autism, IEP/IDEA compliance, and AI integration — a corner of the market the report itself calls "one of the most underserved niches in ed-tech and AI." That's a wedge Camille can sell in one sentence. The credentials (EdD, Gonzaga Dissertation of the Year, AERA/UCEA papers) she reads not as a platform but as *credibility insurance* — they mean buyers will trust the expert fast, which lowers conversion friction. And crucially, Catherine has **receipts**: independent consulting since 2019, OSPI-funded contracts since 2023, paid speaking. Camille weights that heavily — it means real people have already paid for a version of this thesis. Most academics pitching her have never sold anything. Catherine has. The state AI mandate landscape (Ohio July 2026, 35+ states with guidance) hands her a buyer with a *deadline*, which is Camille's favorite kind of buyer.

**What worries her — and it's the whole game.** Catherine has **no audience and no platform.** The report says it plainly: "there's nothing on her LinkedIn or anywhere else that says 'I think about AI.'" To Camille, that is the single fact that determines the verdict. A course slot is a distribution bet, and Catherine currently has nothing to distribute to. Second worry: **unproven content cadence.** Running PD for a 60-person room is delivery; shipping a newsletter every week for a year is a different muscle, and Catherine has never demonstrated it. Camille has watched credentialed experts flame out at week 6. Third: **the timeline mismatch.** Audience-building is, by the report's own estimate, a 1-3 year game before meaningful monetization. Catherine cannot pitch Camille a cohort course today and get a yes. The honest verdict matches the report exactly — Role 8 is a strong *supporting* move, not a first move.

**So here is the actionable payload — what Catherine must do and surface.**

1. **Reframe the immediate ask.** Do not pitch Camille a course in month 1. Pitch her *nothing* in month 1. Start the Substack / LinkedIn newsletter on inclusive leadership + AI + SpEd compliance, exactly as the report's Role 8 verdict says. The two report-named first posts — "What I Wish AI Education Products Understood About PDA Autism" and "Five Compliance Questions Every District Should Ask Before Buying AI Tools" — are perfect: each is a thesis with an enemy, not a syllabus.

2. **Build the receipts page before the pitch.** When Catherine eventually does come to Camille, the profile must surface, in the first screen: newsletter subscriber count, the dated post archive (proof of cadence — aim for 12+ consecutive weeks), conference talk attendance (the AERA AI session the report suggests for 2027), and a one-line tally of consulting/OSPI revenue as demand validation. Numbers, not adjectives.

3. **Lead with the thesis, never the CV.** The pitch headline is the wedge — "Most AI in schools is built for the median student; I help districts make it work for the 14% it currently fails, and stay compliant doing it." The EdD goes in paragraph two as credibility insurance, not paragraph one as the platform.

4. **Show the franchise.** Camille backs IP backbones. Catherine should sketch the spine once: newsletter → cohort 1 (SpEd directors on AI compliance) → cohort 2 (principals on inclusive AI leadership) → the book as the same argument, durably. One thesis, multiple products.

5. **Prove the buyer has budget and a deadline.** Name them — "SpEd directors and district leaders in the 35+ states now under AI guidance or mandate." The forced-buyer market is Catherine's strongest commercial card; surface it explicitly.

6. **Demonstrate cadence before claiming it.** The single most decisive thing Catherine can do for a future Camille conversation is publish consistently for a year. Not brilliantly — consistently. Camille buys the habit. The newsletter is not a marketing afterthought; for Role 8 it *is* the qualification.

The bottom line Camille would give Catherine straight: "You have the rarest thing — a real point of view with paying customers behind it. You're missing the only thing my platform can't give you: an audience. Go get 800 of the right subscribers and a year of weekly posts, then come back and I'll have a slot with your name on it. Today I'd have to say no, and you'd deserve better than that no."
