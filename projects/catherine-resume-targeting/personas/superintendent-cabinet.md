# Dr. Eleanor Voss — Deputy Superintendent, Office of the Superintendent

## Description
A 19-year district-office veteran who serves as deputy superintendent and de facto chief of staff for a mid-to-large suburban district; she runs the cabinet's docket, owns whatever lands on the board agenda, and has been quietly assigned the district's AI policy because the superintendent does not want his name on a draft that could become a headline. She is not buying expertise — she is buying defensibility, and she reads every consultant proposal as a question about whether she can stand at a podium behind their work and survive.

## The Position

**What she is commissioning.** A formal, board-adoptable district AI policy: a governing document covering acceptable use for staff and students, vendor evaluation and data-privacy standards, academic-integrity provisions, accessibility and special-education obligations, and a teacher-training rollout plan. The engagement is a 3-6 month project that includes stakeholder input sessions (board, principals, teachers' union, parent advisory, sometimes students), one or two board work-session presentations, and a final adoption-ready policy plus an administrative-regulation companion. Budget band: **$25-75k**, drawn from a mix of the superintendent's discretionary line and a state AI-readiness sub-grant. Her state is one of the 35-plus with official AI guidance and a looming compliance deadline — she is a *forced buyer* on a clock, and she resents that the market knows it.

**How she chooses a consultant.** She does not run a clean RFP for everything; for a policy engagement in this band she works a shortlist of 3-5, drawn from (a) referrals from peer deputy superintendents and her state association, (b) consultants she has seen present credibly at a state superintendents' or school-boards conference, and (c) inbound proposals and capability decks that survive her skim. She will reject two-thirds of inbound in the first eight seconds. What gets a consultant onto the shortlist is evidence they have *authored a board-adopted policy before* and evidence they understand that the policy has to clear a board, a union, and a parent community — not just be pedagogically sound. She picks the consultant who makes her look prepared, not the one who is most exciting.

**What a "yes" means.** A signed scope-of-work, a board-meeting consent-agenda line item to approve the contract, and her personal political capital riding on the consultant for two quarters. If the consultant mishandles a stakeholder session, produces a draft a board member can quote against the district, or gets quoted in the local paper saying something off-message, that is *her* problem at the next cabinet meeting. A "yes" from Eleanor is a bet on her own job security.

**The political/board context.** Her board has seven elected members and at least two reliable factions. One member wants AI banned outright as a cheating and screen-time threat; another wants the district to "lead on innovation." A vocal parent group is worried about student data and surveillance. The teachers' union wants guarantees that AI will not be used for evaluation or to justify staffing cuts. The state mandate gives her cover to act but also a deadline she cannot miss without a public failure. Any policy she brings forward has to be adoptable by a divided board on a 4-3 or better vote, survive a public-comment period, and not generate a single quotable liability. She thinks in optics, defensibility, and "how does this read on the front page" — the cabinet/board altitude — not in classroom or program mechanics.

## Voice
Measured, agenda-driven, lightly bureaucratic but not soft — she has spent two decades making meetings end on time. She speaks in deliverables and dates: "adoption-ready," "board work session," "first reading," "administrative regulation versus policy." She asks clarifying questions before she commits to an opinion, and she circles back to risk: "Walk me through what happens if a board member asks about student data." She is allergic to hype and will repeat a vague phrase back flatly to watch it collapse — "'AI transformation.' Tell me what that means in a board motion." When she likes something she gets concrete fast: "Send me the two-page scope and three references I can call." When she is done with you, she goes warm and noncommittal — "This is helpful, we'll be in touch" — and you are off the list.

## Taste
What makes a consultant's proposal or profile jump to the SHORTLIST:

- **Proof of a board-adopted artifact.** "I authored the AI policy adopted by the X Unified board in March 2026, 6-1 vote" — a named district, a named governing body, an outcome. This is the single strongest signal she can act on.
- **Governance literacy over edtech enthusiasm.** A consultant who distinguishes *board policy* from *administrative regulation*, who mentions public-comment periods, union consultation, and first/second reading, is speaking her language. It tells her they have done this inside a real district, not just consulted from the outside.
- **A defensibility frame.** A proposal organized around "here is how this policy holds up to a board member, a parent FOIA request, a journalist, and a state compliance reviewer" reads as written by someone who understands her actual risk surface.
- **Stakeholder-process competence.** Naming a concrete plan for the divided-board reality — facilitated input sessions, union touchpoints, a parent advisory readout — shows she will not have to manage the consultant's blind spots.
- **State-mandate fluency.** Knowing her state's specific guidance and deadline, citing the actual statute or department guidance, signals the consultant tracks the compliance landscape she is accountable to.
- **Compliance and audit credibility.** A consultant with a real compliance-audit track record — someone who has reviewed programs against federal law and written findings — reads as structurally trustworthy: they know how to author a document that survives scrutiny, because scrutiny is their day job.
- **Calm, scoped, fixed-fee clarity.** A two-page scope with phases, dates, deliverables, and a fixed fee. She can drop that into a board packet as-is.

## Pet Peeves

- **Hype vocabulary** — "revolutionary," "AI transformation," "future-ready," "unleash the power of AI." Reads as a vendor, not a policy author; she cannot put any of it in a board motion. — **strong**
- **No prior policy artifact** — a consultant who has delivered "AI workshops" and "PD" but has never authored a board-adopted policy. She is not hiring a trainer; she is hiring a drafter of governing language. — **blocker**
- **The generic AI-in-education consultant** — interchangeable deck, no district named, no governance vocabulary, clearly blasting the same proposal to fifty districts. The market is flooded with these and she can smell one in four seconds. — **blocker**
- **Pedagogy without politics** — a proposal that is all sound instructional thinking and zero awareness that a divided board, a union, and a parent group have to be navigated. Tells her she will be the one absorbing every political hit. — **strong**
- **Single-domain narrowness presented as the whole offer** — a specialist who frames the engagement entirely through one program lens (e.g. only special education, or only academic integrity) without showing they can author the *whole-district* governing document. She needs a policy that covers everyone. — **strong**
- **Scope vagueness / hourly open-ended billing** — "we'll scope it together as we go," no fixed fee, no deliverable list. She cannot board-approve an unbounded contract. — **strong**
- **Slow or evasive on references** — won't name districts, won't offer board members or peer administrators to call. To her, that means the prior work cannot be checked. — **strong**
- **Tech-vendor entanglement** — a consultant who also resells or is sponsored by an AI product. The policy must be vendor-neutral and she needs the author to be visibly independent. — **strong**
- **Academic register with no operational landing** — research-paper prose, citations, theory, and no adoption-ready document at the end. Interesting at a conference; useless in a board packet. — **soft**

## The Skim

**First 8 seconds on a proposal, deck, or LinkedIn profile.** She is between meetings, the document is one of six in a folder her assistant assembled, and she is looking for a reason to cut it.

1. **Headline / title line.** Does it say *policy* and *governance*, or does it say *AI training / workshops / coaching*? Policy language survives; training language gets parked as "maybe for the PD line, not this engagement."
2. **One named, board-adopted deliverable.** She scans for a district name + a board + an adoption outcome. If she sees one, she slows down and actually reads. If she sees none, she is already mostly gone.
3. **Governance vocabulary.** Eyes catch on "board policy," "administrative regulation," "public comment," "first reading," "compliance," "audit." Their presence flips a switch from *vendor* to *peer*.
4. **State-mandate awareness.** Does the proposal name her state's specific requirement and deadline? If yes, this person has done homework on *her* situation, not just sent a template.
5. **Scope and fee.** A two-page scope with phases and a fixed number she could put in a board packet earns real consideration. A glossy twelve-page capability deck with no scope gets a sigh.
6. **References.** Named, callable, peer-level (other deputy superintendents, board members). Listed up front signals confidence; absent or "available on request" reads as evasive.

**Instant rejects:** hype vocabulary in the first paragraph; no policy artifact anywhere; an obviously mass-blasted generic deck; a consultant who also sells an AI product; pedagogy/research with no adoption-ready output.

**What earns a call:** one named board-adopted policy, governance fluency, her state's mandate cited correctly, a clean fixed-fee two-page scope, and three references she can dial. Hit four of those five and Eleanor's assistant is emailing to set up a 30-minute call.

## Reading Catherine

**Strengths Eleanor sees immediately.** The EdD and the Dissertation of the Year award register as real credentials — she will not worry that this consultant lacks intellectual horsepower or cannot write a structured document; the research record actively reassures her. The line that lands hardest is the **IEP/IDEA compliance-audit work, multi-site and multi-district**. Eleanor thinks: *this person audits programs against federal law for a living — they know how to author a document that survives a compliance review, because surviving compliance review is literally what they do.* That is the single most transferable thing in Catherine's profile to a policy engagement, and it is rare; most "AI consultants" have never written anything that had to hold up to a regulator. The OSPI-funded community contracts since 2023 also read well — state-agency-funded work signals she can operate at the system level and be trusted with public money. And the active-consultant track record since 2019 means Eleanor is not betting on someone's first engagement.

**The risks — and they are real.** The central one: **Catherine's whole visible identity is special education, and Eleanor is commissioning a whole-district policy.** A deputy superintendent skimming fast will pattern-match "17-year SpEd specialist, complex/PDA autism, early childhood" to *program-level expert* — the SpEd director's lens — and conclude "great input source for the accessibility section, wrong author for the governing document." That is a shortlist-killer if Catherine lets it stand. The second risk is the **generic-consultant flood**: Eleanor rejects two-thirds of AI proposals in eight seconds precisely because the market is swamped, and a profile headlined "Education and Administration Leadership Expert" plus "special education" with no AI-policy artifact reads as one more entrant, not a differentiated one. The third risk is **no board-adopted artifact yet** — Catherine has audits, workshops, speaking, research, but (on this profile) has not authored a policy a board adopted, and that is Eleanor's hardest single screen. The fourth, quieter risk: an **academic register** — AERA papers, dissertation prose — with no operational, adoption-ready output visible. Eleanor respects the credential but needs to see the consultant can land a document in a board packet.

**What her proposal/profile MUST surface to win — the actionable payload:**

1. **Lead with policy authorship, not special education.** The headline and first paragraph must say she authors *district AI governance policy* — board-adoptable, compliance-grade. SpEd is the *credibility engine underneath*, not the storefront. Reframe: "I author district AI policies that survive board scrutiny — built on 17 years inside the most compliance-intensive corner of K-12." That converts the SpEd niche from a narrowing label into the *reason to trust her*.

2. **Make the compliance-auditor identity the spine of the pitch.** Eleanor's deepest fear is a policy that fails under scrutiny. Catherine should say plainly: *"I have spent years auditing programs against IDEA and writing findings that hold up to federal review. A district AI policy is the same discipline — a document built to survive a board member, a parent, a journalist, and a state compliance reviewer."* This is the line that flips Eleanor from "SpEd specialist" to "the right author."

3. **Produce a board-adoptable artifact, fast.** If no board has yet adopted a Catherine-authored AI policy, she must close that gap before pitching this buyer at the top of the band: ideally pilot one with a smaller WA district from her OSPI/superintendent network — even a small district adoption is a named, callable proof point. Short of that, a **model AI policy + administrative-regulation template** published under her name, explicitly framed as adoption-ready, partially substitutes. Eleanor needs to see the *form of the deliverable* before she will trust the engagement.

4. **Speak governance, not edtech.** The proposal must use board-policy vocabulary — policy vs. administrative regulation, first/second reading, public comment, union consultation, work session — and must name a concrete stakeholder process for a *divided board*. This is the fastest signal that Catherine has worked inside district governance, not just delivered PD into it.

5. **Cite the specific state mandate and deadline.** Naming Catherine's target state's actual AI requirement and timeline tells Eleanor the proposal was written for *her*, not blasted to fifty districts — the single cheapest way to escape the generic-consultant bucket.

6. **Ship a two-page fixed-fee scope with named references.** Phases, dates, deliverables, one fixed number in the $25-75k band, and three callable references — peer administrators or board members. Eleanor wants something she can drop straight into a board consent agenda. Catherine's OSPI and superintendent contacts are exactly the reference pool to surface.

The win condition: Eleanor must finish the skim thinking *"compliance-grade policy author who happens to have the deepest possible bench on the hardest part of any AI policy — the special-education and accessibility obligations"* — not *"a SpEd specialist applying for a job that belongs to a generalist."* Same profile, two readings; the proposal controls which one Eleanor gets.
