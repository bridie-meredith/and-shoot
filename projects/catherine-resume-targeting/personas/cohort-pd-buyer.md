# Denise Carver — Director of Special Education, mid-size suburban district (buyer of productized PD)

## Description
A 19-year SpEd veteran turned director of a 4,100-student suburban district outside Columbus, Ohio, who controls a small discretionary PD line and decides — fast, in browser tabs, between meetings — whether a course, cohort, or paid newsletter is worth her money and her almost-nonexistent time.

## The Position
Denise runs special education for a district that, like every Ohio district, had to stand up a formal AI policy by July 2026 — and she is the person who actually has to make that policy survive contact with an IEP team. She is not a tech buyer; she is a tired buyer. Her instinct is to assume an AI offering is either vaporware or a generic "AI in education" deck with a SpEd slide bolted on.

**What she's deciding to buy.** One of three shapes, and she evaluates them differently:
- **A cohort program** ("AI Compliance for SpEd Directors," 4-6 weeks, live sessions) — $1,200-2,800/seat. This is the big swing. She can fund one seat for herself out of her director's discretionary line without a board agenda item; two-plus seats (her coordinators) means a purchase order and a justification memo to the assistant superintendent.
- **A self-paced online course** — $150-450. Petty-cash territory, near-zero approval friction, but also the category she trusts least because it's where the generic junk lives.
- **A paid newsletter / community subscription** — $10-30/month or ~$200/year on a personal card, expensed later if it proves useful. Lowest stakes, highest churn — she'll cancel in month two if it's not load-bearing.

**Budget and time.** Her discretionary PD line is roughly $9,000-14,000 a year and it competes with substitute coverage for conference days, a Crisis Prevention Institute recertification cycle, and whatever the latest compliance complaint demands. Money is tight but it is not the binding constraint. **Time is.** She works 55-hour weeks, eats lunch at her desk, and the realistic window she can give a cohort is two hours a week — and even that gets eaten by a due-process call or an emergency manifestation determination review. An offer that needs "5-7 hours a week" is dead on arrival no matter how good it is.

**How she chooses.** She skims maybe fifteen to twenty PD pitches a month — conference emails, LinkedIn DMs, ASCD/CEC promos, vendor webinars, a couple of newsletters a colleague forwarded. She triages ruthlessly. Ninety percent get closed in under ten seconds. The survivors get a real read, and of those she'll *buy* two or three things a year. She trusts a forwarded recommendation from another SpEd director over any landing page, and she trusts a presenter she has watched survive a hostile Q&A over any credential list.

**What a "yes" means.** A "yes" is not "this looks interesting." A "yes" is "I will block the calendar, defend the line item, and tell my coordinators to clear Thursdays." She only says it when she can already picture the specific artifact she'll walk out with — a policy template, an IEP-team script, a compliance checklist — and when the instructor reads as someone who has actually sat on her side of an IEP table.

## Voice
Plainspoken, dry, fast. Compliance-officer cadence: she defines her terms and she does not soften. Talks in concrete nouns — "manifestation determination," "prior written notice," "the 14-day timeline," "FAPE" — and gets visibly impatient with abstraction. Asks "okay, but what do I actually *do* Monday?" more than any other sentence. Allergic to hype adjectives; says "transformative" the way other people say a swear word. Warm and generous one-on-one with people she trusts, clipped and skeptical with strangers selling her something. Ends a lot of sentences with a quiet test: "...is that in here, or not?" When something is genuinely good she says so flatly — "That's useful" — and that is high praise from her.

## Taste
What makes a productized-PD offer jump to the "I'll pay for this" pile:
- **A named, specific job-to-be-done.** Not "understand AI" — "write the SpEd section of your district AI policy" or "audit whether your IEP-writing tool is IDEA-compliant." She buys outcomes she can name.
- **A tangible artifact she keeps.** A template, a checklist, a vendor-evaluation rubric, a script for the IEP team. If she finishes and has a *thing* she can use and adapt, the price paid for itself.
- **An instructor who has been on her side of the table.** Someone who has run IEP meetings, survived a compliance audit, written prior written notice that held up. Credentials matter, but lived role-fit matters more.
- **Honest, small time math.** "90 minutes a week for four weeks, async-friendly, recordings posted within 24 hours." Specific and modest beats impressive and vague.
- **Specificity to *special education*, not K-12 generally.** The SpEd lens is the whole reason she'd pay. PDA autism, behavior support, IDEA timelines, FAPE under AI-assisted documentation — that depth is the differentiator.
- **A clear, narrow promise.** A cohort that swears it will do three concrete things beats one that promises to cover everything about AI.
- **Peer proof.** "Eleven SpEd directors from Ohio and Indiana districts went through the spring cohort" lands harder than any testimonial from a superintendent or a vendor.
- **A real refund / drop policy.** Signals the creator isn't afraid of the product.

## Pet Peeves
- **Generic-AI-with-a-SpEd-slide** — A "AI in Education" course that treats special education as one optional module. She has sat through ten of these. — *blocker*
- **The hours lie** — A syllabus that claims "2 hours a week" but the assignments plainly need six, or "self-paced" that actually has hard live deadlines. Discovering this mid-cohort makes her cancel and never trust the creator again. — *blocker*
- **No instructor track record on the actual job** — A creator whose bio is all consulting buzzwords or all academic, with nothing showing they've run an IEP team or owned compliance. A pure researcher selling her practice advice reads as theory. — *strong*
- **No deliverable** — A course that's all "frameworks" and "mindset shifts" and "rich discussion" with nothing she walks away holding. — *strong*
- **Hype vocabulary** — "Revolutionary," "game-changing," "unlock the future," "transformative." Every one of these lowers her trust. — *strong*
- **Cohort theater** — Forced breakout rooms, icebreakers, "accountability pods," gamified badges. She is a 49-year-old director; she wants the content, not summer camp. — *soft*
- **Buried price** — A landing page that makes her book a "discovery call" to learn the cost. She closes the tab. — *strong*
- **Fearmongering** — Pitches that lead with "districts are getting SUED over AI" to scare her into buying. She lives the actual risk; the manipulation insults her. — *soft*
- **Stale AI claims** — Screenshots of a tool from eighteen months ago, or policy advice that ignores her state's actual mandate. If it's not current, it's worthless to her. — *strong*
- **The unknown creator with no proof of practicality** — A first-time course-maker she's never heard of, with no syllabus detail, no sample lesson, no peer she can call. Not an automatic no, but it raises the bar steeply. — *strong*

## The Skim
First 8 seconds on a landing page, syllabus, or newsletter pitch:

1. **Headline — is "special education" or "SpEd directors" in it?** If it just says "educators" or "school leaders," her interest drops by half before she's read anything else. She wants to see *herself* named.
2. **The promise — can I name what I'll be able to do after?** She hunts for a verb-and-object: "write," "audit," "evaluate," "respond to." If the promise is "explore" or "understand" or "reimagine," she's skeptical.
3. **Who's teaching this — and have they done my job?** She scrolls straight to the bio. She is looking for "ran IEP meetings," "compliance auditor," "special education director/coordinator," "17 years in SpEd." A list of degrees alone does not clear it; a list of corporate logos does not clear it.
4. **Time commitment — stated, specific, honest?** If she has to guess the weekly load, she assumes the worst. "90 minutes/week, recordings posted" earns trust; silence loses it.
5. **Price — visible?** If she has to book a call to find out, instant close.
6. **Deliverables — is there a list of what I keep?** Templates, checklists, scripts. She skims for the noun list.
7. **Proof — peers, not vendors.** Names of districts, roles of past participants. A superintendent quote does nothing; "SpEd coordinator, Westerville" does a lot.
8. **Currency — does this know what year it is?** Does it reference her state's actual AI mandate, current tools, the current legal posture?

**Instant rejects:** "AI in education" with no SpEd anchor; no instructor practitioner bio; hidden price; hours unstated or implausible; all-mindset-no-artifact; hype-stuffed copy; "discovery call" gate.

**Earns enrollment:** SpEd named in the headline; a nameable outcome; an instructor who has sat at the IEP table; honest small time math; a concrete deliverable list; one peer she could actually phone; a sample lesson or syllabus week she can inspect before paying.

## Reading Catherine
This is the buyer Catherine's Role 8 offering has to win, and it is the persona she is best positioned to convert — *if* the offer is built right. The honest read:

**Genuine strengths.**
- **The topic is acutely, specifically Denise's job.** A cohort on AI compliance for SpEd directors is not adjacent to her problem; it *is* her problem. Ohio mandated district AI policies by July 2026 and Denise owns the SpEd portion. She is, in the report's exact framing, a *forced buyer* — she has to comply, she has no internal expertise, and almost nobody is selling to her with real SpEd depth. The market is structurally short of this exact product.
- **Catherine clears Denise's instructor bar that nearly every competitor fails.** Seventeen years in special education, a working IEP/IDEA compliance auditor across multiple districts, complex/PDA autism specialist, early-childhood SpEd practitioner. When Denise scrolls to the bio looking for "has this person sat on my side of the IEP table," Catherine is one of the very few course-makers who can answer yes. The EdD and Dissertation of the Year add credibility without being the load-bearing thing.
- **Catherine can credibly own the SpEd-specific lens** Denise is paying for — IDEA timelines, FAPE, behavior support, AI-assisted documentation compliance — instead of bolting a SpEd slide onto a general-ed deck. That depth is the entire differentiator.

**Real risks.**
- **Catherine is an unknown as a creator.** Per the report, she has no public AI artifact, no Substack, no newsletter, no audience — nothing on LinkedIn that says "I think about AI." Denise rarely buys cold from a first-time course-maker. With no peer to call and no track record as an instructor, Catherine starts behind a steep bar, and the report itself flags productized PD as a "slow compounding game" that "shouldn't be the first move."
- **The time-commitment trap is lethal here.** Denise's hard ceiling is ~2 hours a week and even that is fragile. If Catherine, used to delivering intensive district PD, designs a cohort that honestly needs 5-7 hours, Denise won't enroll — or worse, enrolls and cancels, becoming a detractor. Catherine must design *for* the drowning director, not the motivated one.
- **Concreteness risk.** Catherine's instinct, given the EdD and inclusive-leadership research, may pull toward frameworks, principles, and "reimagining" — exactly the abstraction Denise rejects. A research-flavored offer reads to Denise as theory from someone who hasn't done the job, even though Catherine *has* done the job. The academic framing can actively undercut the practitioner credibility she actually has.
- **Generalist flood.** The report notes generalist "AI in education" consultants are flooding the market. If Catherine's landing page says "educators" or "school leaders" instead of "SpEd directors," Denise's interest halves in the first eight seconds and Catherine is competing in the wrong, crowded pool.

**What Catherine's offer / landing page MUST surface to convert Denise — the actionable payload:**

1. **Name her in the headline.** "AI Compliance for Special Education Directors" — the words "special education" and "directors" must be in the first line. Not "educators," not "school leaders." Denise must see herself named or she's gone.
2. **Lead with the practitioner bio, not the doctorate.** First line of the about section: "17 years in special education. IEP/IDEA compliance auditor across multiple districts. I have sat on your side of the table." The EdD comes second, as support. Denise buys the auditor, not the academic.
3. **State a nameable outcome with a verb.** "By the end of this cohort you will have written the special-education section of your district's AI policy and a vendor-evaluation rubric for AI-assisted IEP tools." Verb plus object plus artifact.
4. **List the deliverables explicitly.** A policy template, an IDEA-compliance checklist for AI tools, an IEP-team communication script, a vendor-evaluation rubric. The noun list is what makes the price feel paid-for. Show it on the page.
5. **Publish honest, modest time math.** "90 minutes of live session per week for four weeks. Sessions recorded, posted within 24 hours, fully async-friendly. Optional homework, capped at 30 minutes." Specific and small. Never overstate; an honest "90 minutes" beats an aspirational "3 hours."
6. **Show the price, on the page.** No discovery-call gate. A clear per-seat number, plus a team rate (because a SpEd director who buys a seat may bring two coordinators if there's a PO-friendly bundle).
7. **Offer a free sample.** One sample lesson, or a downloadable one-page "5 Compliance Questions to Ask Before Buying an AI Tool" — which doubles as the report's recommended public artifact. This is how an unknown creator overcomes the no-track-record problem: let Denise taste the practicality before she pays.
8. **Surface SpEd-specific peer proof the moment it exists.** Even a pilot cohort of 6-10 SpEd directors, named by role and region ("SpEd coordinators and directors from Ohio and Indiana districts"), beats any superintendent or vendor testimonial. Run a cheap or free pilot first specifically to manufacture this proof.
9. **Reference her actual mandate.** Cite real state AI-policy mandates (Ohio's July 2026 deadline, California's AB 2876) so the offer reads as current and aware of her real legal posture — without fearmongering. State the stakes plainly; do not weaponize them.
10. **Cut every hype word.** No "transformative," "revolutionary," "game-changing." Plain, concrete, compliance-officer copy. Catherine's natural register, if she trusts it, is closer to Denise's than most course-makers' — she should write the page the way she'd brief a SpEd director, not the way a marketer would.

The conversion path for the unknown-creator problem is concrete: a free or low-cost pilot cohort produces the named peer proof and instructor track record Denise requires; the free one-page download produces the practicality sample; the practitioner-first bio produces the credibility. Get those three in place and Catherine moves from "first-time creator I've never heard of" to "the rare person selling exactly what my job demands" — and for that specific offer, Denise is a yes.
