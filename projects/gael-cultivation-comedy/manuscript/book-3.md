# BOOK III — *the narrow escape*

## III.1 · The Commission

The counting-house smelled of tallow and old ledgers and the river, and the shipwright's agent had a smudge of pitch under his thumbnail he kept worrying at, which Gael watched the way she watched everything — as data. He was a heavy, careful man with a clerk's stoop and an artisan's hands, come up three flights of a fish-merchant's stair to meet a buyer he did not know, on behalf of a master who did not ask questions, to be paid in coin that came up so clean through so many hands that whose rain it was had stopped being a thing a sane man wondered.

He kept his eyes on the table, which Gael approved of. A grandmaster artificer ought to be incurious about the patron commissioning a transcendent vessel; the great forges of the old novels never asked why the cultivator wanted the cloud-skiff, only how much spirit-jade he could spare. This one only wanted gold, which was simpler, and which she had in unspeakable quantities behind three walls of paper. She had come to the meeting herself — not as herself, never as herself, but folded behind her maid like a second shadow, a small noble child brought along to a tedious errand, dressed plain, saying nothing a grown man would think to listen to.

"Tell your master," she said, "that the timber must be seasoned."

She was nine years old, sitting on a cushion that made her tall enough to see over the table, and she had said *tell your master* through Wylla, who said it again in the voice of a lady-in-waiting placing an order on behalf of a household that did not exist — itself a household on behalf of a Braavosi factor who had never been to Braavos, who was in fact a column of figures in a book only Gael could read. The agent heard a noble lady's maid. He did not hear the child on the cushion at all. Nobody ever did. That was the whole architecture of her life, and she had built it the way the manuals said to build a foundation — patiently, in courses, so that nothing above could ever find the bottom.

"Seasoned how long?" Wylla asked, which was Gael's question, passed down through her like water through a pipe.

The agent unrolled a corner of the drawings she'd had sent ahead and tapped them with the pitch-stained thumb, and his face did a complicated thing — half a builder's pleasure at a worthy problem, half a builder's despair at being handed one. "A hull of this size, this grain, fitted as the drawings ask — and they ask for things I've not built in twenty years, things I'm not sure anyone's built. A double hull. Water stowed for — for a great while. Sealed lockers a man could live out of. Whoever drew this," he said, and Gael felt a small warm flush of pride that the agent would never see, because she had drawn it, "drew it for a voyage with no port at the end of it." He spread his hands. "Three years to lay her and four to finish her well. Closer to five if your master wants her to swim through a storm and not just a harbor."

Wylla's hand went still on the table. Gael felt the stillness beside her and understood it as her maid doing the courtly thing, absorbing a blow with grace, and was proud of her for it. Wylla was absorbing a different blow. *Years,* she was thinking. *Years, and the wedding is months. She doesn't even —* and then she made herself stop thinking it, the way she'd taught herself to stop thinking a great many things, and folded her hands, and waited.

Gael was not troubled in the least.

Of course it took years. You did not forge a natal treasure — a vessel meant to carry an immortal across the great water, provisioned forever, sealed against the world — over a single firing. The manuals were explicit. A low cauldron could be hammered out in a season; it cracked at the first real heat. A true vessel needed the long cycle: the slow accumulation, the tempering and re-tempering, the patience that separated the disciple who lived from the disciple who exploded in a ditch trying to skip a stage. Five years in the furnace was *correct.* It was, if anything, reassuring. It meant the artificer understood his craft. A man who promised the boat by autumn was a man whose boat she should not trust her ascension to.

"Five years is acceptable," she had Wylla say.

The agent blinked. He had braced for an argument. Patrons always argued about time; time was the one thing gold couldn't buy, and rich men hated being told so. He had a whole speech ready about grain and curing and how a rushed hull drank its crew, and he never got to give it. This one's maid simply wrote *acceptable* in a tidy hand and pushed the bill of exchange across the table, and it cleared, and that was the end of the haggling that did not happen. He looked once at the small plain child at the lady's elbow — the way you'd glance at a stool, a thing in the room — and the child looked back with the flat patient attention of a person inspecting a kiln, and he felt, briefly and for no reason he could name, that he should go home and embrace his sons, and then the feeling passed and he rolled up the drawings and went down the three flights to the river to start finding seasoned oak.

⁂

*To the Conclave, from the Red Keep, the maester Lorren, in the matter of the princess Gael —*

*My lords, I write in the relief of a man reporting that his patient is, for once, behaving as a child of nine ought. She has taken an interest in HER TROUSSEAU. The royal betrothal having a date now fixed, she applies herself to the lists and ledgers of her own going-north with a diligence I would call touching in a less unsettling child. Her lady-mother, hearing of it, wept; she takes it for a daughter at last reconciled, at last softening, at last — her word — NORMAL. I would dearly love to share the lady's comfort. I confess I cannot account for why a bride's trousseau requires the drawings of a deep-water keel, which I glimpsed and which she folded away before I could look twice. I am likely mistaken in what I saw. I am very often, these last nine years, likely mistaken in what I saw.*

⁂

The betrothal letter sat on her reagent-map like a stone on a map of the sea, and she did not like it there.

It had come up from the King by way of a herald and a great deal of ceremony Gael had ignored, and it said, in the curling hand of a Northern maester, that the Lord of Winterfell's heir would be pleased to receive his bride at the turn of the year. *The turn of the year.* Eight months, perhaps nine, and the hull not yet laid.

Wylla had read it aloud twice and then sat with her hands in her lap. "They'll send a party," she said. "To collect you. Lords have to come and *collect* a bride, it's — there's a procession, there's a feast, there's —"

"I know how a wedding works," Gael said, who did not, but knew how an extraction worked, which was the same thing seen correctly. The evil sect was calling in its marriage-piece. The disposal-match her cold sect-master father had arranged to be rid of a useless daughter was now a closing door, and she meant to be on the far side of it before it shut. The trouble was only the furnace. The vessel could not be hurried; the door could not be slowed.

She uncrossed the two clocks in her mind and looked at them. Eight months of wedding. Five years of ship. The gap was not a gap; it was a cliff.

"There's the other thing," Wylla said, low, because she made herself say the hard things now even when she'd rather not. "The men. The suitor's party that came to look you over and never — that the King's man is still tearing the city apart over. He's not stopping, Gael. He's worse. He's got every gold cloak in the lower city turning over stones."

"Let him turn stones."

"He's looking for *you.*"

"He's looking for a kingpin," Gael corrected, gently, as one corrects a promising student. "There's a difference, and the difference is the whole of why we're safe."

"Then we make the furnace burn hotter," she said.

"You can't make the *sea* go faster, Gael, it doesn't —"

"You can make a builder go faster if his patron is generous and patient and never once asks why." Gael smoothed the reagent-map flat, the dice-coin account a tidy black river down its margin, fat past any safe size now and getting fatter. "We pay for a second crew. We pay for the timber already seasoned, in another man's yard, bought clean. We pay the way you pay a forge to never let the fire go out — more fuel, more hands, no questions, and a stack of gold so high the man stops thinking about anything but the gold." She tapped the column. "Years come down when you flood the furnace. They never come down to *months.* But they come down."

Wylla looked at the black river of coin and at the child who could make a five-year boat appear in two if she simply set fire to enough of it, and something moved behind her eyes that was no longer quite horror and not yet quite anything else. "And the King's man," she said quietly. "The grandfather. He's still — they're still looking. For the suitor's men. The ones that didn't come home."

Gael waved a hand. Somewhere out in the city, an inquisitor was turning over the disappearance of an advance party that had ridden in to assess her and ridden nowhere ever again. She had read him correctly, she felt, the first time she'd heard of his diligence: an orthodox sect-hunter, righteous and tireless, sweeping the lower city for the demonic Quiet Coin, the phantom dark-lord he could feel in everything and find in nothing. Let him sweep. He was hunting a ghost in a counting-book — a man chasing the smoke of a fire he was personally feeding gold to.

"He looks for a kingpin," she said. "He'll keep looking for a kingpin. There isn't one. There's a furnace, and us, and a door with a date on it." She rolled the reagent-map closed, the keel-drawings safe inside it. "Place the second order tomorrow. Pay the seasoning premium. Tell the artificer his patron admires his patience and asks only that he forge a little faster, for love of the work."

Wylla wrote it down. Outside, eight months began their count, and five years, and somewhere below the city a man whose name they did not yet know watched the river of clean coin go by and thought, the way greedy men always thought just before the realm proved how grimdark it was, that a river that big surely wouldn't miss a cup.

## III.2 · The Excursions

The trouble with overseeing the forging of a transcendent vessel was that the furnace would not come to you.

So Gael went out at night. She told herself it was inspection — the master ought to look upon the work — but the truth, which she would not have called truth, was that she liked it. She liked the city with its eyes shut.

She read the drawings in the shipyard by a hooded lantern while the carpenters slept, checking the line of the laid keel against her own folded copy, correcting a man's honest work with the confidence of a child who has never built anything and the strange luck of one who is, in fact, usually right. She stood in the timber-yards before dawn and laid a hand on the great seasoned ribs stacked like the bones of something enormous, and felt, she was sure, the qi of good wood — the slow patient mountain-qi a tree spends a hundred years gathering, exactly the substance a vessel of ascension required. She went to the harbor and sat on a bollard with her knees drawn up and watched the black water that would carry her out of the story, the masts of other men's ships ticking against the stars, and she was, in those hours, as close to happy as her cold heart came — which is to say she stopped, for a little while, doing arithmetic, and simply looked at the sea, and the sea looked back and promised her the one thing she had wanted since she opened her eyes in a cradle nine years and a lifetime ago, which was: out.

Getting there was the pleasure. She went up the wall of Maegor's Holdfast and the dry moat of spikes below it might as well have been a stream for stepping; she let the breath sit low, the weight come off the heels, and she went up the courses of stone as though the wall were a ladder laid on its back. The city opened under her. She crossed it the way the manuals described the light-body art at maturity — roof to roof, the lead to the slate, the slate to the thatch she avoided because thatch told tales — and below her the great grim sect of Westeros slept with its mouth open and did not know its truant immortal walked over its head.

And the watch began to talk about a ghost.

⁂

*— and Old Hobb swears it again, swears he saw it go over the Holdfast roof, light as a cat, light as nothing, and the captain told him to dry out and sleep it off, but Maisie's boy saw a shape on the sept roof too, and the fishwife by the Mud Gate, and now they're calling it the ghost of the Red Keep, and mothers tell it to the little ones — be good or the Keep-ghost takes you over the rooftops — and the captain's doubled the night patrols and they catch nothing, nothing, a shadow that's gone when you look, and meanwhile the thieving's worse than it's been in years, plate gone, purses gone, and SOMEONE has to answer for it —*

⁂

She heard the legend three different ways within a week, and each time it pleased her more than the last.

A serving-woman murmured it doing up Gael's hair: the ghost of the Red Keep, my lady, mind you don't catch a chill at the window. A page told it to another page in a stairwell, hushed and delighted. The septa heard it and pronounced it a judgment on a sinful city, and looked at Gael's meditative stillness, and added that *some* in the Keep were plainly protected from such evils by their piety, and felt very pleased with her ward.

Gael received it the way a master receives the first temple built to him by mortals who have only ever glimpsed his shadow: with quiet, almost embarrassed satisfaction.

This was the city-legend. Every cultivator who reached a certain stage earned one — the unseen expert, the hermit who hides in the marketplace, the shadow on the rooftop that the common folk weave into a story because they cannot hold the truth. *The great recluse conceals himself in the city.* Her light-body art had matured to the point that ordinary eyes caught the edge of it and minds too small for the truth made a ghost of her. It was, frankly, a milestone. She noted it in the bestiary under her own progress, a private and rather smug little line, and did not for one instant connect the ghost to the manhunt — because in her frame the manhunt was an orthodox sect hunting a phantom kingpin and the ghost was an awed populace honoring a passing immortal, two different things happening to two different people, neither of whom was a nine-year-old girl on a roof.

There was one man who saw her plainly and said so.

His name was Hobb, and he had been a still-room boy in the Keep years ago — had seen, once, a small princess bite a ruby off a brooch and swallow it, and had said so, and been beaten for lying, and learned the lesson the Keep teaches its truth-tellers, which is to stop telling. He was a watchman now, older, greyer, posted on the night wall, and on a cold clear evening he looked up from his brazier and saw a shape go over the roof of the sept light as a blown leaf and he knew it, he *knew* it the way you know an old voice in the dark — the princess, the biting-girl, walking on the air the way she'd always been able to do impossible things while grown men told each other they hadn't seen it.

"There," he said to the watchman beside him, pointing, his voice gone thin. "There — on the roof — that's no ghost, that's a *girl,* I've seen her, I know her face —"

The other man looked where he pointed and saw an empty roofline and a fool with too much wine in him, and told him so, kindly, because Hobb was old and good for the long nights. "Sleep it off, Hobb."

"I'm not — I've seen her *before,* she ate a stone, she —"

"Aye. The demon-girl who eats stones and walks on roofs. Sleep it off."

So Hobb stopped pointing, and stood at his brazier, and watched the shape go over the river and out of sight, and said nothing more, because he had been right twice in his life and ignored twice, and a man learns. He was the one creature in the city who saw the literal truth — a princess on a roof — and the city's whole machinery existed to make sure that truth never landed anywhere it could be believed, and so it didn't, and Gael went over his head into the dark not knowing she'd been seen, and not caring if she had, because to her a mortal glimpsing the unseen expert and being disbelieved was simply how the city-legend got made.

What she could not see at all, because she never looked, was the rest of the story the watch was telling itself.

The thieving *was* worse. Not hers — she stole nothing; she had everything. But a city whose watch is hunting a ghost is a city whose watch is not minding the doors, and the ordinary thieves of King's Landing, who were legion and unghostly, went through the distracted streets like water through a broken weir. Plate vanished from a merchant's hall. A strongbox went from the customs-house. A jeweler on the Street of Steel woke to an empty case and a back window standing open. And because a watch cannot arrest a ghost but must arrest *someone,* and because the men above them wanted answers and the men above *them* wanted the ghost-talk stopped before it reached the throne, the watch went where the powerless were, which is where the watch always goes.

They took the jeweler's two scullions down to the black cells to ask them about the open window. A boy of fourteen and a woman with a bad hip who'd done the man's washing nine years. They asked them about the ghost, which the scullions had never seen, and about the missing pieces, which the scullions had never touched, and the boy came back up after three days walking strangely and not meeting anyone's eyes, and the woman did not come back up at all. Her sister went to the gate and asked after her, once, and a man there took the sister's name down on a list, and the sister stopped going, because she had seen him write her name and understood that asking at that gate was only a way to be invited inside it.

Gael did not know their names. She would not have logged them if she had; they were not assets, not obstacles, not vendors, not anything that touched her work. They were the cost the reader carried out of the room while she stood on a roof above their street and felt the cool clean qi of the harbor air and thought how well, how very well, her cultivation was coming along.

Below all of it, two other things moved.

The rivals moved first. The Quiet Coin's people had grown thin and slow while the Coin's true mind walked rooftops and read keel-drawings, and the other crews of the lower city smelled it the way crews do. A dice-den that paid the Coin's tithe got leaned on by a man named Greenhand — a real underworld figure, broad and unhurried, who had decided over a winter that the Quiet Coin was a story crews told to keep the soft ones in line, and that a story could be pushed. A runner of the Coin's got knifed in an alley off the Hook. A table went dark.

Marra brought the word up the line.

She was one of the clever commoners who fronted the Coin's tables — a smallfolk woman of perhaps five-and-twenty, quick and decent, sharp at sums and softer of heart than the trade allowed, in over her depth and not yet drowning. She came to Wylla's rooms white-faced and twisting her shawl. "The Greenhand's taken two of the floors on the Street of Sisters," she said. "Walked in, sat down, said the Coin's gone to ground and won't be coming back, and any house that pays the Coin's tithe pays *him* now or burns. And — Wylla — they're saying it loud, they're saying the Coin's nobody, the Coin's a tale, and the more they say it the truer it gets, because where *is* he? Where's the Coin? The houses are scared. *I'm* scared. What do I tell them?"

Wylla took her hands and said the things she'd learned to say — that the Coin was not gone, that the Coin saw everything, that the Greenhand had made a mistake he'd learn from, that Marra should keep the books square and trust the dark. She believed about half of it. She brought the rest to Gael.

Gael answered two of the tests, fast and quiet and from a great height, the way she did everything now — a word here, a debt called in there, a man who had been bold on a Tuesday simply not at his table on a Thursday. The Greenhand she looked at and weighed and set down again. He was a complication, not a danger; killing him was effort, and effort was the one currency she was short of these months, with the furnace eating all her nights. *Spare him,* she decided — judged him, on the page, not worth the spending — and turned back to her drawings, and did not feel the small cold click of a thread left dangling, because she never felt those. She could not answer all the tests, and did not much care to. The empire had only ever been scaffolding around the ship. Let it fray at the edges. She would not be here to mind it.

And the factor moved.

His name was Lothar Quint, and he ran the pits — the dice-dens and cock-floors where the dirtiest of the coin came in — and he was, by his own lights, a careful man and a clever one. He had watched the river of money that flowed up through his pits and out to wherever the Quiet Coin lived, and he had begun, lately, to skim a cup from the river, and then a dipper, and then to lie awake at night doing a different arithmetic. Whoever the Quiet Coin was, he reasoned, the man was distracted. The man was bleeding money into some great mad project nobody could name. A distracted king is a king with a thin crown. And Lothar Quint had a family to feed, and a notion that he was the smartest man in the lower city, and no idea at all that the throne he was eyeing was a cushion, on which sat a child, who logged greedy men under *Obstacle (pending)* and waited.

## III.3 · The Greedy Factor

Quint asked to meet the Coin.

He asked it of Marra, who brought it to the other operators, who brought it shaking to Wylla, because in the lower city you did not *ask to meet the Coin;* the Coin was the dark behind the door, the reason your tithe was safe and your rivals afraid, and the surest sign a man had lost his sense was that he wanted the dark to have a face.

"He says he won't pass another month's take up the line," Wylla reported, "until he's shaken the hand that takes it. He says — " she steadied her voice, " — he says a man ought to know who he works for. He's telling the pits the Coin might be nobody. A *story.* He's saying it where it'll spread."

Gael was at her reagent-map, weighting the corners flat. She did not look up. "He leaned on you."

"He came to my rooms. He was — courteous." Wylla's hands were not quite still. "He made it courteous, which was worse. He said it would be a *shame,* he said, if a respectable maid found herself tangled in whatever the Coin really was, when the King's men are turning the city over looking. He said he could keep my name clear. For a consideration."

So the factor had found the maid, and meant to use her as a stair up to the dark, and had said *the King's men are turning the city over* in the same breath — which meant Lothar Quint, careful clever Quint, had drawn a line between the Coin's money and the inquisitor's manhunt and was prepared to walk along it toward Wylla if she did not buy him off. He was a thread that led from the empire to her one true asset, and from there, if pulled, to her.

Gael considered him with the flat attention she gave a reagent that had begun to spoil.

"Is he a good man?" she asked, which startled Wylla, because Gael never asked.

"He —" Wylla thought about it. "He's not cruel. He's frightened, mostly. He's a man who got close to something too big for him and can't stop reaching. He has a wife. Little ones. He talks about them."

"Then he's the worst kind," Gael said, and Wylla did not ask what she meant, because she had learned what Gael meant by such things, and it made her cold.

Here, Gael ran the self-audit she always ran, because she was a careful soul and would not act without clearing her conscience first. *Am I the villain of this story?* she asked herself, as she had asked at every removal since the cradle. And she answered the way she always did, on a technicality, in cosmic robes. A villain *struck* people down. A villain raised a hand. She would raise no hand. In the old novels there was a figure for exactly this: the wayward disciple, the one who turns on the sect that fed him, who lets greed swell in him past all sense until it draws down his own *tribulation* — the heavens themselves descending to balance the books of a soul that overreached. You did not even need to punish such a man. The cosmos did it for you. You merely had to be standing in the right place, with clean hands and a clear eye, when the lightning fell. Cause and effect. *Karma.* Quint had courted his own ruin with both hands; she would be nothing but the instrument through which a just world collected its due, which was not the same as murder, she was quite certain, having checked.

She was nine. She had built a doctrine of innocence so total it could absorb a man's whole household, and she ran it cleanly, and it cleared her, and she felt, closing the audit, the small clean peace of a person who has done the right thing.

This is what she told herself, and she was nine, and it cost her nothing now, and that was the change in her this book — not that she'd grown crueler in the heart, but that the doing had grown *quiet.* The first killing had been a thunderclap she'd spent a chapter bracing for. This one she arranged between two cups of tea.

She did not go near him. She never went near them. She used the paper, which was her real weapon and always had been.

A factor in the pits stood at a junction of a dozen supply lines — the candles and the coin-sacks and the watered wine and, because the lower city's pharmacopeia ran through one narrow channel, the things men used to settle their nerves and dull their hurts. All of it came up the long grey supply chain of the maester's stores, the great slow river of powders and tinctures that fed every household and dosing-house in the city, and Gael had quietly put a finger into that river two years ago — a clerk she owned, an entry she could move — and never taken it out. A man in Quint's trade took something to sleep; the trade was loud and bright and frightening and a man did not lie down in it without help. She knew which something. She had it in the bestiary, in fact, a tidy reverent entry on the poppy and its cousins — *the milk of the sleeper, three drops a comfort, thirty a doorway* — between a note on dragonbone ash and a sketch of a spider she admired.

She knew the hand it passed through, and the dose that helped, and the dose that didn't. And she changed nothing about the man's life except one quantity, far up a chain he could not see — a single figure shifted by a clerk who thought he was correcting an error, in a tincture a man bought himself, with his own coin, and carried home, and took as he always took it, on a night like any other. She did not watch. She had a keel to inspect. She went out over the roofs and stood in the cold timber-yard with her hand on the seasoned oak and felt the patient qi of it, and somewhere across the city a careful clever man lay down for a sleep he'd bought and paid for himself, and did not wake, and the heavens, as she would have said, balanced the account.

⁂

*— "let go," they're saying, Lothar Quint, the pit-master, "let go" of his place, which is a queer way to say a man went to bed hale and didn't wake, but that's the word that's traveling, he was "let go," and nobody will say by who, and his rooms are emptied and his crew scattered and his wife — he'd a wife, and the little ones — gone north to her people they say, or gone somewhere, and the strange thing, the thing that's got the lower city quiet, is his two house-servants, the cook and the door-man, the watch came for them the morning after, on account of the King's men want everyone near a man who dies sudden, and they went down to the cells to answer about the Coin and about the dead pit-master, and that was a fortnight ago, and the cook's mother goes to the gate every day, and —*

⁂

He had said it, at the end, the thing that in her frame was simply the death-flag cashing and in any other frame was a frightened man pleading with a door he could not see behind.

She had it second-hand, through Marra, who had been there when Quint last leaned on the operators and grown loud and grown small all in the same hour. *I'm not a greedy man,* he'd said. *I'm a careful one. I've only a family to feed, that's all it's ever been, a family to feed.*

Gael, hearing it, felt the small clean click of confirmation she always felt. There it was. The platonic death-flag — *I'm only doing this for my family* — spoken aloud by a man with weeks to live, exactly as the genre promised. She had learned to dread those words in others because the grim world around her killed everyone who said them, and here was the world keeping its bargain again, proving her paranoia sane one more time, and she did not once turn the proof around to see that she was the world. The account is closed, she thought, and meant it as cosmology, the heavens balancing a wayward disciple's greed, and ran her self-audit and cleared herself, because she had not raised a hand, had not been in the room, had merely stood where karma fell.

What she did not see, because the camera of her serene head did not point that way, was the cook's mother at the gate. The cook had a mother. The door-man had a girl he was saving to marry. They had stood near a man who died, in a city whose watch needed someone to take below, and they went down into the dark under the Keep to answer for a ghost and a Coin and a corpse, none of which they had ever touched, and they did not come back up. That was the price of Lothar Quint, paid by people whose names Gael would never know, and the comedy of the chapter — the careful clever man who thought he was the cleverest thing within fifty feet of the maid, and was not the cleverest thing within fifty feet of the *child* — sat on top of it the whole way, the laugh and the ledger, hers and the reader's, never the same.

And out in the city the inquisitor's man heard *let go,* and heard *house-servants taken below,* and heard *a gang-flare around the respectable maid the bestiary had flagged,* and drew, at last, the clean straight line he had been wanting for a year. A natural death that was too convenient. A maid at the center of it. He did not have the Coin. But he had, for the first time, a thread that did not vanish when he pulled it, and it ran toward Wylla, and he began, patiently, to pull.

## III.4 · The Deadline

The King decided to send her north early.

No reason was given, because none was owed to a daughter. But Gael, who read the court the way she read a ledger, could see the shape of it well enough. The Hand's clampdown had made the court airless and strange — men disappearing, the cells loud, a low constant fear running under the marble like damp — and the King, who liked his house orderly and his pieces placed, had decided that the simplest way to be rid of all the trouble that gathered around his odd useless granddaughter was to gather *her* up and send her a thousand miles away to be some Northern lord's problem. A wedding solved a great many things at once. It put the strange girl beyond the reach of the strange rumors. It paid a debt to a powerful house. And it removed from the Red Keep the one loose object the King had never been able to place, who unsettled him in a way he could not name and did not examine, because a king does not examine why a nine-year-old makes the back of his neck cold.

So the word went up the Kingsroad: send the party sooner. And down in the Keep the trousseau-carts began, genuinely now, to be loaded — the linens and the plate and the furs, the apparatus of a girl being shipped north to be made a wife — and the great machine of the wedding, which had been a date on a letter, became carts in a yard and lords on a road and a thing with weight and momentum, bearing down.

Wylla brought the word and the dread together, white around the mouth. "The turn of the year's been moved. They're saying high summer. They're saying — Gael, they're saying weeks. Not months. *Weeks.* The party's already being assembled to ride; the carts are in the yard, I've seen them. They mean to take you."

Weeks. The hull was three months from her keel, even flooded with gold, even with two crews burning night-oil round the clock. Weeks against months. The cliff again, and steeper than it had ever been, and no rooftop in the world to climb to get above it.

So Gael got sick.

She did it the way she did everything, with a craftsman's calm and a complete misunderstanding of why it worked. She took to her bed. She let her color go — there was a tincture for that, far up the maester's chain, a thing that whitened the lips and hollowed the eye and did nothing else — and she let her hands tremble at the right moments and her appetite fail at meals where it would be reported back, and received her visitors all from a heap of pillows, a small frail thing too delicate by half to be jolted a thousand miles up the Kingsroad to a cold castle in autumn.

The maester came and held her wrist and frowned at a pulse that did exactly what she told it to. The septa came and prayed over her and pronounced the illness a trial sent to refine a saint, and was, Gael felt, not entirely wrong. And the King's own man came — sent down to assess whether the bride could ride — a hard grey steward with a list, who stood at the foot of her bed and looked at the wasted little princess and the basin and the shuttered window with the air of a man pricing a horse he suspects of being sound.

"The Hand wishes to know," he said, "when the lady may travel."

Gael turned her head on the pillow, slow, as though it weighed a great deal. She let her eyes go large and wet and unfocused. She was, in her own mind, executing one of the supreme techniques of the cultivation arts — *the pig that eats the tiger,* the master who hides his mountain-moving power inside the body of a dying child — and she performed it for the grey steward with all the art she had, which was considerable, and which was completely unnecessary.

"I want to go north," she whispered. "I want to be a good — I want to do my duty. Tell my grandfather." A small cough, beautifully timed. "I only want to be strong enough not to shame the King upon the road."

The steward looked at her a long moment. Then he wrote something on his list and went away and reported that the bride was genuinely and gravely reduced and would shame no one only by remaining alive to the wedding, and the date slid — a week, then two — and Gael lay back in her pillows the instant the door shut and felt the cold clean satisfaction of a flawless deception, never once suspecting that the steward had believed her before he ever walked in.

She thought it was a masterstroke.

In her frame this was the oldest disguise in the book — *concealing the cultivation, playing the weak pig that eats the tiger.* An immortal who could go over the Holdfast roof in the dark feigning the frailty of a court flower, so flawlessly that not one of these soft mortals suspected the power coiled in her thin little body. She congratulated herself on the performance nightly. She had become, she felt, a master of the hidden base.

She fooled no one, because no one was looking. That was the joke the reader got and she didn't: the realm believed her delicate not because her act was good but because it had written her delicate years ago — the strange quiet scholar-princess, *always* sickly, *always* odd, everyone said so. Her cover was not a thing she'd built. It was a story Westeros had told about her since the cradle, and she stepped into it like a coat already cut to her size, and called the perfect fit her own genius.

She looked at the court from her sickbed with new clarity in those weeks, and what she saw steeled her for the leaving. A sect of soft mortals, she thought. No cultivators among them, not one — only frightened short-lived people clutching at land and each other, and a few tamed spirit-beasts on the island that they had the gall to think made them strong. Nothing here to stay for. Nothing here at all but the door, and the date on it, and the furnace racing the date.

⁂

*To the Conclave, from the maester Lorren, in haste —*

*The bride is gravely reduced. I have seen her keep her color through a fever that emptied half this castle and I have seen her, this fortnight, fade in a draught, and I cannot reconcile the two girls, and have stopped trying. The Hand presses for the wedding regardless; he wants her north and married with a force I find, frankly, disproportionate to one delicate child, as though her continued presence in this Keep were itself a wound he means to close. He has men everywhere now. The cells are not quiet. I do not ask after what fills them. I have learned, in nine years near this child, that the questions one does not ask are the only ones that let a man sleep.*

⁂

The Hand's certainty had become a machine, and the machine needed fuel.

He was sure now — sure of the maid, sure that the maid led to the dark, sure that the dark led to the missing men and the missing money and the daughter the King wanted gone — and a sure man with the cells at his disposal does not wait for proof; he manufactures the conditions in which proof appears. So the watch went down into the lower city in earnest, pulled in the operators it could find, and the smallfolk who had stood near the Quiet Coin's money — the table-fronts, the runners, the clever commoners the buyers thought they dealt with — went down to the black cells to be asked who they worked for.

Some of them did not know. The triple-blind that protected Gael protected nothing of theirs; they had been told to take coin and ask no names, and they had, and now they could give no names, and a man who cannot give a name to an inquisitor in a hurry is a man who stays below. The cells filled. Pella, who had run a faro table, went down and did not come up. Nona, who had carried purses, went down and did not come up. A half-dozen more whose only crime was that a child upstairs had needed hands and the realm had needed someone to blame. They went down into the dark, and the ones who came up came up changed, walking strangely, flinching at doors, and the ones who did not come up were spoken of, after a while, in the past tense, by people who learned not to speak of them at all.

Marra came up.

That was the thing that would matter, though no one could have known it yet. They took her down with the rest, the quick decent woman who'd fronted a table and brought the Greenhand's threat up the line, and they asked her who she worked for, and she did not know, truly did not know, only that a kind man came to her in the cells — not a gaoler, a gentle grey-bearded man who sat with her and gave her water and spoke softly — and told her she'd been used by something wicked, a demon's creature, a maid called Wylla who served a bewitched princess, and that Marra could help save the poor maid and herself both if she only watched, and remembered, and told him where the maid went. And Marra, three days in the dark and wanting only to be out of it and on the side of the kind man instead of the gaolers, said yes, and meant it, and came up into the light believing with her whole grateful heart that she was helping. The kind man's name was Otto. She would feed him the maid's movements for a season, and never once think herself a traitor, because no one had told her she was one.

And the girl who had used them all, who had reached down through three layers of paper to make a market out of their hands, lay in a heap of pillows playing dying, two clocks crossing over her head — the hull crawling up behind, the wedding rushing down — and felt, between performances, only a serene and rising certainty that her timing would hold.

## III.5 · The Tempering

A letter came up from Dragonstone that week, in her mother's own hand, which Gael read once and set aside and Wylla read twice and could not look at.

⁂

*My darling girl —*

*They tell me you are too unwell to travel and my heart is in my throat, but the maester swears it is only a passing weakness and that you will mend, and I choose to believe him because I cannot bear the other thing. I send you this — your grandmother's grey cloak, the warm one, lined, for the cold roads north — and a little book of prayers, and dried plums, which you loved as a babe. Wear the cloak on the journey. Promise me you will keep warm. I think of you on that long road every hour and I am so very proud of how brave you are being, how dutiful, how you go to your wedding without a word of complaint. You were always such a strange brave quiet child. Keep warm, my love. I will see you at the wedding if the Seven are kind, and if they are not, know that no mother ever loved a daughter more. — Your mother.*

⁂

Gael had glanced at the cloak, judged it serviceable against sea-cold, and folded it into the ship-stores. She did not read the rest twice. There was work to do, and the work, that week, was Wylla.

⁂

The days before the rite were the strangest of Wylla's life, which was saying a great deal, given the life.

The ship was days from ready and the wedding was days from arriving and the two facts should have made her princess frantic, and instead Gael had gone calm — gone deep and quiet and busy in a way Wylla had only seen at her meditations, as though the closing of the trap had simplified everything down to a few clean tasks. They packed in the dark hours. Salt-meat, water-casks, oil, grain, the reagent-map, the bestiary. *Voyage-prep,* Gael called it, and most of it made sense — a body needs feeding on a long crossing, a hull needs provisioning — and then there was the other thing, the thing Gael spoke of in the flat technical voice she used for the parts of her work that frightened Wylla most.

"You're not provisioned," she'd said one night, looking at Wylla the way she looked at a cask she suspected of leaking. "The ship is. You're not. An untempered body is a liability at sea. We'll see to it before we sail."

And Wylla, who had learned by now that there was no asking *what do you mean,* had only nodded, and gone cold, and waited for the night Gael would *see to it,* and dreaded it the way you dread a thing you've already decided to let happen because the alternative is leaving the only person you have. She had crossed, somewhere in this last year, from a woman things were done *to* into a woman who chose. She did not feel saved by the crossing. She felt, mostly, that she had gone somewhere she could not come back from, and that the girl who'd taken her there was asleep most afternoons now like an ordinary tired child, and that she would follow her onto the sea regardless, and that this was either love or madness and there was no longer any way to tell them apart.

⁂

It was Wylla who knew it was love, though she would never have used the word, and Gael never would have permitted it.

She had been summoned to the still-room after dark — *bring the long-handled spoons, tell no one* — and she had gone, the way she always went now, with the small old fear and the larger new faith folded together so tight she could no longer find the seam. The still-room was warm, the brazier lit. On the bench Gael had laid out a mix Wylla didn't know, dark and resinous and faintly sweet, and beside it her own thin hands, scrubbed pink, sleeves pushed up past the elbow like a woman about to do real work.

"Sit," Gael said. "Drink this. All of it. It will be unpleasant; it is meant to be. Then be still and don't fight me, whatever you feel. Fighting it makes it hurt."

Wylla drank it. It was unpleasant. She sat, and Gael came and stood behind her and set both small hands flat between her shoulder-blades, and said something low that Wylla didn't catch — *opening the meridians,* it might have been, or some other phrase out of the strange country her princess lived in — and then the warmth came.

It came in through her back like the brazier had reached out a hand. It went down her spine and out along her arms and into her legs, warm and then hot and then a deep terrible *full* feeling, as though her body were a cup that had always been half-empty and was being poured into past its brim. For one whole bright moment Wylla was afraid — purely, animally afraid, the old horror surging up clean and new — because something was *changing* in her, under the skin, in the meat and the blood, and the thing changing her was a nine-year-old girl with her hands flat on her back and her breath going ragged, and Wylla understood with total clarity that her princess was not a girl at all and never had been and that she had bound her whole life to something she would never, ever be able to name.

And then it passed, the way the worst of the sea passes, and left her — warm. Glowing. Steadier than her own body had ever felt. And Gael's hands came away, and Gael sat down hard on the bench like all her strings had been cut, gray-faced, breathing as though she'd run up the Holdfast stair, smaller than Wylla had ever seen her.

Wylla turned and looked at her princess spent on the bench and understood, in the slow way you understand the thing you're forbidden to know, exactly what had happened. She didn't have the cultivator's words for it. She had the human ones. *She poured part of herself into me. She made herself less, to make me more. She has never once made herself less for anything.*

"Don't," Gael said, before Wylla could speak, reading her face and refusing it. "Don't make it a *thing.*"

"What did you —"

"An investment." Gael's voice was thin but the contempt for sentiment was perfectly intact. "You're an untempered mortal. Mortals die at sea — the salt, the cold, the long bad water, the fevers a ship breeds. A liability. I can't crew an immortal vessel with a body that breaks the first hard month. So I've tempered you. Opened your channels, set my own qi running in them to season the flesh. A master-to-disciple transmission. You'll fever-proof now, and sleep less, and last the voyage. That is all this is. Don't look at me like that."

She believed it. That was the wonder of it, and Wylla saw the wonder and held her tongue. Gael genuinely, serenely believed she had performed a cold technical procedure on a piece of equipment she meant to keep — *investing in the asset,* she'd think later, logging it; she logged everything — and had no idea, none, that she had just made the one choice her entire creed forbade. The cultivator's whole law was *accumulate, never spend; ascend, owe nothing; the dao is taken alone.* And she had reached into her own hard-won self and given a measure of it away, on purpose, knowing the cost, to keep one frightened mortal alive across an ocean. The genre had a word for the rite and no word at all for the love, and so she'd filed it under the rite, and the cover was exactly what let her do it. She would have died before she did it for any reason she could name.

Wylla sat in her new warm steady body and looked at the girl who'd given her a piece of herself and called it accounting, and felt the last of the horror burn off her for good, and something take its place that she also would not name, because some doors you don't open. She'd been raising the small flags lately without quite meaning to — *after we're away,* she'd catch herself saying, *when there's the two of us and the sect and the open water, after* — talking about a future the way you do when you've started, against all your sense, to believe in one.

She did not know that Gael heard those flags and dreaded them. That her princess, certain past all argument that everyone who spoke of *after* and *when this is over* and *the future* was a marked soul the grim world would collect — had watched Wylla start to speak of the future and felt the old cold fear close on the one person she'd kept, and had reached, the way she always reached, for the genre's logic to fix it. You could not unsay a death-flag. But a master could *temper* a disciple past the danger the flag foretold. So she'd tempered her. To clear the flag. To save her from an omen.

It was the one spend a cultivator's whole logic forbade, and the genre had handed her the one rationalization that let her make it. *Clearing a flag.* She could not have given a piece of herself away for love — love was an attachment, a weakness, the red dust the dao required her to sever — but she could perform a *transmission rite* on a junior sister to ward off a narrative omen, and so she had, and the thing she would not let herself want she got anyway, smuggled in under cover of cosmology. The most human act of her life, filed as the coldest. She would have died before she did it for any reason she could name.

She was right, in the end, for entirely the wrong reasons, which was the shape of her whole life. The tempering did save Wylla — not from the omen the flag foretold, but from the omen made flesh. The fever-proofing held; the maid would cross the long bad water and not sicken, exactly as promised. But that was the small saving. The large one came at the dock, weeks off, and it ran the other way: the strength Gael had poured into her maid's back to keep her *alive* was the same strength that would let that maid's hands stay steady on a knife and her nerve hold past the hesitation — would let her cross the worst threshold a person crosses, and not break, and not falter long, and do the killing that kept them both free. Gael had set out to clear a death-flag from the one person she loved. What she had actually done was make her a killer. The gift that saved Wylla's life was the gift that took whatever Wylla had been before, and the girl who poured it in would never understand either half of the bargain, because she had filed the whole transaction under *investing in the asset* and closed the book.

⁂

## III.6 · The Narrow Escape

The wedding party came down the Kingsroad on the morning the hull came ready, and the two things met at the water, which was the only fitting place for them to meet.

She had it timed to the hour, or nearly. The shipwright's agent had sent word two nights before — *she swims; she's provisioned; she'll catch the morning tide* — and Gael had read it the way a disciple reads the sign that the furnace has gone quiet and the treasure is forged: the long cycle complete, the vessel true. She'd had the dice-coin's last river run out to fill the holds. Salt-meat and water and oil and grain enough for two girls and a lifetime, because an immortal provisions *forever;* she'd never thought past *forever* to the smaller truer thing, that she'd packed a child's idea of how long two people need to be fed, which was: always.

It came down to a single morning, the way these things do — the two clocks that had run the whole book finally striking the same hour.

The Stark party rode in under banners at first light, a long line of grey and white come a thousand miles to collect a bride, lords and horses and a wheelhouse hung with furs for the cold roads north, and they clattered up to the Keep full of the small grumbling cheer of men at the end of a long ride, ready to be fed and to feast and to carry their lord's new wife away. The King's herald went to wake the princess for her wedding journey. He knocked, and got no answer, and knocked again, and went in, and found a sickbed cold and a window standing open and a girl gone — gone in the dark hours before, over the roofs, the way the ghost of the Red Keep was said to go. He stood in the empty room with the cold sheets in his hand and could not, for a moment, make his mind say the thing his eyes were telling it, because the thing was not possible: brides did not simply *leave.*

And down at the waterline, in the same grey light, a little way along the harbor from where the Stark banners would never think to look, the great forged treasure rode at her mooring with the morning tide just beginning to pull, and a child came down to the water to board her and rise.

Gael had crossed the city one last time in the dark and come down to the docks light as a leaf, Wylla a step behind her carrying the one bag that held the reagent-map and the bestiary and nothing else that mattered, and the harbor was just waking — a few fishermen, a gull or two, the smell of tar and salt and morning. The boat to carry them out to the ship was waiting. Twenty yards of wet planking, and then the sea, and then forever.

She had a vessel and a tide and a crew bought clean. She had, for the first time in three books, *won* — and was a step from the boat, already an immortal in her own mind, already half gone into the open water ahead.

Otto's man was already at the dock.

He was there because Otto, for once, had been right — exactly, precisely right, the one clean deduction of a year of wrong ones. He had pulled the thread from the maid, and it had not vanished, and where every other lead dissolved into the smoke of a phantom Coin, this one held, because this one ran through people who were real. A rival Gael had spared in the spring — the Greenhand man she'd judged not worth the spending, met on the page, let live — had sold the Coin's movements to the King's men for his own skin when the cells started filling. And Marra. Marra, the quick decent smallfolk woman Gael had clocked turning, months back, and had *spared* — looked at, weighed, and let live, because killing her had seemed like effort and she'd seemed harmless and the ship was nearly done — Marra had been feeding Otto the maid's movements for a season, terrified into it and then, worse, *won* into it, because Otto had told her she was helping save a poor bewitched maid from a demon, and Marra had believed him, and kept believing him, and had told his men, just last night, that the maid had asked after the morning tide.

The loose ends she could never close, and the one she had *chosen* not to, came together at the waterline and put Otto's man on the dock with a long knife and orders to take the maid alive and the demon-child dead, and he was a competent man, and he was waiting in the right place, and for one cold moment the whole five years of furnace and paper and rooftop nearly came apart on the last yard of dock.

Gael saw him a half-second late. She was carrying the reagent-map and her own small certainty and was already half into the boat in her mind, already gone, already an immortal on the deck of her vessel watching the lower realm recede — and so she was slow, the only time in three books she was slow, and the man came off the piling fast with the knife low and there was no height to climb to and no paper to hide behind, only the wet boards and the dark water and a competent grown man between a nine-year-old and the sea.

Wylla killed him.

She hesitated first. That was the thing — that was the whole of who she now was, gathered into one half-instant on a dock. She was between him and Gael with the boat-line still in her hand, and her tempered body was fast and her tempered nerves were steady and she *could,* she knew she could, the strength was in her like a held breath — and still she stopped. For the length of a heartbeat the maid who had begun this story fainting at the sight of blood stood at the edge of the worst thing a person can do and felt the full horror of it open under her, clear-eyed, no genre to gloss it, *this is a man and I am about to end him,* the door wide open and her standing in it.

And then she looked at Gael — small, slow, surprised, the only person in the world she had left or wanted — and she crossed.

She did it with the boat-knife, close, the way you do a thing you've decided, and it was not clean and it was not reflexive and it was not a master's stroke; it was a frightened devoted woman committing the whole of herself in one motion because the alternative was a world without the girl who'd poured a piece of herself into her back and called it stock. The man went down between the pilings and the dark water took him the way it had taken the suitor's party, the way the Keep took its people, corpseless and quiet, and Wylla stood over the place breathing hard with her princess's gift running steady and terrible in her arms, and she had become, in that half-second, the last thing the convert's road had left to become.

For a long moment neither of them moved. The harbor went on around them as if nothing had happened — a gull cried, the tide slapped the pilings, somewhere a fisherman called to another fisherman about a net — and a man was gone into the dark water and two girls stood on the wet planking and the world did not so much as pause.

"Get in the boat," Gael said. Her voice was not quite even. She had seen it — had seen the maid she'd made falter at the threshold and then cross it, had seen the whole human shape of the thing she usually never had to look at, the cost up close and wearing a face she loved. She would not say what she'd seen. She filed it somewhere she did not keep a register for, and looked away, and said it again, gentler. "Get in the boat, Wylla. It's done. We're going."

They got in the boat.

The little craft took them out across the brightening water to the vessel riding at her mooring, and the crew Gael had bought clean and questionless hauled them aboard without a word — they had been paid not to have words — and the lines came off the bollards one by one, and the sail came down and filled, and the morning tide took the hull the way the manuals promised it would, the way she had known since the cradle it someday must: slow, then sure, then certain. Out past the anchored ships. Out past the breakwater. Out toward the open grey water, the great forged treasure swimming at last, five years in the furnace and a lifetime in the wanting, and the city falling away behind them course by course like a wall taken down stone by stone.

And as the harbor slid back, Gael made herself look at what she was leaving, because a master should look once upon the lower realm before he rises out of it.

The shore held everything her freedom had cost, if she had only had the eyes to count it. The Keep stood grey on its hill, and under it the black cells were full of people who had touched her money and could not name the hand that paid it — Pella, who had run a faro table and gone down into the dark and not come up; Nona, who had carried purses and gone down and not come up; a dozen more whose only crime was that a child upstairs had needed hands. Out in the lower city the cook's mother still went to a gate each morning and was not let in, and the door-man's girl had stopped saving for a wedding, and the jeweler's washerwoman's sister had learned not to ask. The watch had hunted a ghost across the rooftops all winter and, finding no ghost to take, had taken the powerless instead, and the whole human wreckage of it — the questioned, the changed, the vanished, the ones spoken of now only in the past tense — lay strewn along the coast behind her like the long bright wake a ship leaves, widening, fading, gone. Marra was down there too, somewhere, still believing she had helped, still believing in a kind grey man who had told her she was saving someone. They had paid for Gael twice over, the smallfolk — used by her market, then swept up by the realm's terror for crimes she had committed and they had not — and they would go on paying after she was a speck on the horizon, because the Keep would need someone to blame for a princess who simply vanished, and it would never, ever blame the princess.

Gael looked at all of it and did not see one piece of it. She saw a lower realm receding. She saw the red dust she was severing to rise. She filed the whole grim shore under *attachments left behind* — serene, cold, oblivious to the very last — and turned her face to the open sea.

She had one tie left to sever, and she reached for it, because the dao is thorough.

Her mother. Alicent, away on Dragonstone, who had given her the egg in the cradle and smothered her with love for nine years and was, this morning, being told that her daughter was simply gone. Gael stood at the rail with the continent going small behind her and reached for the gloss to file it, the way she'd filed every cost of every chapter, the cool clean cultivator's frame that made the unbearable bookkeeping —

*Mother: an attachment severed. The dao requires that one feel nothing, cutting the red dust. The disciple ascends alone, and owes —*

And it didn't come.

There was a blank where the register should have been. She reached again and the words were not there; the frame that had caught everything, that had turned a dead hatchling and a drained dragon and a vanished suitor and a poisoned factor each into a clean line she could carry — found nothing to write here, closed on nothing, came up empty. She stood at the rail with her hand out for a sentence that would not finish itself, and felt the place where it should have been, the small cold draught of it.

She did not name it. She looked at the water.

She went on.

Beside her, Wylla saw her princess go quiet at the rail, go still in a way that was not meditation, and did not ask. Some doors you don't open. She put her tempered, terrible, steady hand on the cold wood near Gael's, not touching, just near, and the two of them watched the grey water and the continent getting smaller, and the south ahead getting warmer, and neither of them said anything at all.

## Epilogue

⁂

Wylla stood at the rail on the fourth day and watched the water and tried to find the place where she ended.

The continent was long gone. The water was warmer now, and bluer than the grey northern sea she'd been born to, and the wind was kind, and below decks her princess was asleep — actually asleep, spent and recovering, breathing like an ordinary tired child, which she did more often now that the chase was behind them and which Wylla had learned to watch for and not mention. There was meat enough in the hold for a lifetime, and water, and oil. There was a girl who had given her a piece of her own self and would deny it with her last breath. There was the open sea, the warm south, and a sect of two that would never have a third.

She turned her hands over and looked at them. They were steady. They had been steady since the dock. The gift held; the warmth ran clean in her still, fever-proof, sea-durable, exactly as promised. The same hands had done up a princess's hair, and fronted a criminal empire, and held a dying woman's secret, and, four days ago, on a wet dock at dawn, put a knife into a man and not shaken.

She was a noble girl who had become a maid who had become a manager who had become a believer who had become, four days ago, a killer. She knew all the words for the road behind her. She did not have one for where it had ended. *What am I now,* she thought, watching the water, and found that she could turn the question over and over and never make it sting — that it sat in her, open, unanswered, the way the warm horizon sat ahead of them, neither a promise nor a threat, only a thing she had chosen and would go on choosing, eyes open, because the alternative was a world without the girl asleep below.

She did not ask it aloud. There was no one to ask, and no answer she needed. She watched the water. It was very blue. She found, to her own quiet surprise, that she was not afraid.

⁂

The realm, for its part, never did work out what had happened, which suited everyone.

There had been a princess, and then there had not, and a Keep cannot abide a hole that size in its own account of itself, so it filled the hole with whatever came to hand. The official version was that the lady Gael, ever delicate, had been lost — drowned, most said, in a boating mishap on the eve of her northern wedding, a tragedy, the Seven keep her. The unofficial versions were better. A foreign prince had carried her off; she'd been seen on a Tyroshi galley with her hair dyed and a lover at the rail. A god had taken her, the way the gods took the too-beautiful and the too-strange. The septas favored the prophecy — she had been the chosen of some scripture all along, gathered up to her purpose, the maegi-princess ascended, and Aldith wept at the wonder of having tended a saint and never knowing it. Septon Barth, the one mind in the realm that had ever come within a hand's breadth of the truth, set the contradictions side by side in his careful way — the missing men, the missing money, the missing girl — and felt the shape of an answer move just under the surface of them, and was called away to the King's war before he could close his hand on it, and never came back, and died with the theory one fact short.

The Hand never closed his case. He had been right — gloriously, uniquely right, his one true deduction in a year of false ones — and his man had been in the right place at the right hour, and still the girl was gone and his man gone with her, vanished off a dock at dawn into the same nothing that had swallowed the suitor's party a year before. He had won every move. He had lost the game. He filed the matter, in the end, as an open disappearance, a wound that would not close, and went back to the King's business, and told himself the girl had drowned, because the alternative was that a child had beaten him, and that was not a thing a man like Otto could afford to have believed, least of all by himself.

⁂

*To the Conclave, from the maester Lorren, the last he wrote upon the matter —*

*My lords, I will trouble you no further with the princess Gael, who is drowned, the court is agreed, and whose strangeness I have catalogued these nine years to no profit and considerable cost to my sleep. I record only, for the honesty of the archive, that I do not believe she drowned. I believe nothing in particular instead; I have no theory; I have learned the danger of theories where this child is concerned. I note merely that the girl who could not be poisoned and would not weaken and walked, some swore, upon the air, has gone out of the world by water on the one morning the water could carry her, and that I feel, writing it, not grief but a queer cold relief, as a man feels when a storm he has watched gather all season finally breaks over someone else's roof. May the Seven keep her, wherever she is. I find I hope, against all sense, that she is well. I will not write of her again.*

⁂

For not one of them, not the King nor the Hand nor the Conclave nor the singers who would make a sad pretty song of the drowned princess, could reach the only explanation that fit: that a girl had wanted to leave, and had built a way, and had taken it. A foreign prince they could imagine. A god, a prophecy, a conspiracy of unseen men — all of these the realm could hold. A princess with the will and the patience and the cold clear agency to author her own escape from the story written for her was the one thing Westeros could not picture — could not even reach the edge of, because the realm had spent her whole life telling itself she was delicate, useless, a marriage-piece, a strange quiet girl to be placed and forgotten — and so it never did, and the truth sailed south unimagined while the legend sealed shut behind it like water over a stone. The last and best of the realm's cheat codes, the one that had hidden her since the cradle, held to the end: no one looked for a girl with agency, because no one believed there could be one.

Far out on Dragonstone, where she had gone to keep her grief away from a court she could not bear, Alicent was told that her daughter was gone, and got, at the last, her terminal *what the hell* — not a fret this time, not a misread, just a mother standing at a high cold window with the news in her hand and nothing in her at all but the size of the absence. She would mourn the wrong girl forever, the delicate strange beloved daughter who had drowned; she would never learn the true one, never know that the daughter had lived and chosen and sailed, never know that her own smothering, blundering, lifelong love had been the one shield that kept the cold father and the hunting grandfather off the child long enough for the child to grow her way free. She had been the wall Gael never saw she stood behind. She would die not knowing it. She looked out at the same grey water her daughter had crossed and did not know she was looking the right way, and after a while she went in, and the door closed, and the sea went on without comment.

Two girls on a ship, the continent behind them, the warm south ahead. One of them slept; one of them watched the water. Both of them had got exactly what they'd wanted, which is the most dangerous thing that can happen to anyone in any story, and one of them was now a killer, and the sea did not care, and the wind was kind, and whether it was freedom they were sailing into or only a prettier cage of their own making was a question the water would not answer and the girls would not ask.

They sailed on. The south was warm. The door was open.
