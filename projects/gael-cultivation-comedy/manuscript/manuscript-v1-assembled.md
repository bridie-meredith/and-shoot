# DRAGONBANE
### *a comedy of cultivation, in grimdark Westeros*
#### Books I–III  ·  *(working title)*

> A reincarnated modern nerd, reborn as a dragonless Targaryen princess, is convinced she has been
> dropped into a cultivation novel. She grinds dragon-adjacent matter into immortality reagents, reads
> every grimdark horror around her as a genre trope, and — because Westeros's cruelties make accidentally
> perfect cover — builds a criminal empire and escapes by sea, without ever once understanding the world
> she is in. The reader understands it completely.

---

# BOOK I — *the cage*

## I.1 · Dragonbane

The egg would not stop humming at her, so Gael, who was four days old and bored to the seams of her small wet existence, did the only sensible thing a cultivator could do with a radiant spirit-relic left in her crib: she got her gums around it and bit down.

The shell was warm. It had been warm since they laid it beside her, a black-and-scarlet thing the length of her forearm, and it had been singing to her the whole time in a register that sat just under hearing, the way a struck bell sings after the sound is gone. Nobody else in the room seemed to hear it. Gael had decided some hours ago that this was significant. She had been dead, after all, and now she was not, and there was a relic humming in her crib, and a person who had read four hundred cultivation novels and died face-down in the four-hundred-and-first knew a spirit-treasure when one was placed at her elbow.

So she bit it. The shell took a chip of her gum with it, a bright clean pain, and her mouth filled with blood, and into the blood came the other thing — the warmth, the bell-note, pouring off the broken edge of the egg and into her like water finding a low place. She swallowed the blood and the warmth both.

*Qi*, she thought, with enormous satisfaction. *There it is.*

So. Isekai'd. She'd suspected it since the ceiling — no ceiling in the afterlife had any business being that water-stained — and here was the confirmation, sweet as a lozenge. A lesser disciple might have panicked at being four days old and unable to lift her own head. Gael simply began, methodically, to draw. Nine revolutions, the way the manuals said, warmth into the belly, hold, release. She had a cauldron to forge. She had, she reflected, all the time in the world. She intended to make it considerably longer.

Above her a wet-nurse leaned in, saw the blood on her lips and the egg in her two fat fists, and made a sound like a kettle.

Gael filed the sound under *ambient sect activity* and kept drawing.

The egg disagreed.

She felt it the way you feel a held breath about to break — the bell-note climbing, the warmth in the shell turning frantic, pushing back against her pull. *Resisting cultivation,* she thought, scandalized. *A treasure with a will of its own.* This was, she knew, a known phenomenon; the high-grade relics always fought you; you had to subdue them. She set her tiny jaw and pulled harder, all nine revolutions at once, greedy.

The egg split down its long axis with a wet structural crack.

What came out of it was not warmth. It was teeth. It was a hand's-length of wet black hide and a head all jaw, blind and steaming, mewling, hauling itself up over the wreck of its own shell toward the only heat in the cradle, which was her. Its claws were already out. They were the size of grass-seeds and they were the sharpest things she had ever felt, and one of them opened a line down her cheek before she got her arm up.

*Trial-beast,* Gael thought, with the clean joy of a reader who has reached the part she was promised. *First kill. Power from the first kill — textbook.* The genre did not lie about everything. She got both hands around the small thrashing throat — her grip strength was an abomination, four days old or not, the warmth had seen to that — and she squeezed and bore down and put her whole tiny weight into it, and the hatchling raked her arms to ribbons and screamed, and she did not let go.

It took longer than the novels implied. That was the only complaint she would later file. In the novels the first kill was a paragraph; nobody ever mentioned that a paragraph could go on this long, that a thing could take this much killing, that her own arms would shake. She made a note of the discrepancy. *Two stars for accuracy,* she thought, somewhere under the effort. *The genre oversells the ease and undersells the mess.* She would learn, over the years, that it always did.

When it stopped, she lay panting in a cradle gone red, the dead thing across her chest, and she did the methodical thing: she got a claw loose from its slack foot, and she ate it. Tougher than the shell. Worth it. The warmth that came up out of it was a different colour entirely, deeper, older, and she drew it down into her belly, nine revolutions, and felt her dantian — she was sure now she had a dantian — settle and bank like a covered fire.

She lay there a while afterward in the cooling red, taking stock with the satisfaction of a clerk closing good accounts. She had been alive four days and she had already survived a culling, banked her foundation, and made her first kill. By the arithmetic of every novel she'd ever read, she was ahead of schedule. The discomfort — the trembling, the long bad minute when the thing wouldn't die, the wet weight of it now stiffening on her chest — she set aside as a cost already paid and therefore not worth feeling. She had read four hundred of these books. She knew the shape of a beginning when she was lying in one.

She was still drawing it down when the nanny came in, and screamed, and dropped the basin, and ran.

*To the Conclave, from the Citadel's man at the Red Keep:*

*The princess Gael, fourth living child of King Jaehaerys and Queen Alysanne, four days delivered, presents a trance-state I can compare to nothing in my training save deep study or a greyscale stupor — yet she is in rude health, gaining weight, taking the breast (when a nurse can be found who will hold her). She lies open-eyed and rigid the better part of each day. The hatchling was found dead in the cradle; the dragonkeepers cannot account for it; the maester before me would have said the babe rolled upon it, but the babe cannot yet roll. I have no diagnosis. I have requested more parchment.*

⁂

It was the wet-nurse who put the word out, and the word was *cursed*. A babe that never cried. A babe that lay still and open-eyed half the day "like a little corpse practising." She would not take the night shift; nor would the next one; nor the one after, once she'd seen the dried blood crusted in the seams of the cradle and heard about the dead dragon. Two nurses gave notice in a sennight. In the guardroom a practical man with a spear said, "Good. One fewer dragon for the stables to feed," and was hushed, but not before he'd grinned.

None of this reached Gael, who had problems of her own. She was, she had concluded, in an evil sect.

She had not wanted to conclude this. She'd held out hope. But she was a fair-minded soul, and the evidence was the evidence: *what righteous order drops a lizard-monster into a cradle with a four-day-old disciple?* That was a culling. That was a trial designed to weed out the weak before they cost the sect anything. The warm presence that had laid the egg here — she could feel it sometimes, a soft despairing heat that came and bent over her and dripped on her face — must be an inner-sect elder, then, running the cull and grieving the ones who failed it. The grief told her the elder was conflicted. *A reluctant villain*, she thought, charitably. She would remember that.

This raised the obvious question, the only one that mattered, and she turned it over in the long open-eyed hours while the household crept around her crib. *What am I, in this story?* The overlooked reincarnator, plucked from a low place, destined to rise? She liked that. Or — and here she was scrupulous, here she ran the audit she would run a thousand times after — the *villainess*? The reborn schemer everyone roots against, the one whose whole arc is her comeuppance? Because she had, undeniably, just strangled a baby animal with her bare hands in a crib and eaten part of it, and that did read a little villainous, set down plainly.

She cleared herself on a technicality. The thing had attacked *her*. Self-defence. A righteous cultivator was permitted self-defence; it was practically the first rule. She resolved, very seriously, to be one of the good ones, *especially* now that she knew the org she'd been born into was rotten. She would not become the villainess. She would simply get strong, and watch the people above her — there were powers in this place, she could feel them moving, comings and goings that pressed on her senses with a weight that meant authority, and one of them in particular, cold and very high, that her gut filed at once as *sect-master, final boss* — and she would keep a list, and she would learn the shape of the cage before she so much as rattled it.

She lacked a young master. The genre promised one — the arrogant disciple, the rival who dies early to teach the protagonist that the world has stakes — and she had not yet located him. She settled provisionally on the spearman who'd grinned in the guardroom, on the grounds that he had a stupid face and a loud voice, which was most of the qualification. It was thin evidence. She knew it was thin. She filed him anyway, because an unfilled role made her anxious, and moved on.

She watched for the death flags, too. She'd started watching for them the moment she'd worked out where she was, because the death flag was the cheapest information the genre gave you and the most useful: a character speaks fondly of going home, of the wedding they'll have when this is all over, of the child they're saving coin for — and then they die, every time, the omen as reliable as a struck bell. In a world full of murder, a person who could read the flags lived longer than a person who couldn't. So she listened, in the cradle, to the nurses and the guards passing the door, and she catalogued who spoke of futures and who didn't, and resolved to keep clear of the ones who did. It was, she'd find, the single piece of genre wisdom that grimdark Westeros never once let her down on. The realm killed flag-raisers with a thoroughness no novelist would have dared. She would be right about the death flags, again and again, for entirely the wrong reasons, for the rest of her life.

Her mother came in the dark and lifted her out of the bad crib and held her against the soft despairing heat and wept into her hair and whispered that she was strong, she was *strong*, look how strong, her brave clever girl who'd killed a dragon in her cradle and lived — and Gael, drawing nine slow revolutions of warmth out of the woman's pulse without the least idea she was doing anything unkind, agreed completely.

She had unlocked her first title. The nurses had supplied it, hissing it in the corridors, and she'd caught it through the door and turned it over and found it good.

*Dragonbane.*

She had been alive four days. She was off to a frankly excellent start.

## I.2 · The Heist

The lock was a problem of leverage, and Gael, who was two and a half and could now reach the latch if she stood on the slop-bucket, had been studying leverage for some weeks.

She had plateaued. This was the shameful truth at the bottom of her, the thing she examined in the long meditative hours and could not fix: she had eaten her crib-monster, banked the warmth, drawn the egg dry months ago — and then nothing. No new fuel. You could not forge a cauldron on memories. A cultivator without reagents was a forge without ore, and the Red Keep, for all its grimness, did not leave spirit-treasures lying about in the cradles of toddlers a second time. She had been reduced to drawing thin warmth off people, off the cat, off a brazier once until a nurse snatched her back — subsistence cultivation, beneath her dignity.

But she had ears, and she paid attention, and she had heard the maester's still-room described in a hushed voice as a place of "poisons that would drop a horse." Poisons. Concentrated essences. *Reagents.* A righteous disciple did not steal, of course. But she had reasoned her way around that weeks ago: a sect that fed monsters to infants had no moral claim on its storeroom, and besides, she was only borrowing, and besides that, *the strong take what they need to grow* was a load-bearing axiom of every novel she'd ever read. She'd run the self-audit. She'd cleared herself. She was, she had decided, simply doing a heist, and a heist was a respectable thing for a cultivator to do.

She had a method now. She was proud of the method. *Sight, plan, implement, reward.* She'd seen the door (left ajar by an acolyte hauling water; the man never latched it twice running). She'd planned the route (under the long table while the acolyte's back was turned, behind the curing-rack, up the shelf by the gaps between jars, which she'd practised on the nursery shelving until her grip held). She implemented it now, in the grey hour after the noon meal, a small grave child going hand over hand up a wall of crockery with the focus of a master ascending a sword-peak.

The shelf was a wonderland. Forty jars, fifty, labelled in a hand she couldn't read, and she didn't need to read them, because she could *feel* them — most of them dull, inert, dead mineral, but here and there a jar that hummed the way the egg had hummed, that warmth-under-hearing, that *yes*. She passed over the loud-smelling ones. She passed over a powder that made her eyes sting. She put her hands flat on the jars one by one until she found the one that sang loudest — a squat black-glazed pot near the back, sealed in wax — and she worked the wax with her thumbnails, patient, and got it open, and dipped a finger, and tasted.

It was, in fact, the one jar in the room that would drop a horse.

It went down her throat like a hot wire, and her whole small body seized, and for a heartbeat she thought, with genuine interest, *ah, so this is how I die the second time* — and then the warmth came up through the burn, enormous, the most she'd taken since the egg, and her body took the poison and *ate* it. Turned it. Banked it. She sat back on the shelf among the jars with her finger still in her mouth, flushed and glowing faintly and entirely well, and after a moment she dipped her finger again, because waste was a sin.

It had been, she reflected later, almost insultingly easy. And that was the thing that troubled her, walking the long way back to the nursery with the warmth purring in her belly. It troubled her for days.

Because a *protagonist struggles*. That was the rule under all the other rules. The reincarnated hero claws up a mountain of suffering; every reagent is paid for in blood and cunning and the bones of rivals. And her life was — frictionless. She had walked into a poison vault that a child could open, picked the single lethal jar by instinct, drunk a horse's death, and improved. No guard. No trial. No rival lunging from the dark. Even her *power* had come easy, handed to her in a cradle. That was not a hero's data. That was — she turned it over, and didn't like it, and made herself look at it squarely — *set dressing*.

She wasn't the protagonist. She saw it clearly, sitting in the truth of it. She was the *prize*. The kept jade-beauty in the deep palace of the evil sect, the dangling reward, the thing the *real* hero would someday fight his way in to claim. That was why it was all so easy — you don't make the treasure earn its place on the pedestal. You just keep it beautiful and wait for someone to come win it.

She found, examining the feeling, that she was relieved. Heroes had terrible mortality rates. Heroes got tortured in act two. If the realm wanted to file her as the passive reward and stop watching her — well. She had a method, and a poison-vault she could re-open, and a whole evil sect that had just decided she was furniture. A prize that quietly helps itself, she thought, while everyone admires the pedestal, is the most active person in any room. Let them not-win her. She'd be busy.

*To the Citadel, urgent: A jar is short. The black-glazed, the essence-of-monkshood, sealed when I last inventoried it, now broached — and no man dead, no horse dropped, the household entire and hale. I have questioned the still-room boy until he wept. He swears the count was right, swears the levels are down, swears the princess-child glows. The boy is unwell and drinks. I am left with two propositions and I dislike them equally: either this thing cannot poison, or it should not exist. Send a second opinion. Send more parchment.*

⁂

The boy was not believed, of course. Hobb, his name was, the still-room boy, and he kept saying it — *I counted right, I counted twice, the little one was up there, I saw her on the shelf* — and the more he said it the more they pitied him and reached for the wineskin on his behalf. A toddler scaling the still-room. A child glowing. He took to drink in earnest. He was the only person in the whole affair who was completely correct about everything, and it ruined him.

Somebody had to answer for the broached jar, because thieving from the king's own house is the Wall if you're lucky and the block if you're not, and the obvious somebody was the acolyte who'd left the door ajar. He protested. He protested to the floor and the walls and a guardsman who wasn't listening; he had never touched the jar, he didn't even know which jar, he'd only gone for water. None of it weighed anything. Hand of the King set his seal to the sentence — Septon Barth, who at that hour was theorising in his solar that the immune child might be *blessed of the gods, or cursed of them, the symptoms being identical* — and the man was marched north before the moon turned. The Wall took him. The household closed over the gap the way water closes over a stone, and within a fortnight nobody at the Red Keep could have told you his name, and nobody, nobody at all, connected the missing poison to the small grave princess who had begun, that month, to thrive so visibly that her mother wept with relief.

There was a new girl about the maester's rooms in those days — a minor-noble daughter, a few years older than Gael, sent up to court to be aged into a lady-in-waiting, brought through the still-room corridors for her lessons and her health. Wylla, she was called. She was there the day they found the jar short, hovering at the edge of the grown men's anger, and she had — though she could not have said why she was sure — *seen* something. A small shape on the high shelf, the week before, where no small shape should be. She did not say it. The men were already furious; the acolyte was already weeping; saying it would have changed nothing except to put her own self into the centre of a terror she did not understand. So she shut her mouth and watched the acolyte marched out, and felt sick, and buried it. She was eight years old. She had just stood beside the first crime, and chosen, out of fright, to say nothing — which is exactly how every long complicity begins.

Gael never noticed her. Gael, when she noticed her at all in the months after, filed her in the small clay-tablet journal she'd begun to keep — beasts and reagents in a reverent hand she was still mastering, humans by function — under *Asset (hands, legs)*. A useful pair of hands that went where Gael could not yet go. She did not record, because she did not know, that the asset had seen her on the shelf.

Her mother, told that the princess had eaten something that ought to have killed her and instead grown rosy, did not faint or pray. Her mother clasped her hands and declared, radiant, that the child had *a refined palate and a scholar's curiosity* — a connoisseur's stomach, a seeker's mind — and that such gifts must be nourished. And she assigned her daughter a tutor, the most learned man in the Keep, to feed that hungry curiosity.

The maester. The man whose vault she had robbed. Installed now as her personal instructor, with standing leave to be in her company and answer her every question.

Gael accepted the punishment for her crime — which was unlimited supervised access to the storeroom and its keeper — with the grave decorum of a disciple receiving a great and undeserved boon, and added a second title to her quiet collection. The corridors had supplied it, the way they'd supplied the first.

*Poison-Eater.*

Two stars for the genre's plotting, she thought. The prize wasn't supposed to fail upward this hard. But she'd take it.

## I.3 · The Cage

The skulls were enormous and they hummed, and Gael had been edging toward them across the throne-room floor for the better part of an hour, one careful pace at a time, in the spirit-tranced creep of a disciple approaching a forbidden relic.

She was four, perhaps five — she'd lost the thread of it, time being a thing that happened to weaker people — and the great hall of the Red Keep was a forest of dead dragons. They lined the walls, skull upon skull, the small ones the size of hounds and the great ones the size of carriages, Balerion at the head black as a hole in the world, and every one of them *sang*. That under-hearing bell-note, the egg's note, multiplied a hundredfold and old, banked, vast. The whole hall was a treasury and nobody guarded it, which fit her theory of the place exactly: an evil sect leaves its dead spirit-beasts on display, because in an evil sect the display *is* the threat. She did not intend to take anything. Not yet. She only wanted to put her hands on Balerion and *listen*, and learn what a relic that old could teach her about the shape of her own banked fire.

She got within three paces before the guards converged, and then there were large men with their hands under her arms, lifting her away from the black skull, and she went limp and serene in their grip — she'd learned that limp and serene got you set down faster than thrashing — and let them carry her out, having memorised the floor plan, the sightlines, the rotation of the watch, and the precise angle she'd need next time. *Proximity is not access,* she noted, filing the lesson without rancour. *Foiled, observed. Adjust.*

What she had not expected was to be summoned. The sect-master himself. An audience.

She prepared for it the way she prepared for everything, which was thoroughly and on entirely false premises. The patriarch of an evil sect did not call a disciple in for nothing. This would be a *test* — or, better, a bloodline-revelation, the moment the cold elder unbends just far enough to hand the overlooked youngster the forbidden manual that is her birthright. She practised her humble-disciple etiquette in the nursery for two days. She would keep her eyes lowered. She would watch his hands — the manual would pass hand to hand, it always did, a scroll, a jade slip, a pressed seal. She would be ready to receive it.

Jaehaerys received her in a small cold room and looked at her across it for a long time, an old king with a face like a shut door, and then he told her, in a voice with no heat in it at all, that a princess did not climb upon the bones of her forebears. That the dragons of the conquest were not toys. That she would conduct herself, in future, as befit her blood and her House, or there would be consequences, and that he had better things to attend to than the misbehaviour of the youngest of his daughters, of whom — he did not say this, but it stood in the room with them, cold as he was — he expected very little.

Gael lowered her eyes and nodded and said, gravely, that she understood.

She watched his hands the whole time. No manual came. She was, briefly, disappointed.

And then she understood the audience, and her disappointment evaporated, because she'd been reading it as a young disciple and she should have been reading it as a *strategist*. Of course no manual. A manual would have been *too much favour* — lavish, suspect, the kind of attention that gets a rising disciple resented and watched. What the sect-master had given her instead was exactly calibrated: cold, brief, conditional, distant. The precise, measured, deniable acknowledgement that an evil-sect patriarch extends to a disciple he has noticed and means to keep on a long leash. Any warmer would have been a lie; any colder, an insult. This was *patronage*. This was the elder telling her, in the only language his rank permitted, *I see you, I am not done with you, do not embarrass me.* She left the cold room flushed with quiet triumph. Favour secured.

Jaehaerys, watching the small solemn nodding girl led out, noted that she had taken the correction well — docile, chastened, no trouble — and dismissed her from his mind, and never knew that he had just confirmed, to a four-year-old crime-lord, her standing as a made disciple. They both walked out of the cold room satisfied. They were both completely wrong. It was, the room being what it was, almost peaceful.

There had been an empty place at the maester's that whole season — a stool nobody sat on, a name nobody said, a hush where the acolyte had been. Gael had noticed it. She read it correctly as a removal and incorrectly as everything else. *The inner court culled a weak disciple,* she thought; *sect politics, above my station, someone moved against someone.* The lesson she drew was that the sect was more dangerous than she'd reckoned, that powers were moving in the dark, and that she must hide her strength better and grow *colder*, give nothing away, trust no one. She did not connect the empty stool to the broached jar. She did not connect the broached jar to herself. She mistook the wake of her own crime for ambient menace, and let it temper her in precisely the wrong direction, and never once suspected that the reason a man was freezing at the end of the world was four years old and resolving, in his honour, to be more careful with her poisons.

She had also noticed the watcher. The older girl, the one filed *Asset*, was always somewhere at the edge of things now — running errands past her door, eyes on her at the wrong moments, present too often to be chance. Gael, freshly cold, freshly paranoid, drew the obvious conclusion: a monitor-disciple. A spy, planted by some elder to report on the promising youngster. She resolved to feed the spy false impressions, to be boring and pious in the watcher's sightline, and she upgraded the journal entry — *Asset (hands, legs). Surveillance. Manage.* She had correctly identified that the girl mattered. She had inverted, completely, every single thing about *how*. The person she filed as a threat to be deceived was the one who would, in the fullness of a terrible decade, convert and be tempered and kill on her behalf — and the most important relationship of Gael's whole second life began, on her side, as counter-espionage.

She tested the spy once, to be sure. She left a thing where the girl would see it — a scrap of clay-tablet with a few false marks, a meaningless diagram she'd invented to look like a secret — and watched, sidelong, to see whether it would travel up some chain to some elder. It didn't travel anywhere. The girl had glanced at it, and at Gael, and said nothing, and gone on with her sweeping with a face Gael could not read and therefore filed as *trained*. A good spy, she concluded, approving despite herself. Patient. Gives nothing. She would have to be careful with this one. She upgraded the entry a second time — *Asset (hands, legs). Surveillance. Skilled. Manage with care* — and felt the small clean pleasure of an opponent correctly assessed, and was wrong, root and branch, about every part of it. The girl had said nothing because the girl was frightened, and loyal to no one, and already, without a word for it, half in orbit around the strange child she'd been set to watch.

What she did not see — what the chapter was, underneath, entirely about — was that the watcher was caged too.

⁂

*To the Citadel: the doubled lessons. I had thought to weary her into ordinary childhood — septa in the mornings, scripture and stitchery and the duties of a princess; myself in the afternoons, that she might tire of questions. She tires of nothing. She accepts double the burden of any child her age without a flicker of complaint. The septa calls it piety and is unsettled by it. I would call it appetite, were it directed at anything I could name. She studies the cage we have built for her — Faith, marriage, House — with the calm of a clerk taking inventory, and I cannot for my life decide whether she is the most dutiful child in the realm or merely the most patient. More parchment.*

⁂

The thing the maester half-saw and could not name, Gael had seen entire, sitting through the septa's morning lessons with her hands folded and her face still: the *real* cage. Not the guards, not the skulls she couldn't reach — those were nothing, those were a floor plan. The cage was the lessons themselves. Septa Aldith, doubling the hours and crediting herself loudly for the girl's blossoming devotion, was unknowingly teaching her the bars: that a princess was a piece on a board, that her body was a treaty, that there was a marriage coming someday with her name on it and her wishes nowhere in it, that the Faith and the dynasty and the simple fact of being a girl were three walls of a room she had not agreed to enter. *Outward, yes,* Gael concluded, surveying it with that clerk's calm. *Inward, absolutely not.* She rated the Faith's worldbuilding poorly — derivative cosmology, seven gods, no cultivation in it anywhere, a righteous-sect antagonist with very dull doctrine — and she filed the marriage-clause the way she filed everything she could not yet defeat: as a problem of leverage, to be studied, to be routed around, in time, by method.

And Wylla, eleven now and grown quiet, ran the small errands and watched the strange small princess at her serene lessons and said nothing — about the still-room, about the shelf, about any of it, to anyone. Sitting in the cold of the corridor between tasks, she knew without the words for it that her own road was drawn for her too: a daughter of a small house, posted to court to be useful, her marriage and her place arranged in rooms she would never be admitted to. She had no name yet for the kinship she felt with the eerie child she'd been set to wait upon. She only knew they were both being moved by larger hands, and that the princess didn't seem to know it, and that this somehow frightened her more than if she had.

## I.4 · Valyrian Beauty

The sword on the wall of the lower gallery had been singing to Gael for a month, and tonight, at six years old and barefoot in the dark, she had finally come to eat it.

It was Valyrian steel — she didn't have the word, but she had the *note*, and the note was unmistakable: dragon-kin, the same banked-bell hum as the skulls and the egg, folded into a blade. A minor lord's heirloom, hung in a draughty gallery nobody walked at night, guarded by a sleeping man she could step around like a piece of furniture. *Dragon-steel,* she thought, reverent. *Distilled, forged, concentrated. The finest reagent in the Keep.* She had no idea how to eat a sword. She would solve that. She had eaten harder things than they looked.

She got it off the pegs — heavier than it had any right to be, she had to drag it — and she set the edge against her teeth and bit, and her cultivated jaw, which had filed through bone before, met the steel and *did not slip*. It gave. A curl of it came away into her mouth like a shaving of cold dark sugar, and the warmth that came up off it was not warmth at all but a roar, a flood, the biggest single draught since the cradle and the poison put together, and she sat down hard in the dark with the sword across her knees and *drew*.

The breakthrough took her like a wave takes a swimmer. She felt the banked fire in her belly catch and roar and climb, felt some wall inside her she'd been pressing against for two years simply *go*, felt the whole architecture of herself re-settle into a higher shape. Her senses bloomed — she could hear the sleeping guard's heart three rooms off, could see the grain of the floorboards in the dark. And her body changed under her hands as she sat there. She stopped feeling tired. She would, she'd find over the coming weeks, stop *sleeping* much at all — two hours, an hour, none — and never flag. She would find she could go up a sheer wall now with the quiet certainty of a cat, that she could fall and not bruise, that nothing wore her down and very little hurt her. Not a fighter — she had no interest in fighting, fighting was for protagonists — but a child the world could simply no longer get a grip on. *Body refinement,* she thought, glowing in the dark with a Valyrian sword half-eaten in her lap. *Post-breakthrough. Impurities expelled.* She was, frankly, proud of the readout.

She finished as much of the blade as she could carry inside her, propped the ruined rest behind a chest where it might not be found for a year, and went back to bed to consolidate.

The consolidation took weeks, and they were the best weeks of her second life. She'd lie in the dark — she barely needed the bed anymore, sleep had become a thing she visited rather than required — and feel the new realm settling into her, the way a house settles after it's built, small ticks and creaks of her own body reorganising itself around the dragon-steel. She tested the gifts as they came in. She climbed the nursery wall to the ceiling and clung there upside down for an hour out of sheer scientific interest, watching the room from the angle of a spider, and felt no strain and no fear of falling. She held her hand in a candle-flame longer than a hand should go and felt the heat as information rather than pain. She went a full day and night without food and noticed only that she'd forgotten to be hungry. *Tireless,* she catalogued, pleased. *Sleepless. Hard to mark. Hard to wear down.* Not a fighter's gifts — she had no use for those, fighters were protagonists and protagonists died in act two — but a survivor's, a thief's, the gifts of a thing the world could no longer quite get its hands on. She added them to the journal in the reverent hand, under her own name, the only human entry she would ever write with any warmth, and even that was really an entry about a reagent's effects.

The trouble started when people began to look at her.

⁂

*To the Citadel, in some distress: I have a contradiction I cannot dissolve and it is keeping me from sleep. Valyrian steel cannot be destroyed by any forge in the world; it cannot be reworked save by arts we no longer possess; it certainly cannot be — I write the word and feel a fool — eaten. A blade is missing from the lower gallery. The princess was seen, by a guard since transferred for his nerves, "biting at jewellery." These two facts cannot both bear weight. Either the steel was not consumed — and then where is it? — or it was — and then everything I know of the substance is wrong. I find I cannot hold both halves of the thought at once. The framework I have built across six years to contain this child has just, very quietly, come apart in my hands. I have stopped requesting more parchment. I am requesting leave.*

⁂

The court could not stop talking about how beautiful the princess had become.

It was true, and it was uncanny. Six years old and the breakthrough had remade her — skin like the inside of a shell, hair pale as poured silver, a stillness and a symmetry to her that made grown courtiers trail off mid-sentence and forget what they'd been saying. The most beautiful child in the realm, they said, and meant it, and felt obscurely frightened as they said it, because it was *too much*, it was Valyrian perfection past anything the line had bred in three generations. Her mother was, for the first time, simply and uncomplicatedly *proud* — a beautiful daughter, a normal thing, a thing a mother could enjoy without dread — and Alicent let herself enjoy it for almost a whole season before the curdle set in.

Gael, naturally, misread the attention entirely. She knew the breakthrough had remade her body; she'd done it on purpose; the beauty was just the surface readout of a refined core. What worried her was *concealment*. A leap that big put out a signature; a rival cultivator would feel it, would come sniffing for the disciple who'd jumped a realm overnight. She watched the court for the rival, armed against cultivation-espionage, and entirely failed to identify the actual attention she was drawing, which was not a rival sensing qi. It was a man.

Prince Daemon had begun to circle.

He was much older and he was charming and he found reasons to be where the beautiful small princess was — a hand on her shoulder that stayed a beat too long, a smile pitched just for her, a habit of telling her how *special* she was, how rare, how the blood they shared set them apart from common things. To anyone watching with clear eyes it was plain and it was sick, an older man's regard settling on a child, and the court watched with clear eyes and looked away and said nothing, because he was a prince, and the realm did not see fit to make trouble over the way a prince looked at a girl.

Gael watched him with cultivation-poisoned eyes and saw a sect weirdo.

She had run the obvious reading and discarded it. *Romance?* — she nearly laughed. Enemy-to-lovers, with a *cousin*, two of the most overused tropes in the genre stacked on top of each other; absolutely not; she had a do-not-do list and that was at the top of it. So she crossed off romance and was left with a residue she had no clean trope for — a creepy, hovering, over-fond elder who kept cornering her to murmur about bloodline — and an evil sect, she reasoned, was simply *full* of weirdos. Half the elders in any decent novel were unsettling. She filed him under harmless background cult-flavour and stopped watching him as a threat.

Worse — and this is the part the court would not have believed of a child, and the reader could not unsee — she started *using* him. An indulgent, important elder who sought out her company was access. Was cover. Was a door held open. She leveraged his interest with the bland competence of a junior employee managing a senior stakeholder, smiling up at the man circling her, asking her small clever questions, letting him feel special so that he'd keep being useful, and never once — not for an instant — clocking what she was actually standing inside of. A six-year-old cheerfully exploiting a predator's regard as a resource, because she'd filed the predator under *sect weirdo* and moved on.

And it paid. Charmed, indulgent, he began to bring her things — a string of small gifts across the months, old Valyrian trinkets, a worn finger-ring, a melted-looking pendant, a hair-pin black and dense. Baubles to him. To her: *reagents*. Every one of them sang the dragon-note; every one was a compatible source she could draw down at her leisure; one made a fine focus for meditation. She pocketed them, delighted, and angled for more, reading a grooming pattern as a standing supply line and the supply line as patronage — *the favour of the evil sect, the gifts a rising disciple attracts.* Exactly like the king's audience two years gone, she was pleased and not wary. The same lens. The same beautiful blind spot. The sect-weirdo was a free material stream, and she meant to keep him producing.

The whispered name had spread, too — *Dragonbane*, on more lips now, in lower voices — and she misheard that exactly as she misheard everything. *My dao-name,* she thought, a little pleased; *my cultivation reputation precedes me; a fearsome epithet, well earned.* She added it to the collection with the others and never caught the thing the realm actually meant by it, the dread under it, the way they were not calling her a feared cultivator at all but something else, something they whispered in temples, a word she'd have rejected on genre principle if she'd ever heard it spoken plain.

The only person who'd reported the truth — the guard who'd seen her gnawing the heirloom, who'd said plainly that the princess was *biting the jewellery* — had been laughed at, and then, when he wouldn't stop saying it, quietly transferred somewhere his nerves wouldn't trouble the household. Two for two, the Cassandra. Right again. Believed by no one again. He went where they sent him, and the framework that should have caught a child eating Valyrian steel walked out the gate behind him, and the maester upstairs put his face in his hands and requested leave.

## I.5 · The Bond-Breaker

The dagger took the better part of a night to file down, and Gael had nearly finished — the king's heirloom blade reduced to a glittering pile of dragon-steel shavings she'd swallowed one pinch at a time — when Prince Daemon found her alone in the empty gallery and gave her, at last, a real lesson.

Some servants would answer for the dagger. She knew that abstractly, the way she knew the sun would rise; a thing went missing from the king's rooms, a thing always answered for it, and it was never her. She'd stopped finding this remarkable. She filed the dagger under *consumed* and the eventual blamed servants under nothing at all, and was sweeping the last shavings into her palm when she heard him in the doorway and turned, and there he was, the sect-weirdo, the supply line, smiling down at her with a light in his eyes she'd never bothered to read.

He'd been drinking. He came in close — too close, in her space, crouching down to her level so that she could smell the wine — and he started in on the old refrain, but warmer tonight, hungrier, his voice gone low and intimate. How *special* she was. How rare. How the blood of old Valyria ran in the two of them and in almost no one else now, how the dragonlords had kept that blood pure and close *for a reason*, how a thing as rare as what they shared should not be wasted on common notions of — and his hand was on her arm, and his face was very near hers, and what he was doing was unmistakable to anyone in the world except the one person it was happening to.

Gael's genre-brain lit up like a struck match.

*Bloodline-resonant partners. Shared rare blood. Circulating together.* He was — she could barely believe her luck — he was offering her *dual cultivation*. The real thing, the technique from the higher manuals: two compatible cores, blood-matched, drawing and circulating qi between them to break through faster than either could alone. Platonic, of course — she had no interest in the romance-trope nonsense and this plainly wasn't that, this was *technique*, an elder finally sharing the advanced art she'd earned through patient patronage. She had been waiting for this since the cold king failed to hand her a manual. The sect-weirdo, of all people, was coming through.

She was so delighted she ran it then and there.

She reached for his core the way the higher manuals described — found the resonant thing in him, the bright living current that matched her own dragon-note, the thing she'd been feeling off him for months and mistaking for the source of his trinkets' power — and she opened herself and *drew*, the way you draw on a willing partner, nine revolutions, take and circulate and give back.

She took. She did not give back. There was nothing in her built to give back; she had only ever learned to draw.

What she had her grip on was not a cultivation core. It was his bond — the living tether that ran out of the man and across the city to the great red dragon that was half his soul — and she pulled on it the way she pulled on everything, hard, greedy, all at once, and it did not circulate. It *tore*.

It came loose into her like a riptide. Years of another life poured up the broken tether and into her belly in the space of a few breaths, an infusion so vast it dwarfed the sword, dwarfed the poison, dwarfed everything, and the wall inside her went again, a second breakthrough on the heels of the first, and she sat down in it, glowing, *roaring* inside, the richest she had ever been.

Daemon made a sound she didn't have a category for and folded down onto the gallery floor.

And across the Keep, with no wound and no warning, a half-mile from any living soul, Caraxes went mad.

She glimpsed it through the gallery's high window, drawn by the noise — the great red dragon thrashing on its chain-yard far below, screaming, beating its head against the stone, the dragonkeepers swarming out with chains and goads and terror in every line of them, hauling the maddened thing down by main force as it raked the air and shrieked at nothing. It was a horror. It would not stop. They would chain it in the dark before the night was out, and it would never be ridden again, and the men who'd raised it from the egg would lie awake the rest of their lives over the hour their sweet beast simply turned.

Gael watched it for a moment through the glass, mildly.

*A kindred spirit-beast,* she filed, *lost to a qi-deviation.* Pity, in a clinical sort of way — spirit-beasts were kin, worth a grave bestiary entry, and she'd give it one in the reverent hand she reserved for beasts and reagents. Then she looked down at Daemon, slack and hollowed on the floor, breathing but gone somehow behind the eyes, and she filed *him* too, in the flat plain hand she reserved for people: *an elder who lost his cultivation base.* A stat-change. Recoverable, in-genre, more or less. She felt a detached, distant pity, and underneath it — she audited herself honestly and found it there — a small clean relief, because the predator she'd never named had stopped circling. She gave herself a ⑥ credit for restraint: she'd only run the technique the once.

She did not flinch. There was nothing in her that flinched. The reader, watching her sit in a maddened dragon's screaming and a gutted man's silence and feel *neither*, carried all of it, the whole weight, alone — which is precisely how the gallery was built.

She had a problem, and she addressed it with characteristic focus. *Was that the demonic path, or not?* She genuinely could not tell. The technique had worked — gloriously, enormously, she was rich beyond reckoning now — but it had *cost* the partner everything, which was the textbook signature of the demonic drain, the forbidden art that steals what it cannot share. She did not feel guilty. Guilt did not enter. She felt a methodological uncertainty, and she resolved it the way she resolved everything: she booked a flat rule. *That may have been demonic. Therefore I will not use that technique again, on principle.* The line went where it always went — at the *method*, never the *harm*. She had not done a wrong thing; she had possibly used a wrong tool, and a righteous cultivator did not use wrong tools, and so by declining to do it twice she filed herself, with perfect serenity, among the good ones. She did not give back the gains. One did not give back gains. She'd keep the power and swear off the path that bought it, and call the keeping clean — the doctrine the whole rest of her life would run on, drawn flat and white in the gallery floor while a man breathed shallowly beside her and a dragon screamed itself hoarse across the city.

⁂

*To the Citadel — and I no longer expect to be believed, so I write for the record only. Caraxes is mad. No wound. No fever. No mark on him the keepers can find. Sweet-tempered an hour before; gone wild and chained within it; he will not be ridden again. The same hour, in the same Keep, the prince Daemon was found insensible on a gallery floor and is now near bed-ridden, hollowed, his fire gone out of him, no physician able to say why. And the same hour the princess Gael — "Dragonbane," the smallfolk are calling her now, and they do not mean it kindly, and I am beginning to wonder whether they are simply right — was alone in that gallery and is entirely well. I cannot prove a thing. I cannot even shape an accusation; there is no blade, no poison, no wound, nothing a man could put in a writ. I have asked that she be confined to her chambers. I told the Queen it was for the child's protection. I told myself it was to study her. The truth is I am afraid of what walks loose between her and a dragon, and I want a locked door between it and the rest of us until I understand it. I will not understand it. More parchment.*

⁂

The realm, which could not make a writ, made a story instead.

It made several. A locked-room theft with no thief, no blade, a timeline that seemed to *shrink* the harder they looked at it, drove the investigators to a phantom master-criminal of impossible subtlety; they hunted a ghost. The dragonkeepers, finding no illness and no injury in a beast that had simply *turned*, chained Caraxes in the deep dark and slept badly and theorised about bad water, bad meat, a hidden wasting sickness, anything. The court at large preferred a conspiracy worthy of the catastrophe — a rival House, a slow poison, a Faceless Man bought with Lannister gold — anything but the truth, which was four feet tall and confined to her chambers and mildly, exclusively preoccupied with whether the technique she'd run, *specifically the technique*, had been demonic. Not the dragon. Certainly not the man. Two stars, she thought of the dagger investigation, distantly offended at how sloppy it was, how she hadn't even had to engineer the cover-up. The scandal-burying machinery of a great House laundered the whole night down into *the beast took ill* before the week was out, and the executed servants who answered for the filed-down dagger went into the dark and never troubled her journal at all.

The confinement, she received as a gift.

She'd resolved on closed-door meditation to diagnose herself — to sit with the enormous new fire and work out, at leisure, whether she'd deviated onto the demonic path — and the maester, terrified, walked up and *granted her exactly that*, locking her in her own chambers for fear of a contagion that turned dragons feral. She read the locked door as the caring sect granting a promising disciple secluded-cultivation space to recover from a qi-deviation; more evil-sect favour, exactly when she needed it. He read the locked door as a quarantine on something that drove great beasts mad. They got, both of them, precisely what they wanted, and were both, as ever, completely wrong about the other.

And shut in, for the first time in her life, she found she had company she could not avoid.

The maids. Wylla chief among them — fourteen now, sent to tend the strange confined princess through her "illness," carrying the trays, covering the long hours of silent meditation, asking no questions about why the girl never seemed to sleep or tire. And Gael, with for once nothing to steal and nowhere to climb and no heist to run, *attended* to her. Found, to her own faint surprise, that the asset was a person, dry and watchful and brave, who smuggled in the things Gael was starved for — harbour gossip, ship-talk, what a coin did down at the docks, the names of the captains and the cargoes. The enforced closeness warmed the bond hard, in a way no scheme of Gael's would ever have allowed; she'd never have *chosen* to sit still long enough to like someone. The chambermaids who waited on her began, in those shut-in weeks, to orbit her too, half from duty and half from the strange pull of her. The first real fuel went onto the slow fire that would, in the end, make Wylla hers entirely.

Gael, of course, did not know she was being warmed toward. She knew she was reclassifying an asset. The journal, opened on her knee in the locked room, recorded a grave and reverent entry for Caraxes — *kindred, deviated, lost* — and not one word for the prince she'd hollowed, who'd brought her trinkets across a winter and pressed his special blood on her and been *literalised into ruin* for it, his supply line and his menace both ended in an afternoon by a child who had never once read him as a man. The favour was repaid in full. She had no idea there had been a favour, or a debt, or a man.

She closed the journal. She sat down in the centre of the warm fire that another life had paid for. She drew nine slow revolutions, and addressed herself, with serene scholarly diligence, to the only question in the world that interested her: whether she might, possibly, have done something a little bit demonic.

She concluded, after long meditation, that on a technicality she had not.

## Interlude · Alicent

Alicent had stopped going into her daughter's chambers without warning, and the morning she understood *why* she had stopped was the morning she finally let herself be afraid.

She'd meant only to look in. The maester's quarantine was three weeks old; the locked door had become a thing the whole Keep stepped quietly past; and a mother had a right to look in on a sick child. So she'd had it opened, soft, expecting to find the girl asleep, or feverish, or fretful — expecting, she realised afterward, to find a *child*. And instead Gael had been sitting bolt upright in the centre of the cold floor in the grey before dawn, open-eyed, perfectly still, breathing so slow and shallow that for one stopped heartbeat Alicent had thought her dead — and the girl had turned her head, and her eyes had not quite tracked, had taken a half-second too long to find her mother's face, and in that half-second Alicent had looked at her own daughter and not known what she was looking at.

She'd smiled, and said something about breakfast, and gone out, and stood in the corridor with her hand against the cold stone until her heart slowed.

*What is my daughter?*

She had been asking it for six years and dressing it up each time as something gentler. *She's contemplative.* (A babe who lay open-eyed like a little corpse practising; she'd shouted at the nurse who said it and then dreamed it for a month.) *A refined palate, a scholar's curiosity.* (A child who ate a poison that should have killed a horse and grew rosy on it.) *She's strong* — oh, she'd loved saying *she's strong*, she'd built a whole tenderness on it, her brave clever girl who killed a dragon in her cradle and lived. She had armed that cradle herself. That was the thing she could not get past, in the corridor, with her hand on the stone. *She* had fought the King for the egg. Jaehaerys had said no — no dragon for that one, a girl, the youngest, not worth the dragonkeepers' trouble — and Alicent had wept and argued and won, had put the gift in the cradle with her own hands because she could not bear her daughter to be the one princess without a dragon. And four days later the hatchling was dead and the babe was changed, and somewhere in the marrow of her, in a place with no words, Alicent had known ever since that *she had done it.* That whatever her daughter was becoming, she, the loving mother, had handed it the first rung.

So she loved her, and she was losing her to something she could not name, and she did the only thing left to a woman who could neither understand the danger nor turn from the child: she stood between them. She made herself a shield, and she fought a quiet war on two fronts that her daughter would never know was being fought at all.

The first front was her husband.

Jaehaerys had filed Gael *worthless* years ago and had not revised it since — a strange, cold, troublesome girl, no use to the House, a thing to be disposed of cleanly — and now, after the maddened dragon and the gutted prince, a worse word had begun to move behind his teeth. He did not say it to Alicent. He didn't have to. She heard it in the new flatness of his voice when the child came up, in the way his eyes went to her and away: *kinslayer.* A blighter of the blood. A thing that turned dragons feral and left princes hollow and might, left to grow, turn its hand to worse. And the King's answer to a thing he could neither use nor trust was the King's answer to everything — *price it, sell it, be rid of it.* He meant to wring her one last worth before he let her go: parade the uncanny jewel a while, inflate the legend, drive up the match, and then marry her off, far away, to whoever paid most and lived furthest, and call the realm well rid. A betrothal was coming. Alicent could feel the clock of it starting. She worked him — a wife's whole leverage spent across a hundred small conversations — not to *stop* it, she could not stop it, but to keep the sentence a *marriage* and not the colder thing the new word in his mouth implied. She bought her daughter's life one room at a time, and Gael, behind her locked door, drawing her serene revolutions, never knew her mother was bargaining for it.

The second front was the realm's mind.

Septon Barth — Hand of the King, the cleverest man in Westeros, the one mind in all the world equipped to look at the evidence and *theorise* what her daughter truly was — had been circling closer for years, half-charmed and half-troubled, reading the maester's increasingly frantic letters with a seriousness no one else granted them. He was one fact short. He was always one fact short; the substance that could not be eaten, the wound that left no mark, the immunity that read identical to a blessing — every theory he built collapsed at the edge, and Alicent labored to keep it collapsing. She redirected his curiosity into safer channels. She let the maester's wildest letters go *astray* before they climbed the chain to him. She made her daughter *boring on parchment* — a pious, sickly, studious girl, nothing to write home about — so that the one man who might have caught her would stay forever a fact short of the truth.

But the thing she guarded hardest, the thing she spent herself most to keep out, was not the King's word or the Hand's theory. It was the *whispers*.

Because the realm had made up its own mind, and its mind was the strangest thing of all. The smallfolk and the septons and the old women in the temples had taken the same facts the King filed as *kinslayer* and the maester filed as *contagion*, and read them backward into wonder. A dragon driven mad with no wound and chained in the dark. A prince hollowed and never the same. The most beautiful child the Valyrian line had bred in living memory. A name — *Dragonbane* — that they did not say as a curse anymore but as an *omen*. And out of all of it the realm had quietly, gobsmackedly crowned her: not a blighter but a *promised one*, blood-of-the-dragon incarnate, a prophesied figure whispered about in temples, an omen-child come for some great turning of the wheel. The court reeled at the spectacle of a King throwing away — selling cheap to a northern nobody — a girl the realm was beginning, in the same breath, to revere and to dread.

Alicent knew about the whispers. She heard them everywhere now, under the ordinary noise of the Keep, and she made it her whole work that they would never, ever reach her daughter.

Half of it was protection, and she knew the cold arithmetic of it exactly. A *revered* child is a *used* child — a banner, a weapon, a thing factions kill over and kill for. A *feared* child is a *dead* child, the moment fear outweighs awe. Either way the whisper that reached Gael was the whisper that ended her, and so the whisper would not reach her; Alicent would see to it; she would stand in the door of every rumour and turn it back. And half of it — she was honest with herself, in the grey dawns, because there was no one else to be honest with — half of it was not protection at all. Half of it was that she could not *bear* it. Could not bear for her strange still daughter to hear the realm call her a legend while her own mother still, desperately, needed her to be a *daughter*. A daughter you could fail to understand and love anyway. A legend you could only lose.

So she sealed it. All of it. The three readings of her daughter drifted past one another in the Keep that winter and never once touched — Gael behind her door filing herself a side-character prize quietly plotting not to be won; the King filing her worthless and pricing the sale; the realm haloing her as the prophesied one — three castings, every one of them wrong, and the woman who stood at the center of all three the only person alive who saw all three at once and could correct none of them. She was the seal. The asymmetry held because she held it. The legend grew without the girl, the King's contempt grew without the girl, and the girl grew on inside her own delusion, serene and oblivious and dangerous, building a world with no room in it for any of them.

Alicent won her small war. She would go on winning it, room by room, for years. The betrothal stayed a betrothal and not the colder thing. Barth stayed a fact short. The new word stayed behind the King's teeth. She kept the noise out and the door shut and the legend sealed, and she did it for love, knowing — half-knowing, the way she half-knew everything about this child — that the thing she was shielding so fiercely was becoming something the realm would one day be right to fear.

And she lost the only thing that had ever been the point. Because the daughter was already gone. Not to the betrothal, not yet — to the locked room, the slow revolutions, the world being built behind those eyes that took a half-second too long to find her face. Pulling inward, sealing herself the way her mother was sealing the realm out, leaving no room. Alicent would shield her all the way to the end. She would spend herself to the last and never be thanked, never be known, never once be seen as the shield — and when Gael finally went, slipped the cage and sailed out of the story written for her, she would go without ever learning that the one camera in all the world that loved her most, and saw her most clearly, was the one that had stood in the door her whole life and kept the truth from reaching her.

The mother looked in clear-eyed and saw a monster, and loved it anyway, and shut the door softly so as not to wake it.

"She's only contemplative," Alicent told the maester in the corridor, with a smile she had practised for six years. "A philosopher. You'll see."

She believed it the way you believe a thing you have decided to die rather than stop believing.

Behind the door, the philosopher drew nine slow revolutions, and grew.

# BOOK II — *the blind machine*

## II.1 · The Recluse

⁂

Wylla had been told the princess wanted harbor records, which was the first strange thing, and that she wanted them brought to the solar and not the library, which was the second, and that she wanted them at dawn, which Wylla privately thought was a courtesy to no one. She carried the rolls up the cold stair with her breath smoking and her arms aching and arrived to find the strange one already awake, sitting cross-legged on the floor in a square of grey light with her spine very straight, doing nothing at all.

"You can put them there," said Gael, without opening her eyes.

Wylla put them there. She was sixteen and the princess was six, and by every law of the world this should have been the simplest arrangement in the Keep — a bedmaid for a small girl — except that the small girl frightened the older maids so badly they had begun trading away their shifts, and so the duty had landed, the way unwanted duties do, on the one with the least standing to refuse it. Wylla's father was a landed knight three days' ride from anywhere that mattered. She had been sent to court to be useful and to be seen, and instead she had been given a child who collected the customs-manifests of Lannisport the way other children collected ribbons, and who was looking at her now with the flat, patient interest of a much older person deciding whether you were going to be a problem.

"Sit," said Gael. "I'll explain the realm to you. You've earned it."

Wylla had not asked to have the realm explained to her. She sat.

What followed she would never afterward be able to repeat, though she tried, once, to a septa, and got halfway through *spirit-roots* and *the nine revolutions* and *the demonic sect that runs this court* before the septa's face closed like a door and Wylla learned not to. The princess spoke of the King's court as an evil cultivation order that had reincarnated her — Wylla did not know that word and Gael did not slow to teach it — into the body of its least-favored disciple, and of the egg she had eaten as a relic, and of a thing called *qi* that she drew into her belly in the mornings, nine turns, which was the doing-nothing Wylla had walked in on. She said all of it pleasantly, the way you'd describe the weather to a guest, and none of it as though she expected to be believed, which somehow made it worse.

"The cells beneath us," Gael said, pointing down through the floor with one small finger, "are where they take the disciples who fail. The black cells. A righteous sect would have a discipline hall and a redemption trial. This one just has a hole." She said it with the dispassionate approval of a critic noting a competent if uninspired choice. "Derivative, but it sets the tone. Two stars. You'll have seen them take people down there."

Wylla had. A scullion last winter, for a theft he had not done. He had not come back up. She had not, until this moment, let herself put the matter so plainly, and she found that hearing a six-year-old put it plainly was not the same as thinking it, and was much worse.

"Yes," Wylla said. Her voice did something strange in the middle of the word.

"Don't fret over it." Gael had noticed the strange thing the voice did and misread it, the way she misread everything, as squeamishness rather than grief. "Mortals are always going down the hole. It's the whole point of a hole. The trick is to not be a mortal." She tilted her head, considering Wylla with that flat patient interest. "I haven't decided about you yet."

*She is mad,* Wylla thought, with great clarity, *and she is mine, and there is no one I can tell.*

And then the princess opened her eyes, and they were that pale violet the Valyrians had, the color the whole court had started lowering its voice around, and she said, "You don't believe a word. That's correct. You shouldn't. You're a mortal; this is above your station. But you're *my* mortal now, and I keep my people fed." She said it kindly. Wylla felt the bottom drop very gently out of her stomach, because no one had ever offered to keep her, and she understood in that moment, with a horror she could not have named, that she was going to be loyal to this child, and that it was already too late to choose otherwise.

⁂

The court assembled to look at her, and Gael let them.

She had been instructed — by Otto, through the King, through a chamberlain who would not meet her eye — to stand upon the dais, to be silent, to say nothing whatever, and above all to remain still. She found the instruction delightful. A whole hall of the evil sect's elders had been gathered to honor their prized jade-disciple, and they had even, thoughtfully, forbidden her to break her meditation by speaking. She drew the morning's warmth down into her belly, nine revolutions, standing perfectly upright in cloth-of-silver with her hands folded, and was so visibly serene that a knight near the doors crossed himself.

This, she understood, was a *display*. The sect was advertising her. She did not begrudge it. A radiant disciple was a credit to her order, and an order that had spent six years and a Valyrian egg on her cultivation was entitled to show off the return. She knew herself beautiful now — the glow-up had come on in the last year, the silver hair, the unsettling skin, the eyes — and she read the beauty exactly as she read everything: a power-level readout, a number gone up. She preened at it the way you'd preen at a clean cauldron. Then she remembered that none of it mattered, because she was leaving — bailing out of this whole derivative court-intrigue plot before its true protagonist arrived and slaughtered the lot of them, the way protagonists did — and the preening folded back into boredom, and the boredom looked, to the watching hall, exactly like the contempt of something not quite human.

The procession went by her in slow tides. Lords she would never learn the names of bowed to the depth their rank required and watched her over the bow for any flicker of warmth, and found none, and went away murmuring. A Reach girl her own age was brought up to stand near her a moment, for contrast or for luck, and stared at Gael's eyes until her nurse drew her back. A septon blessed the air a careful arm's length from her. None of it reached her. She had set herself a standing meditation — feet rooted, weight even, the warmth coming down nine turns and pooling low — and the hall full of staring elders served only to deepen it, because it is easier to sit still inside a held breath than inside an empty room, and the silence-rule meant no one would jar her out of the count. *A gift,* she thought, serenely. *They've given me a hall to cultivate in and called it a duty. Considerate, for a wicked sect.*

The King regarded his daughter from the high seat and saw, with cold satisfaction, a jewel that would price out very well in the North.

And then there was a commotion near the western arch, and a man's voice, too loud, denouncing her.

Gael did not, properly, know Prince Daemon, except as a sect-weirdo with useful gifts who had once gotten too close and come away maddened — she had filed the incident flat and kept the power and never thought of it again. She was therefore mildly interested to see him now, thinner than she remembered and shouting, while the lords on either side of him stepped not toward him but very slightly away, the way you step away from a man who has begun to smell.

"*Demon,*" Daemon was saying, pointing at her with a hand that was not quite steady. "Look at it and tell me you don't see it. Maegi. Sorceress. That is no child of this house — "

A title.

Gael received it the way a dry sponge receives water. *Maegi.* She turned it over. *Sorceress.* She had collected *Dragonbane* and *Poison-Eater,* and they were fine, they were respectable, but this — *Maegi* had a *dao* in it, a whole secret-arts lineage, the kind of name an immortal earned over centuries and a mortal only got to fear. A diminished elder of the sect, evidently jealous, had just elevated her in front of the whole order, and from the way the others were laughing behind their hands at *him* and not at *her,* the elevation had landed. She inclined her head a precise quarter-inch in his direction — *gratitude, junior to senior* — which made him scream something else, and she filed *Maegi / Sorceress* under earned titles, current rank, and went back to her revolutions.

Across the hall her mother watched the whole thing and felt the floor of her composure crack.

Alicent had told herself the parade was harmless. She had told herself the reading and the harbor-rolls were a mercy — *she's taken up scholarship, thank the Mother, a seemly thing, a quiet thing* — and she had believed it for almost a week. But she watched the court drink her daughter in, watched the very reverence she had spent six years smothering swell up again off the dais like heat off a stone, and she understood two things at once and could fix neither. The first was that the King was not protecting Gael by showing her; he was *selling* her, and the showing was the price-tag. The second was worse, and arrived as she was already turning to go: that she could not take her daughter away from any of it. She had dreamed, lately, of Dragonstone — of the salt and the quiet and her strange girl safe behind black walls. But a princess belonged to the King, not the Queen. The one door out had been a painted door all along, and the wall behind it did not move.

By nightfall the kitchens had it three different ways. *The mad prince called her a demon to the King's own face,* a scullion told a potboy, *and she only smiled.* — *Smiled?* — *Smiled, and bowed to him, like he'd paid her a compliment. That's the wrong of it. A natural child would weep. She thanked him.* The potboy crossed himself with a greasy hand, and word of the smile and the bow went out through the under-Keep faster than the parade itself had gone through the hall, and where the lords had seen a discredited man embarrass himself, the servants — who were never wrong about these things, and were wrong about this — saw a girl receive the word *demon* as her due. The maegi-rumor, which Daemon had meant as a weapon, set in the city's mouth like a tooth, and began to do, quietly, for free, the intimidating that Gael would otherwise have had to do herself.

Daemon was being escorted from the hall, still talking. Gael, eyes shut, drew the warmth down nine more turns and decided that the new title would look very well in the bestiary.

## II.2 · The House

The counting-house of the man they called Hobb the Salt — no relation to her Hobb, the still-room boy, though Gael did not know either of them by surname and would have cared about neither — had three guards on the door, a dog in the yard, and a strongbox sunk in the floor under the moneylender's own bed, and Gael walked out of it before dawn with the better part of its contents and an opinion that the formation-array protecting the vault had been, frankly, derivative.

She had drugged the dog from the well-bucket and the guards from the wineskin they shared at the changing of the watch, both doses out of the still-room, both measured to read the next morning as ordinary heavy sleep — the way the manuals never bothered to teach you, because the manuals assumed you'd just be strong. A six-year-old in a servant's brown shift was the most invisible thing in King's Landing; she had walked in through a coal-hatch a grown thief could not have used and that no grown thief would have thought to look for, which was, she reflected, rather the point. The novels were full of master infiltrators who were always somehow *large.* It had never occurred to any of them to simply be a child.

She had read the strongbox's wards — the false bottom, the trip-bar, the second box inside the first — as a third-rate sealing-formation laid down by a competent but uninspired array-master, and she had taken it apart kneeling on the floor in the dark with a pin and a wire and the patience of someone who had four hundred lifetimes of bad novels and one of real chemistry behind her eyes. The trip-bar she found by the faint wrongness in how the lid sat; the false bottom by tapping; the inner box she simply lifted out whole and opened later, at her leisure, in her own rooms, because there was no reason to do delicate work in a dead man's bedroom when you could do it at a desk. The poor disciple robs the wicked sect to fund her ascent. It was practically a *requirement* of the genre. That the wicked sect in this instance was a crime syndicate, the only one in King's Landing organized enough to be worth the trouble, and that a robbed smuggler-boss could no more report his losses than fly, she registered only as the sect being conveniently weak in its mortal protections. That she had, in the same stroke, decapitated her only competition before she had even named her enterprise — that was not a thing she thought about, because she did not think she had competition. She had a *Dao.*

The Faith's coffers she took a week later, and felt even less. The septons and septas she had known all her life as the court's tiresome rival order — an orthodox sect, righteous-pathed, forever pressing its inferior scriptures on better cultivators — and reclaiming a war-chest from an inferior-path rival raised no flag in the moral self-audit she ran, carefully, the way she ran all of them. *Am I robbing the innocent?* No: a rival sect. *Am I taking from the weak?* No: from an order with more gold than the crown. She cleared herself on both counts and was mildly gratified, as she always was, to find herself blameless. The Faith, for its part, discovered the theft, calculated what it would cost in standing to admit that the Seven had let themselves be robbed, and buried the loss so deep that no inquisitor was ever sent. Gael had counted on exactly this and called it the sect's pride. The realm called it nothing, because the realm never knew.

With the founding capital in hand she transmitted the Dao of Games and Fortune.

There was, she had noticed, a *vacuum* in King's Landing — drink, whores, the bear-pits, and nothing for a man with coin who wanted his blood up and his purse at risk in a clean, repeatable way. The city was simply too young to have invented the gaming-hall, and Gael, sole living heir to the half-remembered formats of four hundred read-and-forgotten novels, brought them down out of the air like a patriarch transmitting a lost art. She set the house-edge herself, with the only real mathematics anyone in Westeros had ever applied to the problem, and she explained it to Wylla once, gravely, as a cosmological law: *the house is fate, and fate does not lose.* She built a stable of fighting-beasts and doped and conditioned them out of the still-room, reading each animal's vigor the way she read a reagent, and she set the chambermaids — Pella, Nona, the timid pious one, Marra — to walk the floor and take the coin, because a princess could not, and a noble lady-in-waiting could not, but a clever commoner could, and the buyers would think they dealt with her and never look higher.

It worked the way she said fate worked. The house took its edge off every wager, patiently, the way the sea takes a cliff, and within two moons the converted cellar under a dead-end wynd in Flea Bottom was turning more coin in a night than the bear-pits turned in a week, and men who had never in their lives had a clean way to ruin themselves found one, and were grateful. Gael watched the numbers come up the channel to her in Wylla's careful hand and felt the deep contentment of a law confirmed by experiment. It did not lose.

And then there was the matter of the skimmer.

His name was not important and Gael did not learn it. He was a man Wylla had taken on to keep the dice-tables honest, and he had been quietly bleeding the count for a month, a little off each night, the way a man does when he has decided the operation is too strange and too profitable to ever come looking. Wylla brought it to the princess the way she brought everything now, in a low voice in the solar, expecting — she realized later she did not know what she had been expecting. A reprimand for the man. A docking. A frightening word.

Gael listened. She was sitting at the window with a chart across her knees, and she did not look up from it.

"How much," she said.

Wylla told her — it was not, in the scheme of the take, very much, and she said so, half hoping the smallness of it would make the whole thing go away. "A few stags a night. He thinks no one counts. I only caught it because the table totals ran sweet against the door totals three weeks running."

"You count beautifully," said Gael, with genuine warmth, and Wylla felt the compliment land and was ashamed of how much she wanted it. "That's exactly right. The door total is fate; the table total is the man's hand in the bowl. Good." She made a small mark on her chart. "Outer disciples need discipline. Don't trouble yourself further with it."

"What does that — " Wylla began, and then did not finish, because she had already learned, somewhere underneath where she let herself know things, that she did not want the sentence completed. "Yes, princess."

There was one man in the Keep who saw the shape of it and said so, and was, as ever, not believed. Hobb — the still-room boy from the old days, a watchman now, who had twice seen the truth of the princess and twice been called a liar for it — caught sight of her one grey dawn coming back in through the coal-hatch in a servant's brown shift, small and certain and entirely where no princess should be, and he stood very still in the shadow of the buttery and did not call out, because he had learned what calling out cost him. He told one man, later, a friend, over ale: *I saw the princess crawl out of a coal-hole at dawn like a thief.* The friend laughed at him. *The boy who cried demon,* the friend said, not unkindly, and bought him another cup, and that was the end of it. Hobb stopped telling people things. It was the only lesson the Keep had ever managed to teach him.

The man was dead within three days. The wrong tea, taken at the wrong table; a soft word from a smallfolk girl who did not know what she was carrying or why. He simply stopped coming.

⁂

It was Wylla who confirmed it, and Wylla who carried the confirming up to the solar, and stood in the doorway afterward and understood that she was looking at a corpse.

Not a threat. Not a rival cowed, not an enforcer leaned on and sent away grey — those she had begun, God help her, to find ordinary. A *body.* A grown man who three days ago had been a few stags a night in a ledger and was now nothing at all, gone out of the world, and the child who had ended him had not raised her voice or her hand or even, visibly, her interest. The princess had looked up from her chart when Wylla said it, with mild distraction, and remarked only that she hoped the body had not been left somewhere troublesome — the novels never told you what to do with the bodies, she said, and she found the logistics tedious — and gone back to her work. *Put out by the mess.* Wylla felt the horror go through her clean and cold, and underneath it, worse, the first dreadful drop of understanding: this was why the floor obeyed. This was why grown men took orders from a maid half their age. The unseen thing behind the maid would, if you crossed it, simply remove you from the world, blithely, between one line of accounts and the next, and be mildly annoyed about the tidying. *Ask nicely* worked because the alternative had now been demonstrated.

"Was that necessary," Wylla heard herself say, before she could stop the words.

Gael considered it as a genuine question, which Wylla found, somehow, more frightening than if she had brushed it off. "He was stealing," the princess said, reasonably. "It's Tuesday. Bring me the Braavos manifest when you've a moment — the one with the bent corner. I think the road south is opening." And she smiled, the small private smile she gave no one else in the world, the one that meant *junior sister, inner disciple, mine.* And Wylla — to her own lasting horror, kneeling that night by her cot trying to pray and finding the words would not come — discovered that it still warmed her, that the smile of a child who had a man poisoned over a few stags still reached into her and turned something over, and that whatever was happening to her, whatever this girl was making of her, it was already, quietly, much too late to undo.

⁂

Gael, for her part, did not give the man another thought, and brought her own attention happily back to the road south.

The whole structure ran, Gael had arranged without quite intending to, on three people being wrong about who was in charge. The men who came to wager and lost thought they dealt with a clever commoner — one of the chambermaids, Pella or Nona, the sharp-eyed girls who walked the floor and took the coin and seemed, to a drunk losing his purse, plainly to be the brains of so slick an operation. Wylla, the noble lady-in-waiting who never showed her face below-stairs, thought she was the keeper of the cleverest mind in Westeros, and quietly held the apparatus together in service of it. And Gael thought she was running a sect's tribute-branch, the mortal limb of a great immortal order, attended by an inner disciple and a swarm of outer-disciple servants beneath her notice. Every one of them was wrong about who ran it, and the not-knowing was the strongest wall in the whole edifice, and it printed money. A noble lady cannot work a gaming-floor and a princess cannot be seen near one, and so the smallfolk were the hands and the lady was the manager and the child was the invisible source, and no man who lost his house at the dice would ever, in a thousand years, raise his eyes high enough to find her.

Across the city, in a gallery above the gaming-floor where he had no business being, a man Otto Hightower paid to watch things watched the chambermaids take the coin, and could not for his life make the count come out, and went away to report that the strangeness around the little princess had a shape now, even if he could not yet say what it was — not the coin itself, he told his master, but the *girl,* there was something wrong about the girl, and his master, who had been waiting years for someone to say exactly that, set down his cup and asked him to go on.

## II.3 · The Betrothal

The Stark envoy was a broad grey man named Cregan-of-some-holdfast — Gael did not retain the holdfast — and he stood in the King's solar with snow still melting off his shoulders and looked at her the way a smith looks at iron, and she liked him better than anyone she had met at court, because he at least understood that this was a transaction.

"The Warden's heir," Jaehaerys was saying, "is a man of two-and-twenty, sound in body, blooded against wildlings — "

"We've heard the tales of her," the northman said, over the King, which no one did, and which made the chamberlain by the door go still. He was looking at Gael, not at Jaehaerys. "The dead dragon. The bonded prince unmade. The lady throttled — that's newer; word reached us on the road." He did not say it as an objection. He said it the way a buyer recites the points of a horse he has decided to take. "Soft houses run from it. We don't run from hard weather, in the North. We breed it back in." He inclined his head a precise degree toward the King, and a precise degree more toward Gael, and Gael, meditating, inclined hers exactly the same — *senior to junior, between allied sects* — which the northman took, correctly, for the only honest greeting he had gotten in the south, and which warmed him to her, fatally, for both their sakes.

Gael, forbidden again to speak, meditated.

She had assembled the situation from the harbor-rolls and the gossip and the maps before anyone troubled to tell her, the way she assembled everything. The sect was selling her north. The North was the frozen enemy ice-sect — Winterfell its mountain stronghold, the Wall its great formation, and beyond the Wall a demon-horde the southern orders only whispered of — and they meant to hand her over as a sacrifice-bride to seal an alliance, which was a stock arrangement she had read a hundred times and found, in execution, beneath the dignity of the trope. The groom did not interest her. He was a destination, not a person: a cold place that would snuff her furnace, where the radiant matter she needed could not be sourced, where she would be watched by a whole rival order. *Hard pass.* She was not afraid of him and she was not afraid of the wedding, because she had read enough to know exactly how this went wrong — you did not *jilt* a great sect, did not make a senior order *lose face,* or you became the villain of their hundred-year revenge arc. You vanished clean. You let them think you'd died or fled or been taken by a spirit, and you went to cultivate outside the plot entirely, where no one's face was at stake. The wedding, to her, had quietly become a delivery deadline.

Her mother was not so calm.

Alicent had come to fight the match and discovered there was nothing to fight. She could not say a Great House was beneath them; it was not. She could not say the North was unsafe; the North was, by the realm's lights, the safest place a difficult daughter could be sent. Every objection she reached for dissolved into the one thing she could not say in the King's solar, which was *she is mine and I am losing her and I do not even understand what I am losing anymore.* So her objection collapsed, in the end, into grief, and grief has no standing at a betrothal.

She withdrew to Dragonstone. There was nothing else to withdraw to. Defeated on the match and defeated, more terribly, on the daughter she could no longer read, she chose at least to be defeated behind black walls and the sound of the sea, alone — the refuge she had dreamed of bringing Gael to, fled to without her, because a princess belongs to the King.

They had their goodbye in the solar Alicent had used as a girl.

"I won't be far," Alicent lied, holding her daughter's face in both hands, and her daughter looked back at her with that patient violet calm, and Alicent searched it for the child she had armed with an egg and could not find her. "You'll write. You'll tell me you're — you'll tell me how you fare. Promise me. Whatever happens with — " She could not finish. *With the marriage. With the North. With whatever you are.* "Promise me, my love."

"Of course," said Gael, who was already three-quarters into the reagent-map behind her eyes, and was finding this sentimental and overlong, the way send-offs in the novels always were — a senior leaving the sect for seclusion, a tedious obligatory beat before the plot resumed. She let her mother hold her face. It cost nothing and seemed to be wanted. *Braavos for the resin,* she was thinking. *The Summer Isles for the bark. The road south, the warm road, glows the whole way down.*

"You were always — " Alicent's voice failed and tried again. "When you were small, before any of it, before the egg, you would grip my finger so hard. As though you knew me. I have wondered since whether — " She stopped. There was no end to the sentence that she could bear to reach, and her daughter was watching her with that calm, kind, bottomless attention that gave her nothing back, and Alicent understood, finally, fully, that she was saying goodbye to someone who had already left, years ago, in the cradle, and left no forwarding word. She pressed her lips to Gael's forehead. The skin was cool and faintly wrong under her mouth, as it had been for years, and she had taught herself not to notice, and noticed now. "Be safe. Whatever you are. Be safe, and let me believe you're safe, that's all I — that's all."

"Travel safely, Mother," said Gael, and meant it, in the way one means a wish for a colleague one will not see again; and the moment her mother released her face she was back inside the chart, tracing the warm road south.

Alicent left for Dragonstone the next morning and wept the length of the bay, and Gael, who had filed the entire goodbye under *elder departs for closed-door seclusion, low priority,* did not see her go.

Behind her, the table she had lit did not stay one table.

She had built a gaming-hall and stepped back, and the vacuum and the seed-capital and the house-edge no rival could match had taken it and *run* — dice into protection, protection into territory, territory into laundries and loan-sharks and a quiet retainer paid to the right men of the watch. Factors recruited factors three tiers below her sightline. King's Landing, which had never had a true crime-lord, acquired one without noticing, and the crime-lord was a seven-year-old who registered the whole sprawling racket only as a vague administrative *largeness,* tedious, the worldly branch rendering more tribute than was interesting. It ran behind a faceless name the men themselves invented to fear — the Quiet Coin — and the coin poured off the books, three layers of paper deep, toward reagents in the south and a hull not yet ordered.

And because a lone lady-in-waiting will not be taken seriously by violent men who smell weakness, the master came down to back her. Gael slipped out, more than once, to stand at Wylla's shoulder in rooms where men had begun to test her — and made them stop. No ceremony, no qi, no raised voice: a poison hinted at, a debt called, the maegi-legend doing half the work before she opened her mouth. A challenger came in certain and went out grey, and never could say afterward exactly what had been threatened, only that he would not be going back. It was, Wylla understood — watching her tiny mistress discuss the maiming of an enforcer in the same voice she used to order the laundry — a rehearsal. By the time real danger reached Wylla herself, removal would already be comfortable. Casual. She held the whole apparatus in her two hands and felt the size of it on her shoulders, and felt how much of it now rested on her alone, and was afraid in a way she could no longer afford to be.

And out in the gangs and the gold cloaks, from the bodies that did not appear and the rivals who simply stopped and the money that moved like smoke, men began to assemble a portrait of the power behind it all — a brilliant, invisible, untouchable crime-lord, patient and bloodless, who left no trace and made no demand and could not be found. They gave the bogey a name, the way frightened men do, to make it a thing they could at least speak of: the Quiet Coin. They feared it accurately and entirely, and it was a seven-year-old and her maid, and the very faithlessness of the legend made it a perfect decoy, a monstrous shape they would chase forever in exactly the wrong direction, nowhere near a dais.

Out in the city, too, the first real human wreckage surfaced — a lord ruined at the tables past his means and turned out of his own hall, a debtor who borrowed against the next harvest and then did not come home, men sunk into a grip on the doped fighting-pits they could not name and could not climb out of. Gael caught only the faint edge of it: a souring in the city's air, the first thin whisper of a *curse,* which she read, in passing, as some far-off evil-sect aura beginning to gather — a thing to watch, later, when she had the time. She filed it and thought no more of it. She did not connect it to herself. It had not yet occurred to her, and would not for a while, that there was anything to connect.

The maester, two floors below, wrote a letter.

⁂

*— to the Conclave, with my continued perplexity. The Princess Gael, lately betrothed to the North, has of her own will requested charts of the southern trade — Braavos, Lys, the Summer Isles, the Asshai'i routes — and studies them, her septa assures me, for hours. I am told it is geography, a young lady improving her mind ahead of a long journey. I find I am charmed. And yet I write at this hour, and cannot say why, save that a child betrothed to Winterfell ought, surely, to be studying the snows of the North, and is instead tracing with her small finger every warm port that lies in the opposite direction. It is nothing. I am tired. I remain your servant, &c.*

## II.4 · The Overheard Word

She had not meant to pass the music-room at all; the corridor to the still-room ran the other way, and she took the long route only because she had heard there was a new shipment of Myrish glass in the western stores and wanted to see whether any of it was the clear kind that took an etched array. So it was chance — pure, stupid, genre-flavored chance, the kind the novels used to spring exactly this sort of thing — that put her at the door while the ladies were talking.

There were four of them at their needlework, and they were talking about her the way the court always talked about her: at a careful one remove, not cruel, not warm, the cadence of women discussing a thing rather than a person. The strange princess. The one no one quite warmed to. They had moved on to her hopelessness at the courtly graces — the dancing-master had despaired of her, it seemed, the way the harp-master had despaired of her, the way her embroidery would shame a girl half her age — and a lady Gael knew, a recurring face named Lady Jeyne Rosby who had pinched her cheek once and called her *poppet,* sighed over her stitching and said, half-laughing, not unkindly, *"No talent there, that one. None at all. Talentless."*

Gael caught one word of it.

She had come up the corridor hearing nothing, and arrived in the doorway in time for *talentless,* fixed to her name, in a woman's idle voice. And the word did not mean *dancing* to her, because Gael did not have the frame in which it could mean dancing. *No talent* was the one verdict her cosmology could not absorb. It meant *no spiritual root.* It meant a void where a foundation should be. It meant the negation of her entire path, the single unsurvivable sentence a cultivator could be handed, the one thing that was not fearsome and so could not be reframed as a title — only as erasure, the claim that she was *nothing,* that her four hundred lives and her egg and her revolutions and her radiant climbing self were a void someone could simply name in passing over needlework.

She crossed the room in the time it took Lady Rosby to draw her next breath.

There was no warning and no heat in it. Her body was tempered and it moved fast and flat, and her small hands were at the lady's throat before anyone in the room had registered that the princess was in the room, and she bore down with a strength no eight-year-old has, a cold mechanical correction of an intolerable error, and she did not stop in time. She did not, in truth, stop at all; she was *stopped* — by the woman going limp, by the wet ruined sound, by the matter being, to her, simply over. She let go. Lady Rosby slid to the floor with her hands at her own neck and a noise coming out of her that would never again be a voice, the cartilage crushed, the throat ruined for the rest of her life, and the other three ladies were screaming now, and Gael straightened her sleeves.

The matter was closed. The error was corrected. She turned and walked out, already back inside the reagent-ledger, mildly puzzled at the noise behind her — the novels were always so loud about these things — and gave it no further thought, because to her nothing unusual had happened. Someone had spoken an impossibility over her root, and she had answered it, proportionately, and moved on.

The reader holds four things at once, and none of them touch each other: that the ladies had meant a dancing-lesson; that Gael had heard a death-sentence on her soul; that she had half-killed a familiar, harmless woman over a trifle; and that she had no idea, walking placidly back to her glass, that anything had occurred.

Lady Rosby kept her ruined throat. She was at court still, after, a marked face the whole Keep learned to fall silent around — the woman the maegi-princess had nearly killed for no reason any of them could find. They invented reasons. A curse spoken. An ill omen given. The truth — that there had been no reason they could see, because the trigger was buried in a cosmology none of them shared — was unbearable, so the *maegi* rumor hardened another notch instead, which suited everyone except Lady Rosby, who could no longer say so.

And the King's grandfather, who had been waiting for exactly such an opening, took it.

Otto Hightower could not name what was wrong with the princess, but he knew an unwomanly, dangerous child who throttled ladies at court was a problem with a price, and with the Queen gone to Dragonstone there was no one left to shield her. He went to the King with the language of correction. *The girl wants discipline. She wants the dangerous notions taken out of her.* And he opened, as such men do, with the easiest lever to hand: her allowance was cut. The gift her mother had given her, now undefended, was simply trimmed away, a princess's pocket-coin docked to teach her humility.

It was the funniest thing that had happened to Gael in a year, though she did not laugh, because she rarely did. They had docked the pocket-money of a girl who privately bankrolled the city's underworld and was three layers of paper deep into buying a *ship.* She noted the cut with a faint, genuine amusement: that they imagined a princess's power was the coin a man chose to grant her, that they thought they could starve a river by capping a cup. *They can only conceive my strength as my allowance.* She let them take it. It cost her nothing she needed, and a humility-trial endured serenely only burnished her, as the next one would.

The clampdown had, in fact, only begun. But that was for later.

Three nights after the music-room, a man came over her windowsill with a knife.

She did not wake, because she did not need to. Daemon's coin had bought a competent killer — a real one, a man who had done this work before and would have done it cleanly on any other child in the Seven Kingdoms. He came down the wall on a knotted line in the dead black before dawn, eased the shutter, crossed the floor without a sound, and stood over the sleeping princess and found, as such men are trained to find, the soft place beneath the ribs where one push of good steel ends a small life. He drove the blade down with a professional's economy, his whole weight behind it.

The point met her skin and stopped.

He felt it the way you feel a chisel meet a knot — the give that should have come and did not — and he leaned harder, baffled, and the steel skated sideways off something that was not bone and not armor and not anything a sleeping eight-year-old had any business being, and took a bright notch out of its own edge, and the girl breathed slow and even and did not so much as turn over. The man crouched in the dark over a ruined knife and a child who had not woken, and his nerve, which had carried him over the wall, simply left him. He went back up the line and over the wall and was three streets away before he let himself shake. He never told Daemon the truth of it, only that the thing was done or could not be, he was never sure which he said. In the morning Gael found a long scratch on her window-frame and a thread of grey wool snagged on the latch, and frowned at it for the space of one breath, and filed it — if she filed it at all — under a clumsy servant and a careless ladder, and went down to her revolutions. She never knew there had been an attempt. The discredited prince's voice had made him a joke; his knife made him a villain; and a girl who never once looked his way absorbed the villainy as serenely as the joke, because both of them, in the end, simply slid off.

Word of the throttling traveled north, and landed *well.* A girl who answered an idle word with cold and instant lethality was hard stock, dangerous stock, exactly the iron the North respected. The flash that would have repelled any southern suitor confirmed, for Winterfell, the asset. And the King, hearing of both the maiming and the failed murder in the same week, resolved with fresh urgency to marry the trouble far away, and fast — the sale he had closed becoming, by the day, a disposal he wanted *closed.*

Wylla had seen it from the doorway. She had been bringing a copied manifest up the corridor and had arrived in time for the worst of it — the crossing of the room that was too fast to be a child's, the small hands, the sound, the slow boneless slide of Lady Rosby to the floor with that ruined noise coming out of her. And then the princess had straightened her sleeves and walked out, and her eyes had passed over Wylla in the doorway with no more weight than they passed over the wall, already gone back into the ledger, mildly puzzled at the screaming. *Was that one of ours?* the look seemed to ask, of the woman dying on the floor. *No. Then.*

Wylla went and was sick in the privy, quietly, and knelt there a while after with her forehead against the cold stone, doing the arithmetic she could not stop doing now. The lady had said a word about *dancing.* That was all. There had been no warning and no quarrel and no debt and no theft; there had only been a word, and the word had been the wrong word, and a familiar woman who had once called the princess *poppet* would carry a crushed throat to her grave for it. Wylla understood, kneeling there, that she now lived inside a thing she could not predict — that all her careful management of the operation's *real* dangers was beside the point, because the true danger had no rule she could learn, only a single buried tripwire no one could see, and her whole survival, and the survival of everyone below her, depended on never once stepping where she could not tell the wire was laid. She got up. She rinsed her mouth. She went back to work, because there was no one she could tell, and the manifests still needed sorting, and the dice still needed counting, and the strange terrible child she had bound her life to would want the Braavos figures before the bells.

## II.5 · The Compromise

The face in the gallery had begun to repeat.

Gael noticed it the way she noticed everything she was not meant to — a man she did not know appearing twice in places a man like that had no reason to be, and then, more interestingly, Marra. The timid pious chambermaid had gone strange and skittish, would not meet her eye, flinched at the solar door; and the small things in Gael's rooms had begun, very slightly, to move. A journal a finger's width from where she'd left it. The harbor-rolls re-stacked by a hand that was not Wylla's. She was being watched, closely, by someone patient, and the watching had reached inside.

She had been waiting for this for two years, and her whole genre-poisoned heart rose up to meet it.

"It's a *hidden test,*" she told Wylla, with shining certainty, pacing the solar. "A reclusive master — an expert, a true immortal, someone who has *sensed* my talent — has set an unseen trial. This is the part where they watch in secret to see if I'm worthy. The diligence, the discretion, the dao-heart under pressure. And if I pass — Wylla, if I pass — there's a legacy. A secret discipleship. A fated encounter. I've read this a *hundred* times." Her eyes were luminous. "I have to *perform.*"

Wylla put her face in her hands.

She had watched the operation's real danger gather all book — the exposed chambermaids, the notoriety, the wreckage in the city, the watchers Gael was now mistaking for *destiny* — and she had no way to say any of it that the princess would hear, because the princess had a frame for everything and the frame was always wrong and always serene. *It is not a hidden master,* Wylla wanted to say. *It is a real man with real malice and he has gotten to one of the girls, and you are about to perform for him.* She said none of it. She had learned that the frame did not bend.

And Gael performed.

She did not hide from the surveillance; she *auditioned* for it. She rose earlier, sat longer, walked the corridors with her eyes lowered in a study of modest diligence she had read described in a dozen novels and now reproduced with a craftsman's care. She dressed a decoy diary as her audition piece — pious reflections, dutiful copywork, *Today I considered the virtue of patience; a disciple's first foundation is her stillness* — and left it where a watcher's hand would find it, and felt rather pleased with the touch. "He'll read humility there," she told Wylla, tapping the decoy. "Masters always test for humility first. It's the gate before the real teaching."

"And if it is not a master," Wylla said, very carefully, because she had decided to try, once, plainly, while there was still time. "If it is only a man. A real man, with a knife or a writ, who has gotten to one of the girls and is reading everything you set out."

Gael considered this with the indulgent patience she reserved for Wylla's frights. "Then he's a fool, and he'll learn nothing, and the master who *is* watching will see how I handle a fool. Either way I pass." She smiled. "Stop worrying. This is the good part. This is the part the whole arc is *for.*"

There was nothing to say to that, and so Wylla said nothing, and watched her tiny mistress, as the fatal flourish, leave her *real* journal proudly on display. The bestiary. Because she had never once, in eight years, conceived of it as a vulnerability. It was *proof.* It was the evidence of her merit, the record of her ascent, exactly the thing a hidden master would want to read before extending a legacy, and she wanted it read. She would never hide the one document that told the truth about her, because in her mind it told only the truth that she was magnificent.

The hand that moved Gael's papers belonged to Marra, and Marra did not know she was a thief. Otto had not threatened her — he was far too clever for that. He had come to her as a kindly patron, a great lord taking a fatherly interest, and had sat with the timid pious girl and spoken gently of his worry for the young princess: so strange, so friendless, surrounded by people who might wish her ill, in need of someone watchful to keep her from harm. And he had persuaded Marra, who loved the princess in her frightened dutiful way, that to tell him what the household did was not betrayal but *protection* — for the princess's own safety, for the good of the coming marriage. So Marra told him things, and went home each night believing she had done a small kindness, and slept easier for it, and never once understood that she was the leak, or that the leak would in time doom her, that she was a smallfolk hand set to hold a risk that would be spent first and spent whole. The kindly lord had a gift for finding the one frightened soul in any room and making her the instrument of her own ruin, and calling it care, and being thanked for it.

It was through Marra's dutiful, loving, doomed hands that Otto's man got the bestiary, and took it down to Otto.

And Otto did not recoil. Where Alicent would have broken — would have found, partway down, *Mother: sentimental, useful for egg-procurement, low threat* — and where Barth, had he gotten it, would have brushed a cosmology and missed the girl by one unreadable step, Otto read the bestiary the way he read everything: for the weapon in it. He did not care that humans were logged by function. He cared that one of them, a single entry, had climbed. *Asset (hands)* crossed out, climbing, line over line, to a *name.*

Wylla.

He understood it instantly and coldly: that this name was the one lever that moved an unmovable girl. And more — that this was the noble who held the secret things, the lady-in-waiting who tied the whole invisible apparatus together, the trade and the off-books vice and the Quiet Coin, the single human seam in a nameless empire. The one crack. He had been digging for dirt on a wildcard princess to bank with the heir-faction — with Baelon and young Viserys, who already blamed the maegi for the maddened dragon — and instead he had found the load-bearing point of something far larger than he had imagined, and he had found the maid who was its keystone, and he began, patiently, to plan.

Gael, meanwhile, was *enjoying* her test.

Her own exposure she shrugged off entirely. When she gathered, from the pattern of the intrusions, that the watcher had read her journal, she was briefly, genuinely *offended* — they'd filed her a mere *poisoner,* apparently, when she was so obviously a *cultivator* — and then she set the offense aside, because the examiner's misjudgment was part of the trial. She kept performing. She felt the test was going well. She never once clocked Marra as the channel, because she was not hunting a turned maid; she was hunting a phantom hidden-master, and a phantom does not have a face you can catch.

But the instant the lever resolved into Wylla, the breeze stopped.

It came to her sideways, the way real things did. She was working through the pattern of the intrusions — which papers had been touched, which left, in what order — and somewhere in the middle of it she understood not a fact about herself but a fact about Wylla: that anyone who had read the bestiary now knew there was exactly one entry in it that was a name, and that a careful enemy would draw from one name the one conclusion that happened to be true. That Wylla was the lever. That there was, in the whole nameless invisible empire, a single human seam, and that someone had just found it, and that the someone could now reach the one thing in her flat-filed life that she could not file flat and walk away from.

She set the papers down. She did not know yet that the watcher was Otto, did not know it was Marra, did not know the shape of the threat at all — only its target. And something in her, which had been breezy through betrothal and clampdown and a knife she had never woken for, went quiet. Cold. Precise. She was not afraid — she was never afraid; fear was a mortal's reflex and she had ascended past it — but the wide serene attention she carried over everything narrowed, all at once, to one hard point, and stayed there. She told herself, carefully, in the language she trusted, that this was a *compromised channel that must be closed.* A logistics problem. A vulnerability in the apparatus, to be excised before it could be turned. She believed it as she thought it. The reader saw what she could not: that this was the single thing in the world she was not carefree about, that the cold was not the cold of accounting but the cold of a creature whose one soft place has just been touched, and that she had, calmly, between two thoughts, in the language of logistics, *decided.*

The decision was made there. The deed would wait. But it was made.

Out in the city the curse she could not see was thickening — the ruined lord, the vanished debtor, the addicts, a faceless power *draining* the realm — and Gael heard every word of it and read, with grave concern, a rival demonic sect at its soul-work, some dark formation laid over King's Landing that she ought to guard the people against. She ran the self-audit. *Am I the villain doing this? No — surely some other evil sect.* She was hunting herself across the city with real diligence and never caught the scent, because the scent was her own. The notoriety that frightened her was the same notoriety that had drawn Otto's eye; her blind spot and his opening were one fact, seen from two sides.

And then the cage closed the rest of the way.

The allowance had gone at II.4; now Otto finished the work. With the Queen at Dragonstone and no shield left, he convinced the King to *correct* the dangerous child in full: her tutors dismissed, her library stripped — no more unwomanly books, no more correspondence — and septa training imposed in their place, proper piety, proper accomplishments. The realm meant it as discipline. It landed two ways, neither intended. The book-and-tutor strip was the cut that actually stung, because the library *was* her intelligence service — the harbor records, the trade logs, the raw feed of the reagent-map — and she had to quietly reroute the whole flow of information through other hands. But the septa indoctrination she reframed whole and serene: the Faith was a stuffy rival orthodox sect pressing its righteous dogma, and the dreary catechism was *obviously* part of the hidden master's test, a humility-trial to prove her dao-heart's steadiness. So she sat in Septa Aldith's bare cold cell with her hands folded and her spine straight and let the woman read her the Seven-Pointed Star by the hour, and asked, in her grave clear voice, exactly the questions a worthy prospect would ask under examination. "And the Crone carries the lantern," she said once, "to light the path through the dark. That is the wisdom-aspect, then — the elder who tests the disciple's heart before granting the lamp." Septa Aldith, who had braced herself for a demon and been handed a catechumen of frightening focus, felt her heart lift with a vanity she would have called holy joy. The princess collected the scripture the way she collected everything — as an inferior-path text to be studied and dismissed, the dogma of a stuffy rival order filed for tactical reference — and hid her real gut-revulsion so completely behind the reframe that Aldith never once glimpsed it. The septa watched the maegi-child sit so meekly under instruction, hour upon hour, and took it for a miracle of her own working — *I have turned the demon to the Light* — claimed the credit up and down the Keep, and let the *saintly princess* rumor swell up beside the *demon child* one until the court could no longer tell which of the two it feared, only that it ought to keep feeling both at once.

The correction meant to humble her had burnished her legend instead, on both faces at once. And underneath all of it, where no audit reached, sat the one cold decided thing about Wylla, waiting for the deed.

## II.6 · The Yield

The squeeze that was meant to break her closed on nothing, because there had never been anything there to break.

This was the thing Otto could not have guessed and would never learn. He had built the clampdown as a siege — cut the supply, starve the strange power of whatever fed it, and watch the dangerous child weaken into something a man could manage. It was a sound plan against any creature that ran on what it took in. It simply did not describe Gael. Throttling her allowance and stripping her stores and choking her court-supply changed nothing she needed to *live,* because she did not run on supply. The egg had been a reactor, not a battery. She had generated her own qi since the cradle, a slow inexhaustible warmth she drew down nine turns each morning, the well refilling itself faster than any morning could empty it, and no man capping her purse could touch the furnace under her ribs. The siege closed on a fortress with its own spring inside the walls.

The empire had never been about survival. This was the part she clocked only as a faint, footnoted inefficiency, and the reader was left to hold the weight of: that she could have sat in a stripped bare room with every book and coin and reagent in the realm taken from her, and gone on existing, indefinitely, faintly bored, never an hour from death no matter what they did. The treasury raid, the Faith's coffers, the doped beasts and the ruined lords and the man with the wrong tea and the five who were coming — none of it had ever stood between her and dying. None of it was need. It was all *advancement,* every theft and every body bought to climb a wall she could have simply chosen never to climb, and to buy a door out of a cage that could not, in the end, have killed her if she'd stayed.

The empire was about *advancement.* And here, at last, it paid.

The trade and the off-books vice-coin finally reached past the Keep, past the city, past the realm — and brought back the far-sourced radiant matter the court could never source. The packages came up the channel disguised as anything but what they were: a Lysene resin in a dye-merchant's crate, a Summer-Isle bark folded into a bolt of cloth, and the prize, last and smallest, a black stone the size of a thumb-joint that had come off an Asshai'i hull through six hands and that hummed against her palm, when she finally held it alone at midnight, the way the egg had hummed in the crib.

She prepared the yield over three nights in the still-room with the door barred — grinding, decocting, layering the three sources the way the manuals laid out the forging of a foundation-pill, the real chemistry of her last life braided with the genre-craft of the four hundred before it. On the third night she swallowed it and sat down cross-legged on the cold stone floor and began the revolutions, and somewhere past the hundredth a wall she had been pressing against for a year, patiently, with nothing to widen it, simply *gave.* She felt her foundation spread outward into new room. She felt the whole apparatus of crime and theft and risk *vindicated* in a single bright turn — every robbery, every leaned-on man, every body in a ledger had bought *real power,* measurable, hers, climbing — and the same coin that bought it would buy the ship, the road paid for at last, advancement and escape both purchased with one long outflow of stolen gold. She sat in the dark with the new room ringing in her and was, for a moment, perfectly happy.

And yet — she clocked it only as a faint inefficiency, a footnote she did not read — none of it had ever been *needed.* Her base self-generated. She had never been an hour from death without any of the empire; the whole vice-network, the crimes, the bodies had bought her advancement and her exit and nothing she required to keep breathing. The dark note did not fall on the racket's dead men; they were furniture, and she would no more mourn a spent minion than a spent candle. It fell where she could not see it fall: on the one mortal she had drawn into all of it, the maid pulled into a criminal empire and pointed, though neither of them yet knew it, toward blood — for a survival-panic that had never had any ground at all.

Then, with the first true surplus, she did the thing that gave her away, and did not notice she had done it.

She spent it on Wylla.

⁂

Wylla had not been told what was in the cup, only that she should drink it, that it would be unpleasant, and that the princess would stay with her through the night. She had learned, by now, the precise weight of *the princess will stay with her* — it was the weight of a thing being done that mattered, because Gael did not sit up with people for trifles — and so she drank it, and the warmth went down into her like a swallowed coal, and spread.

For a while it was only strange. Heat under the skin, a glow she could see faintly through her own wrist when she held it to the candle, a feeling of her body being *read* and slowly, carefully *rewritten,* line by line, the way Gael read everything. And then, past midnight, it went wrong. The warmth became a fever; the fever became a furnace; she was burning from the inside and shaking and her bones felt as though they were being unmade, and she heard her own voice making sounds she did not choose, and she thought, with strange clarity, through the white roar of it, *I am going to die, and she will write a clean line in the book and find someone new.*

She did not die. Somewhere in the worst of it there were small cool hands on her face and a voice — flat, certain, not frightened, *never* frightened — saying numbers, counting revolutions, talking her body down off the edge it had climbed to, and the furnace banked, and the fever broke, and toward dawn Wylla surfaced into a body that was not the one she had gone to sleep in. Stronger. Quieter. A heat banked low under everything. She lay in the grey light and looked at the ceiling and understood, the way she understood most true things now, sideways and without permission, that her princess had poured something into her that should not exist, had nearly killed her with it, and had stayed the whole night with cool hands to make sure she lived.

Some doors you do not open. Wylla did not ask what the cup had been. *A tonic for the voyage,* Gael had said, lightly, when the worst was past, and Wylla let it be a tonic for the voyage. She noted, in the days after, that she did not tire the way she had, that a winter fever swept the Keep and felled half the household and never touched her, that she slept less and saw more. She noted, too, the things she had been not-noticing for years — that the princess herself never ate much, never tired, sailed through every sickness untouched, was simply *other* in some fundamental way she had decided long ago not to look at directly. She decided, again, not to look. She had been nearly unmade and held together by the same two hands in a single night, and whatever that made the princess, it had also, unmistakably, been *for her,* and Wylla did not have it in her anymore to be only afraid.

⁂

Gael, for her part, filed it under *fortifying the asset.*

She had a useful junior-sister, an inner disciple who held the apparatus together, and it was simple prudence to harden a valuable mortal against the wear of the work — an investment in a reliable instrument, a line in the ledger between the resin and the bark. She ran the self-audit and cleared herself instantly: a master strengthens a worthy disciple; there is nothing here to examine. She did not register, because she had no frame in which she *could* register it, that the tempering had cost her real, hard-won, far-sourced power; that she had taken the first surplus of her ascent — the thing the whole bloody empire existed to produce — and spent it, not on her own advancement, not on the ship, but on a person. That she had sat up all night with cool hands counting another body's revolutions, frightened — she would have denied the word — that it might stop.

She saw a line in the ledger. The reader saw the rehearsal for the gift that would, one day, "save" Wylla, and the cost of it, and the one warm thing in the whole cold machine, filed as procurement.

Two floors down, the maester read the household accounts and the sick-lists by candle, and reached a conclusion he could neither prove nor say aloud: that the court had cut the strange princess off from every source that fed an ordinary body, and the princess had only grown stronger; that she did not run, could not be running, on what fed everyone else. He sat with it a long time. Then he drew a fresh sheet to the candle, and wrote, because writing was the one thing he could still do with a truth no one would believe.

⁂

*— to Septon Barth, privately, and not for the Conclave. My lord, I will set down a thing and trust your discretion with it, for I can trust it to no one nearer. The Princess Gael has these six months past been cut off, by the King's own order, from her tutors, her library, her correspondence, and the better part of her allowance. A girl so stripped should pine. The pox that took half the household this winter — I buried four of the under-servants — never touched her; she has not had so much as a chill. She eats, by the kitchens' account, almost nothing. She does not tire. I have weighed her on the apothecary's scale against her own measure of a year past and she has not lost an ounce. My lord, I am a maester and I deal in causes, and I can find no cause. A body that is fed less and works the same does not grow stronger. Hers does. I do not write you a theory. I write you a fact I cannot place, and the cold feeling it gives me at this hour, and the request that you tell no one I wrote it. Your servant, &c.*

⁂

He sealed it, and held the candle a moment longer over the small flame of his own dread, and then put it out, and went, at last, to bed.

## II.7 · The Vise

The wedding-clock, which had been a distant thing on the far side of two years, jumped — and Wylla was the one who brought Gael the news, because Wylla was the one who heard everything first now, and her hands were not quite steady on the letter.

"They've moved the date," she said. "The Stark party rides south in the spring. There's to be a — they're calling it a presentation, before. Midsummer." She set the letter down on the chart. "Princess. That's not two years. That's not even one."

Otto had turned the lever he had found. This was the elegance of it, from his side: having located the maid, he no longer needed to understand the empire at all. He had never made the count come out and he had stopped trying. He did not need to know what the strange princess was building, or how, or with what; he needed only to know that everything she did ran through a single noble girl, and that a single noble girl could be pressed — and the fastest, hardest press in his reach was the wedding itself, which leaned on the maid and the operation and the cage all at once, with the full weight of the crown behind it. He no longer needed to understand the empire. He needed only to squeeze the seam. He had gone to the King with the maiming and the failed murder and the maegi-rumors all bundled into one urgent argument — *close the disposal, marry the trouble north before it ripens into something the crown cannot answer for* — and the King, who wanted exactly this, accelerated the match. The distant deadline became a near one. The pressure came down the channel all at once. There were new faces watching the gaming-floor that the chambermaids did not know. A factor three tiers down was questioned by men who showed no badge and would not say who sent them. A bill that should have cleared in Braavos sat, unaccountably, *held.* It was the cage drawing tight from every side at once, and a betrothal Gael had filed as a delivery date for a delivery she did not intend to make was suddenly bearing down on her with months instead of years to run.

She clocked the cage. She did it minimally, breezily, without one gram of earned epiphany — sitting at the window with the held bill in one hand and the moved date in the other, while Wylla stood waiting for the storm and got, instead, a small thoughtful nod.

That the empire was hers in no way that counted — that any of it could be cut off, leaned on, leaked. That her body and her fate had been sold north and were not, in any sense the world recognized, her own. That even her secrets had leaked, that the bestiary had been read, that nothing inside these walls belonged to her. She registered all of it as *typical evil-sect business.* A master owns everything and lets a disciple keep nothing; that was simply how a wicked order ran, flat and unremarkable, no felt loss in it anywhere. She had read this exact arc a hundred times — the disciple who realizes, too late, that her cultivation resources and her freedom and her very person were never hers to begin with, that the sect lent them to her the way a moneylender lends, against a debt called in at the worst moment. The difference, she thought, with the mild satisfaction of a reader who has guessed the twist early, was that those disciples always *despaired* at this point, and she had read ahead, and knew the move that came next. And beneath the court she clocked the matching genre-fact: that the Targaryens were not, in truth, strong — strip away the dragons and you had a hall of soft mortals with a few tamed spirit-beasts and nothing here, *nothing,* worth a cultivator's staying for.

To *own* a thing — she reasoned it straight through, the way she reasoned the house-edge — you had to be *beyond reach.* This was the move the despairing disciples in the novels never made, because they were written by authors who needed them to suffer; they raged against the sect, or schemed within it, or knelt, and were crushed in their various ways, when the obvious play had been in front of them the whole time. You did not win the master's game. You did not lose it either. You *left the board.* And in the leaving, the two desires that had pulled against each other since the cradle — the hunger for endless radiant supply, and the hunger for a solitude no hand could reach into — which she had carried as a contradiction, two roads she could not walk at once, suddenly were not a contradiction at all. There was a single object in the world that was both roads. The answer arrived whole and obvious and aligned at last, and she nearly laughed, which she almost never did:

A ship.

A ship gave her infinite supply, the warm road south unrolling port by port forever, *and* absolute, unclaimable solitude, a hull no King and no sect and no grandfather could put a hand on. The two wants that had pulled against each other since the crib snapped into a single line. She stood up in the solar with the whole thing assembled behind her eyes, and the resolution — being Gael — arrived as comedy.

"I will forge a vessel," she announced to the empty afternoon and to Wylla, who was the only one there, "to bear me beyond the reach of this mortal realm."

Wylla put her face in her hands.

"A *ship,* Wylla. Don't you see?" She had stopped pacing and was holding the held Braavos bill up between two fingers like a specimen. "They can sell my body, they can cut my coin, they can read my journal, they can wall me into a hall full of weak mortals and marry me to an ice-sect — but they cannot own a thing that *isn't there.* You can't cut the allowance of a girl who isn't in the realm. You can't hold the bill of a buyer who's at sea. You can't marry a bride who has no shore. I will be *beyond reach.* Infinite supply, perfect solitude, the road south the whole way down. It is the *answer.* It has always been the answer; I simply hadn't drawn the formation correctly." She was luminous with it, eight years old and declaiming like a patriarch announcing his closed-door breakthrough. "We commission the finest vessel coin can buy. Off the books. Through you, three layers deep, course set south. And we sail out of the entire plot before the wedding bells finish ringing."

Wylla took her face out of her hands. "Princess. A ship is the single most-watched purchase a person can make. They have rolls of every keel laid in every yard from here to Braavos. The harbormaster's men climb aboard before the paint dries. You cannot — a *child* cannot quietly buy a *ship.*"

"Which is why," said Gael, serenely, "I have you." And she smiled, the small private one. "Three layers of paper, a phantom buyer, the Quiet Coin's own men, and a course filed for somewhere I am not going. The same way we hid a treasury and a syndicate and a curse over the whole city. We've done harder things than buy a boat, Wylla. We've done harder things this *month.*"

"They are moving the wedding *up,*" Wylla said, faintly.

"Then we build faster."

It was, Wylla thought, the most insane sentence she had ever heard, and she also knew, with the sinking certainty that had become the texture of her life, that they would in fact build faster, and that it would in fact work, because everything this child decided to do worked, blithely, against all the laws of the possible world. She went, the same afternoon, to begin finding a shipwright through enough intermediaries that no man in the chain would ever be able to say whose coin he had taken or where the hull was bound. Because the maid-squeeze obstructed at every turn now — Otto's pressure came down on the very channel she had to commission the ship *through,* the new faces, the held bills, the questioned factor — and the two countdowns, the hull and the wedding, had begun to run hard against each other, and somewhere in the closing gap between them, Wylla understood, walking the cold corridor with the commission already taking shape in her head, a thing was going to have to give. She did not yet let herself think about what.

It gave, in the end, the way Wylla had feared it would, and sooner. The pressure that came down through the channel did not stay on bills and watchers. Within a fortnight of the moved date the suitor's own people began to arrive — an advance party, ahead of the spring, to smooth the path for a wedding that could not be allowed to slip — and they were not careful men, and the smoothing of a path, to such men, meant finding whatever stood in it and leaning on it until it moved. Wylla felt them circling the operation, and then circling closer, and then — but that was the next chapter, and she did not yet know it would be her.

The household noted, in the days after Gael's strange declaration, that the princess had become eerily *calm* about her rushed wedding. She did not weep. She did not rage. She did not, as a difficult girl ought, have to be coaxed and corrected toward her duty; she had become, overnight, the most tractable bride in the Keep, sitting through the talk of dates and dowries and northern customs with a serene faraway attention that the matchmakers took for acceptance and the septa took for the final flowering of her piety and Alicent, in a letter from Dragonstone, took for a broken heart bravely borne. It was the eeriest misread of all of them. They were watching a girl be calm about a future, and not one of them guessed that the calm was the calm of someone who had read the future, found it not to her taste, and quietly decided not to attend it.

## II.8 · The Disappearance

They were five, and they were men, and they put their hands on Wylla in the lower passage where the gaming-coin came up through the household, and that was the mistake from which nothing else could be recalled.

They were the suitor's advance party — Otto-faction men, sent ahead of the rushed wedding to lean on the obstruction around it, and the obstruction, in their reading, was a lady-in-waiting who could be leaned on. So they leaned. A hand on her arm, a hand at her jaw, a voice telling her what would happen to her and to the people she answered to if the difficulties did not resolve themselves before the bells. Wylla went very still and very white and said nothing, and afterward could not have said how she got back up the stair, only that she did, and that she stood in the solar doorway with the marks already darkening on her arm and told the princess, in a flat careful voice, what had happened.

A prince's oblivious endangerment of a servant — that, Gael had no answer for; you cannot poison a man for failing to notice a maid. But this was different. These men had *meant* it, had reached deliberately for the one thing in the whole serene apparatus of her life that she could not file flat. These were addressable. So she addressed them.

Two of them had raised their flags without knowing it. The suitor himself, a heavy confident man, had said in the great hall, where a chambermaid heard it and it traveled up the channel to Gael, that *after the wedding's done I'll send for my own people and settle proper, a man wants his household about him.* And a sycophant in his train, a thin anxious factor doing the leaning he was told to do, had begged off a third night of it with the truest sentence a man can say: *I've only a family to feed, you understand, that's the whole of it, that's all I am.* Gael, who tracked such omens with religious care, noted both, filed both, and was not surprised when neither man was saved — the realm was a meat-grinder and proved her right about death-flags as reliably as it proved her right about everything else she was wrong about. She did not connect the omens to her own intention. She simply observed that flag-raisers died, and then made it so, and saw no seam between the watching and the doing.

It is not shown. The reader is given the before and the after and nothing in between: that the men were in the Keep, leaning, certain of their leverage; and then, across a handful of days, that they were not in the Keep, nor anywhere, that the suitor and his whole party of five-and-more had simply gone out of the world and left no body and no thread and no thief behind them. There was no drain, no qi, no demonic path — only paper, and the maester's own supply chain, and the still-room: a poison that read as a natural death, a bill of exchange that sent a man somewhere he never arrived, a quiet untraceable subtraction of human beings from the rolls of the living. They were here, and then they were not, and the hole they left was bigger than the men, and into it Otto would pour the rest of his year and find nothing.

The hole was bigger than the men in the other way too, the way no one in the Keep would ever add up. The thin factor's family did not know they had become a family with no one to feed them; the news of his vanishing reached them as a wage that stopped and a winter that came on regardless. The suitor's own household, a hundred leagues off, waited through the spring for a man who had gone south to seal a marriage and would now simply, inexplicably, never write again. The ruin spread out from the five of them in rings, into people Gael had never seen and would never count, dependents of the furniture she had quietly removed — and none of it appeared on any ledger she kept, because to her the matter had one line and the line was closed.

It was colder than the Daemon drain, because the Daemon drain had been a reflex and this was a plan. This she had decided at II.5 and built toward and executed on purpose, cleanly, the first death in eight years that she meant — not the casual swat of the skimmer she had never bothered to count, but a chosen, planned removal of human beings, undertaken with care, *for Wylla.* It was R2, and she did not feel it move under her at all.

She drew the line where she always drew it: at the technique, never the harm. Poison and paper, not the demonic path, so to her hands were *clean.* A channel closed. An asset protected. The books balanced. She told herself it was for the apparatus, for the operation, for the cold logistics of an empire that had grown a vulnerability and needed it excised. The reader, holding the ledger she would not, saw the truth she had filed away under a heading she would never read again: her first cold murders, planned and carried through, committed entirely to protect the one person in the world she would never, ever name as hers.

⁂

Wylla knew.

No one told her. She did not need telling. She had brought the princess five men's names and the bruise on her own arm, and then the men were gone, and she understood the arithmetic of it the way she understood everything now, sideways and complete. She had not lifted a hand. She had not moved a body — she would not have known how, and was not asked, and some animal part of her was grateful for the not-asking. She had crossed only into silence. Into knowing that five men were dead, and why, and by whose small careful hands, and saying nothing to anyone, ever.

She sat alone in the dark of the room she shared with no one and worked out what she was doing, because she found she could not do it without watching herself do it. She was choosing. That was the thing. It was not that she had been kept from telling, not that fear had stopped her mouth — she could have gone to a septa, to the maester, to the gold cloaks, could have ruined the whole impossible machine in an hour and walked free of it. The men had threatened her; she would have been believed. She did not go. She sat in the dark and felt the shape of the silence she was choosing, and she knew it was murder she was keeping, five men into the ground for laying hands on her, and she folded it carefully into herself and resolved to carry it, and the thing that made her able to was not fear.

It was that the princess had done it for *her.*

That was the horror Wylla could not get past and did not, in the end, want to get past. Not that her mistress was a killer — she had known that since the skimmer and the tea. But that her mistress, who did not love, who filed her own mother under *low threat,* who spent power on nothing but her own ascent, had spent murder on *Wylla,* coldly and immediately and without being asked, the moment Wylla was touched. No one had ever protected her in her life. Her father had sent her to court to be useful. And this strange terrible child had reached out and taken five men out of the world for putting a hand on her arm, and called it logistics, and would never once say it was love, and Wylla — God forgive her, she thought; God forgive her, there is no one to tell and I would not tell them if there were — chose the silence, and chose the child, and chose, in the dark, to stay.

She did not yet have the word for what she had become. She would find it in Book III, on a shore, with a knife. For now she only sat with it, and was quiet, and in the morning sorted the manifests, because the work went on.

⁂

With the obstruction gone, the commission was placed.

Through Wylla, paid out of the off-books dice-coin, three layers of paper deep, its course the reagent-map's warm road south — a hull ordered through enough intermediaries that no man who touched the coin could say whose it was or where the ship would carry her. *Nothing here is mine,* Gael had decided at the vise, and the sentence carried its one silent footnote, the thing she would have denied if anyone had been able to ask: *except Wylla.* The one possession she meant to take beyond the walls, filed, of course, as logistics — *transport of essential asset, voyage south.*

The cruel fold she never saw and the reader was left to assemble alone: that the vessel meant to carry Wylla out of all of it was being built through Wylla's own two hands.

Two floors below, Otto Hightower stood over the hole where five men had been and could not make it close. His vise had been meant to yield leverage; it had yielded a corpseless disappearance instead — no bodies, no thief, no demand, no error, only a suitor and his party gone clean out of the world and a wedding-clock now running against a thing he could not name. He probed it melodramatically and learned nothing, lost his men and gained no ground, and the larger the hole stayed the louder it became, until it was no longer an opening he could exploit but a lead he could not let go — the lead that would become, in the year ahead, a manhunt.

He had wanted to close his hand on a small girl. He had found instead that the hand closed on nothing, and that men who reached into the nothing did not come back, and he resolved, in the cold grandfatherly way of a man who has lost a piece and means to win the game, to find out where they had gone.

Up in the solar, Gael filed the whole affair under a heading and forgot it. *A vendor dispute,* she wrote, in the bestiary, in the small fair hand she used for things that did not matter, *resolved.* And she turned to the chart of the southern routes, and traced with one finger the warm road down, port by port, all the way to the open water where no one's face was at stake and no one's hand could reach, and thought how very *unlucky* it had been, for those men.

# BOOK III — *the narrow escape*

## III.1 · The Commission

The counting-house smelled of tallow and old ledgers and the river, and the shipwright's agent had a smudge of pitch under his thumbnail he kept worrying at, which Gael watched the way she watched everything — as data. He was a heavy, careful man with a clerk's stoop and an artisan's hands, and he had come up three flights of a fish-merchant's stair to meet a buyer he did not know, on behalf of a master who did not ask questions, to be paid in coin that came up so clean through so many hands that whose rain it was had stopped being a thing a sane man wondered.

He kept his eyes on the table, which Gael approved of. A grandmaster artificer ought to be incurious about the patron commissioning a transcendent vessel; the great forges of the old novels never asked why the cultivator wanted the cloud-skiff, only how much spirit-jade he could spare. This one only wanted gold, which was simpler, and which she had in unspeakable quantities behind three walls of paper. She had come to the meeting herself — not as herself, never as herself, but folded behind her maid like a second shadow, a small noble child brought along to a tedious errand, dressed plain, saying nothing a grown man would think to listen to.

"Tell your master," she said, "that the timber must be seasoned."

She was nine years old, sitting on a cushion that made her tall enough to see over the counting-house table, and she had said *tell your master* through Wylla, who said it again in the voice of a lady-in-waiting placing an order on behalf of a household that did not exist, which was itself a household on behalf of a Braavosi factor who had never been to Braavos, who was in fact a column of figures in a book only Gael could read. The agent heard a noble lady's maid. He did not hear the child on the cushion at all. Nobody ever did. That was the whole architecture of her life, and she had built it the way the manuals said to build a foundation — patiently, in courses, so that nothing above could ever find the bottom.

"Seasoned how long?" Wylla asked, which was Gael's question, passed down through her like water through a pipe.

The agent unrolled a corner of the drawings she'd had sent ahead and tapped them with the pitch-stained thumb, and his face did a complicated thing — half a builder's pleasure at a worthy problem, half a builder's despair at being handed one. "A hull of this size, this grain, fitted as the drawings ask — and the drawings ask for things I've not built in twenty years, things I'm not sure anyone's built. A double hull. Water stowed for — for a great while. Sealed lockers a man could live out of. Whoever drew this," he said, and Gael felt a small warm flush of pride that the agent would never see, because she had drawn it, "drew it for a voyage with no port at the end of it." He spread his hands. "Three years to lay her and four to finish her well. Closer to five if your master wants her to swim through a storm and not just a harbor."

Wylla's hand went still on the table. Gael felt the stillness beside her and understood it as her maid doing the courtly thing, absorbing a blow with grace, and was proud of her for it. Wylla was absorbing a different blow. *Years,* she was thinking. *Years, and the wedding is months. She doesn't even —* and then she made herself stop thinking it, the way she'd taught herself to stop thinking a great many things, and folded her hands, and waited.

Gael was not troubled in the least.

Of course it took years. You did not forge a natal treasure — a vessel meant to carry an immortal across the great water, provisioned forever, sealed against the world — over a single firing. The manuals were explicit. A low cauldron could be hammered out in a season; it cracked at the first real heat. A true vessel needed the long cycle: the slow accumulation, the tempering and re-tempering, the patience that separated the disciple who lived from the disciple who exploded in a ditch trying to skip a stage. Five years in the furnace was *correct.* It was, if anything, reassuring. It meant the artificer understood his craft. A man who promised the boat by autumn would be a man whose boat she should not trust her ascension to.

"Five years is acceptable," she had Wylla say.

The agent blinked. He had braced for an argument. Patrons always argued about time; time was the one thing gold couldn't buy, and rich men hated being told so. He had a whole speech ready about grain and curing and how a rushed hull drank its crew, and he never got to give it. This one's maid simply wrote *acceptable* in a tidy hand and pushed the bill of exchange across the table, and the bill cleared, and that was the end of the haggling that did not happen. He looked once at the small plain child standing at the lady's elbow — looked at her the way you'd glance at a stool, a thing in the room — and the child looked back at him with the flat patient attention of a person inspecting a kiln, and he felt, briefly and for no reason he could name, that he should go home and embrace his sons, and then the feeling passed and he rolled up the drawings and went down the three flights to the river to start finding seasoned oak.

⁂

*To the Conclave, from the Red Keep, the maester Lorren, in the matter of the princess Gael —*

*My lords, I write in the relief of a man reporting that his patient is, for once, behaving as a child of nine ought. She has taken an interest in HER TROUSSEAU. The royal betrothal having a date now fixed, she applies herself to the lists and ledgers of her own going-north with a diligence I would call touching in a less unsettling child. Her lady-mother, hearing of it, wept; she takes it for a daughter at last reconciled, at last softening, at last — her word — NORMAL. I would dearly love to share the lady's comfort. I confess I cannot account for why a bride's trousseau requires the drawings of a deep-water keel, which I glimpsed and which she folded away before I could look twice. I am likely mistaken in what I saw. I am very often, these last nine years, likely mistaken in what I saw.*

⁂

The betrothal letter sat on her reagent-map like a stone on a map of the sea, and she did not like it there.

It had come up from the King by way of a herald and a great deal of ceremony Gael had ignored, and it said, in the curling hand of a Northern maester, that the Lord of Winterfell's heir would be pleased to receive his bride at the turn of the year. *The turn of the year.* Eight months, perhaps nine, and the hull not even laid.

Wylla had read it aloud twice and then sat with her hands in her lap. "They'll send a party," she said. "To collect you. Lords have to come and *collect* a bride, it's — there's a procession, there's a feast, there's —"

"I know how a wedding works," Gael said, who did not, but knew how an extraction worked, which was the same thing seen correctly. The evil sect was calling in its marriage-piece. The disposal-match her cold sect-master father had arranged to be rid of a useless daughter was now a closing door, and she intended to be on the far side of it before it shut. The trouble was only the furnace. The vessel could not be hurried; the door could not be slowed.

She uncrossed the two clocks in her mind and looked at them. Eight months of wedding. Five years of ship. The gap was not a gap; it was a cliff.

"There's the other thing," Wylla said, low, because she made herself say the hard things now even when she'd rather not. "The men. The suitor's party that came to look you over and never — that the King's man is still tearing the city apart over. He's not stopping, Gael. He's worse. He's got every gold cloak in the lower city turning over stones."

"Let him turn stones."

"He's looking for *you.*"

"He's looking for a kingpin," Gael corrected, gently, as one corrects a promising student. "There's a difference, and the difference is the whole of why we're safe."

"Then we make the furnace burn hotter," she said.

"You can't make the *sea* go faster, Gael, it doesn't —"

"You can make a builder go faster if his patron is generous and patient and never once asks why." Gael smoothed the reagent-map flat, the dice-coin account a tidy black river down its margin, fat past any safe size now and getting fatter. "We pay for a second crew. We pay for the timber already seasoned, in another man's yard, bought clean. We pay the way you pay a forge to never let the fire go out — more fuel, more hands, no questions, and a stack of gold so high the man stops thinking about anything but the gold." She tapped the column. "Years come down when you flood the furnace. They never come down to *months.* But they come down."

Wylla looked at the black river of coin and at the child who could make a five-year boat appear in two if she simply set fire to enough of it, and something moved behind her eyes that was no longer quite horror and not yet quite anything else. "And the King's man," she said quietly. "The grandfather. He's still — they're still looking. For the suitor's men. The ones that didn't come home."

Gael waved a hand. There was, somewhere out in the city, an inquisitor turning over the disappearance of an advance party that had ridden in to assess her and ridden nowhere ever again. She had read him correctly, she felt, the first time she'd heard of his diligence: an orthodox sect-hunter, righteous and tireless, sweeping the lower city for the demonic Quiet Coin, the phantom dark-lord he could feel in everything and find in nothing. Let him sweep. He was hunting a ghost in a counting-book. A man could spend a career chasing the smoke of a fire he was personally feeding gold to.

"He looks for a kingpin," she said. "He'll keep looking for a kingpin. There isn't one. There's a furnace, and us, and a door with a date on it." She rolled the reagent-map closed, the keel-drawings safe inside it. "Place the second order tomorrow. Pay the seasoning premium. Tell the artificer his patron admires his patience and asks only that he forge a little faster, for love of the work."

Wylla wrote it down. Outside, eight months began their count, and five years, and somewhere below the city a man whose name they did not yet know watched the river of clean coin go by and thought, the way greedy men always thought just before the realm proved how grimdark it was, that a river that big surely wouldn't miss a cup.

## III.2 · The Excursions

The trouble with overseeing the forging of a transcendent vessel was that the furnace would not come to you.

So Gael went out at night. She told herself it was inspection — the master ought to look upon the work — but the truth, which she would not have called truth, was that she liked it. She liked the city with its eyes shut.

She read the drawings in the shipyard by a hooded lantern while the carpenters slept, checking the line of the laid keel against her own folded copy, correcting a man's honest work with the serene confidence of a child who has never built anything and the strange luck of one who is, in fact, usually right. She stood in the timber-yards before dawn and laid a hand on the great seasoned ribs stacked like the bones of something enormous, and felt, she was sure, the qi of good wood — the slow patient mountain-qi a tree spends a hundred years gathering, exactly the substance a vessel of ascension required. She went to the harbor and sat on a bollard with her knees drawn up and watched the black water that would carry her out of the story, the masts of other men's ships ticking against the stars, and she was, in those hours, as close to happy as her cold serene heart came — which is to say she stopped, for a little while, doing arithmetic, and simply looked at the sea, and the sea looked back and promised her the one thing she had wanted since she opened her eyes in a cradle nine years and a lifetime ago, which was: out.

Getting there was the pleasure. She went up the wall of Maegor's Holdfast and the dry moat of spikes below it might as well have been a stream for stepping; she let the breath sit low, the weight come off the heels, and she went up the courses of stone as though the wall were a ladder laid on its back. The city opened under her. She crossed it the way the manuals described the light-body art at maturity — roof to roof, the lead to the slate, the slate to the thatch she avoided because thatch told tales — and below her the great grim sect of Westeros slept with its mouth open and did not know its truant immortal walked over its head.

And the watch began to talk about a ghost.

⁂

*— and Old Hobb swears it again, swears he saw it go over the Holdfast roof, light as a cat, light as nothing, and the captain told him to dry out and sleep it off, but Maisie's boy saw a shape on the sept roof too, and the fishwife by the Mud Gate, and now they're calling it the ghost of the Red Keep, and mothers tell it to the little ones — be good or the Keep-ghost takes you over the rooftops — and the captain's doubled the night patrols and they catch nothing, nothing, a shadow that's gone when you look, and meanwhile the thieving's worse than it's been in years, plate gone, purses gone, and SOMEONE has to answer for it —*

⁂

She heard the legend three different ways within a week, and each time it pleased her more than the last.

A serving-woman murmured it doing up Gael's hair: the ghost of the Red Keep, my lady, mind you don't catch a chill at the window. A page told it to another page in a stairwell, hushed and delighted. The septa heard it and pronounced it a judgment on a sinful city, and looked at Gael's serene meditative stillness, and added that *some* in the Keep were plainly protected from such evils by their piety, and felt very pleased with her ward.

Gael received it the way a master receives the first temple built to him by mortals who have only ever glimpsed his shadow: with quiet, almost embarrassed satisfaction.

This was the city-legend. Every cultivator who reached a certain stage earned one — the unseen expert, the hermit who hides in the marketplace, the shadow on the rooftop that the common folk weave into a story because they cannot hold the truth. *The great recluse conceals himself in the city.* Her light-body art had matured to the point that ordinary eyes caught the edge of it and minds too small for the truth made a ghost of her. It was, frankly, a milestone. She noted it in the bestiary under her own progress, a private and rather smug little line, and did not for one instant connect the ghost to the manhunt, because in her frame the manhunt was an orthodox sect hunting a phantom kingpin and the ghost was an awed populace honoring a passing immortal, and these were obviously two different things happening to two different people, neither of whom was a nine-year-old girl on a roof.

There was one man who saw her plainly and said so.

His name was Hobb, and he had been a still-room boy in the Keep years ago — had seen, once, a small princess bite a ruby off a brooch and swallow it, and had said so, and been beaten for lying, and learned the lesson the Keep teaches its truth-tellers, which is to stop telling. He was a watchman now, older, greyer, posted on the night wall, and on a cold clear evening he looked up from his brazier and saw a shape go over the roof of the sept light as a blown leaf and he knew it, he *knew* it the way you know an old voice in the dark — the princess, the biting-girl, walking on the air the way she'd always been able to do impossible things while grown men told each other they hadn't seen it.

"There," he said to the watchman beside him, pointing, his voice gone thin. "There — on the roof — that's no ghost, that's a *girl,* I've seen her, I know her face —"

The other man looked where he pointed and saw an empty roofline and a fool with too much wine in him, and told him so, kindly, because Hobb was old and good for the long nights. "Sleep it off, Hobb."

"I'm not — I've seen her *before,* she ate a stone, she —"

"Aye. The demon-girl who eats stones and walks on roofs. Sleep it off."

So Hobb stopped pointing, and stood at his brazier, and watched the shape go over the river and out of sight, and said nothing more, because he had been right twice in his life and ignored twice, and a man learns. He was the one creature in the city who saw the literal truth — a princess on a roof — and the city's whole machinery existed to make sure that truth never landed anywhere it could be believed, and so it didn't, and Gael went over his head into the dark not knowing she'd been seen, and not caring if she had, because in her frame a mortal glimpsing the unseen expert and being disbelieved was simply how the city-legend got made.

What she could not see at all, because she never looked, was the rest of the story the watch was telling itself.

The thieving *was* worse. Not hers — she stole nothing; she had everything. But a city whose watch is hunting a ghost is a city whose watch is not minding the doors, and the ordinary thieves of King's Landing, who were legion and unghostly, went through the distracted streets like water through a broken weir. Plate vanished from a merchant's hall. A strongbox went from the customs-house. A jeweler on the Street of Steel woke to an empty case and a back window standing open. And because a watch cannot arrest a ghost but must arrest *someone,* and because the men above them wanted answers and the men above *them* wanted the ghost-talk stopped before it reached the throne, the watch went where the powerless were, which is where the watch always goes.

They took the jeweler's two scullions down to the black cells to ask them about the open window. A boy of fourteen and a woman with a bad hip who'd done the man's washing nine years. They asked them about the ghost, which the scullions had never seen, and about the missing pieces, which the scullions had never touched, and the boy came back up after three days walking strangely and not meeting anyone's eyes, and the woman did not come back up at all. Her sister went to the gate and asked after her, once, and a man at the gate took the sister's name down on a list, and the sister stopped going, because she had seen the man write her name and understood that asking at that gate was only a way to be invited inside it.

Gael did not know their names. She would not have logged them if she had; they were not assets, not obstacles, not vendors, not anything that touched her work. They were the cost the reader carried out of the room while she stood on a roof above their street and felt the cool clean qi of the harbor air and thought how well, how very well, her cultivation was coming along.

Below all of it, two other things moved.

The rivals moved first. The Quiet Coin's people had grown thin and slow while the Coin's true mind walked rooftops and read keel-drawings, and the other crews of the lower city smelled it the way crews do. A dice-den that paid the Coin's tithe got leaned on by a man named Greenhand — a real underworld figure, broad and unhurried, who had decided over a winter that the Quiet Coin was a story crews told to keep the soft ones in line, and that a story could be pushed. A runner of the Coin's got knifed in an alley off the Hook. A table went dark.

Marra brought the word up the line.

She was one of the clever commoners who fronted the Coin's tables — a smallfolk woman of perhaps five-and-twenty, quick and decent, sharp at sums and softer of heart than the trade allowed, in over her depth and not yet drowning. She came to Wylla's rooms white-faced and twisting her shawl. "The Greenhand's taken two of the floors on the Street of Sisters," she said. "Walked in, sat down, said the Coin's gone to ground and won't be coming back, and any house that pays the Coin's tithe pays *him* now or burns. And — Wylla — they're saying it loud, they're saying the Coin's nobody, the Coin's a tale, and the more they say it the truer it gets, because where *is* he? Where's the Coin? The houses are scared. *I'm* scared. What do I tell them?"

Wylla took her hands and said the things she'd learned to say — that the Coin was not gone, that the Coin saw everything, that the Greenhand had made a mistake he'd learn from, that Marra should keep the books square and trust the dark. She believed about half of it. She brought the rest to Gael.

Gael answered two of the tests, fast and quiet and from a great height, the way she did everything now — a word here, a debt called in there, a man who had been bold on a Tuesday simply not at his table on a Thursday. The Greenhand she looked at and weighed and set down again. He was a complication, not a danger; killing him was effort, and effort was the one currency she was short of these months, with the furnace eating all her nights. *Spare him,* she decided — judged him, on the page, not worth the spending — and turned back to her drawings, and did not feel the small cold click of a thread left dangling, because she never felt those. She could not answer all the tests, and she did not much care to. The empire had only ever been scaffolding around the ship. Let it fray at the edges. She would not be here to mind it.

And the factor moved.

His name was Lothar Quint, and he ran the pits — the dice-dens and cock-floors where the dirtiest of the coin came in — and he was, by his own lights, a careful man and a clever one. He had watched the river of money that flowed up through his pits and out to wherever the Quiet Coin lived, and he had begun, lately, to skim a cup from the river, and then a dipper, and then to lie awake at night doing a different arithmetic. Whoever the Quiet Coin was, he reasoned, the man was distracted. The man was bleeding money into some great mad project nobody could name. A distracted king is a king with a thin crown. And Lothar Quint had a family to feed, and a notion that he was the smartest man in the lower city, and no idea at all that the throne he was eyeing was a cushion, on which sat a child, who logged greedy men under *Obstacle (pending)* and waited.

## III.3 · The Greedy Factor

Quint asked to meet the Coin.

He asked it of Marra, who brought it to the other operators, who brought it shaking to Wylla, because in the lower city you did not *ask to meet the Coin;* the Coin was the dark behind the door, the reason your tithe was safe and your rivals afraid, and the surest sign a man had lost his sense was that he wanted the dark to have a face.

"He says he won't pass another month's take up the line," Wylla reported, "until he's shaken the hand that takes it. He says — " she steadied her voice, " — he says a man ought to know who he works for. He's telling the pits the Coin might be nobody. A *story.* He's saying it where it'll spread."

Gael was at her reagent-map, weighting the corners flat. She did not look up. "He leaned on you."

"He came to my rooms. He was — courteous." Wylla's hands were not quite still. "He made it courteous, which was worse. He said it would be a *shame,* he said, if a respectable maid found herself tangled in whatever the Coin really was, when the King's men are turning the city over looking. He said he could keep my name clear. For a consideration."

So the factor had found the maid, and meant to use her as a stair up to the dark, and had said *the King's men are turning the city over* in the same breath, which meant Lothar Quint, careful clever Quint, had drawn a line between the Coin's money and the inquisitor's manhunt and was prepared to walk along it toward Wylla if she did not buy him off. He was a thread that led from the empire to her one true asset, and from there, if pulled, to her.

Gael considered him with the flat attention she gave a reagent that had begun to spoil.

"Is he a good man?" she asked, which startled Wylla, because Gael never asked.

"He —" Wylla thought about it. "He's not cruel. He's frightened, mostly. He's a man who got close to something too big for him and can't stop reaching. He has a wife. Little ones. He talks about them."

"Then he's the worst kind," Gael said, and Wylla did not ask what she meant, because she had learned what Gael meant by such things, and it made her cold.

Here, Gael ran the self-audit she always ran, because she was a careful soul and would not act without clearing her conscience first. *Am I the villain of this story?* she asked herself, as she had asked at every removal since the cradle. And she answered herself the way she always did, on a technicality, in cosmic robes. A villain *struck* people down. A villain raised a hand. She would raise no hand. In the old novels there was a figure for exactly this: the wayward disciple, the one who turns on the sect that fed him, who lets greed swell in him past all sense until it draws down his own *tribulation* — the heavens themselves descending to balance the books of a soul that overreached. You did not even need to punish such a man. The cosmos did it for you. You merely had to be standing in the right place, with clean hands and a clear eye, when the lightning fell. Cause and effect. *Karma.* Quint had courted his own ruin with both hands; she would be nothing but the instrument through which a just world collected its due, which was not the same as murder, she was quite certain, having checked.

She was nine. She had built a doctrine of innocence so total it could absorb a man's whole household, and she ran it cleanly, and it cleared her, and she felt, closing the audit, the small clean peace of a person who has done the right thing.

This is what she told herself, and she was nine, and it cost her nothing now, and that was the change in her this book — not that she'd grown crueler in the heart, but that the doing had grown *quiet.* The first killing had been a thunderclap she'd spent a chapter bracing for. This one she arranged between two cups of tea.

She did not go near him. She never went near them. She used the paper, which was her real weapon and always had been.

A factor in the pits stood at a junction of a dozen supply lines — the candles and the coin-sacks and the watered wine and, because the lower city's pharmacopeia ran through one narrow channel, the things men used to settle their nerves and dull their hurts. All of it came up the long grey supply chain of the maester's stores, the great slow river of powders and tinctures that fed every household and dosing-house in the city, and Gael had quietly put a finger into that river two years ago — a clerk she owned, an entry she could move — and never taken it out. A man in Quint's trade took something to sleep; the trade was loud and bright and frightening and a man did not lie down in it without help. She knew which something. She had it in the bestiary, in fact, a tidy reverent entry on the poppy and its cousins, the way she logged every reagent — *the milk of the sleeper, three drops a comfort, thirty a doorway* — filed between a note on dragonbone ash and a sketch of a spider she admired.

She knew the hand it passed through, and the dose that helped, and the dose that didn't. And she changed nothing about the man's life except one quantity, far up a chain he could not see, in a ledger he would never read — a single figure shifted by a clerk who thought he was correcting an error, in a tincture a man bought himself, with his own coin, and carried home, and took as he always took it, on a night like any other. She did not watch. She had a keel to inspect. She went out over the roofs and stood in the cold timber-yard with her hand on the seasoned oak and felt the patient qi of it, and somewhere across the city a careful clever man lay down for a sleep he'd bought and paid for himself, and did not wake, and the heavens, as she would have said, balanced the account.

⁂

*— "let go," they're saying, Lothar Quint, the pit-master, "let go" of his place, which is a queer way to say a man went to bed hale and didn't wake, but that's the word that's traveling, he was "let go," and nobody will say by who, and his rooms are emptied and his crew scattered and his wife — he'd a wife, and the little ones — gone north to her people they say, or gone somewhere, and the strange thing, the thing that's got the lower city quiet, is his two house-servants, the cook and the door-man, the watch came for them the morning after, on account of the King's men want everyone near a man who dies sudden, and they went down to the cells to answer about the Coin and about the dead pit-master, and that was a fortnight ago, and the cook's mother goes to the gate every day, and —*

⁂

He had said it, at the end, the thing that in her frame was simply the death-flag cashing and in any other frame was a frightened man pleading with a door he could not see behind.

She had it second-hand, through Marra, who had been there when Quint last leaned on the operators and grown loud and grown small all in the same hour. *I'm not a greedy man,* he'd said. *I'm a careful one. I've only a family to feed, that's all it's ever been, a family to feed.*

Gael, hearing it, felt the small clean click of confirmation that she always felt. There it was. The platonic death-flag — *I'm only doing this for my family* — spoken aloud by a man with weeks to live, exactly as the genre promised. She had learned to dread those words in others because the grim world around her killed everyone who said them, and here was the world keeping its bargain again, proving her paranoia sane one more time, and she did not once turn the proof around to see that she was the world. The account is closed, she thought, and meant it as cosmology, the heavens balancing a wayward disciple's greed, and ran her self-audit and cleared herself, because she had not raised a hand, had not been in the room, had merely stood where karma fell.

What she did not see, because the camera of her serene head did not point that way, was the cook's mother at the gate. The cook had a mother. The door-man had a girl he was saving to marry. They had stood near a man who died, in a city whose watch needed someone to take below, and they went down into the dark under the Keep to answer for a ghost and a Coin and a corpse, none of which they had ever touched, and they did not come back up. That was the price of Lothar Quint, paid by people whose names Gael would never know, and the comedy of the chapter — the careful clever man who thought he was the cleverest thing within fifty feet of the maid, and was not the cleverest thing within fifty feet of the *child* — sat on top of it the whole way, the laugh and the ledger, hers and the reader's, never the same.

And out in the city the inquisitor's man heard *let go,* and heard *house-servants taken below,* and heard *a gang-flare around the respectable maid the bestiary had flagged,* and drew, at last, a clean straight line he had been wanting for a year. A natural death that was too convenient. A maid at the center of it. He did not have the Coin. But he had, for the first time, a thread that did not vanish when he pulled it, and it ran toward Wylla, and he began, patiently, to pull.

## III.4 · The Deadline

The King decided to send her north early.

No reason was given, because none was owed to a daughter. But Gael, who read the court the way she read a ledger, could see the shape of it well enough. The Hand's clampdown had made the court airless and strange — men disappearing, the cells loud, a low constant fear running under the marble like damp — and the King, who liked his house orderly and his pieces placed, had decided that the simplest way to be rid of all the trouble that seemed to gather around his odd useless granddaughter was to gather *her* up and send her a thousand miles away to be some Northern lord's problem. A wedding solved a great many things at once. It put the strange girl beyond the reach of the strange rumors. It paid a debt to a powerful house. And it removed from the Red Keep the one loose object the King had never been able to file, who unsettled him in a way he could not name and did not examine, because a king does not examine why a nine-year-old makes the back of his neck cold.

So the word went up the Kingsroad: send the party sooner. And down in the Keep the trousseau-carts began, genuinely now, to be loaded — the linens and the plate and the furs, the apparatus of a girl being shipped north to be made a wife — and the great machine of the wedding, which had been a date on a letter, became carts in a yard and lords on a road and a thing with weight and momentum, bearing down.

Wylla brought the word and the dread together, white around the mouth. "The turn of the year's been moved. They're saying high summer. They're saying — Gael, they're saying weeks. Not months. *Weeks.* The party's already being assembled to ride; the carts are in the yard, I've seen them. They mean to take you."

Weeks. The hull was three months from her keel, even flooded with gold, even with two crews burning night-oil round the clock. Weeks against months. The cliff again, and steeper than it had ever been, and no rooftop in the world to climb to get above it.

So Gael got sick.

She did it the way she did everything, with a craftsman's calm and a complete misunderstanding of why it worked. She took to her bed. She let her color go — there was a tincture for that, far up the maester's chain, a thing that whitened the lips and hollowed the eye and did nothing else — and she let her hands tremble at the right moments and her appetite fail at meals where it would be reported back, and she received her visitors all from a heap of pillows, a small frail thing too delicate by half to be jolted a thousand miles up the Kingsroad to a cold castle in autumn.

The maester came and held her wrist and frowned at a pulse that did exactly what she told it to. The septa came and prayed over her and pronounced the illness a trial sent to refine a saint, and was, Gael felt, not entirely wrong. And the King's own man came — sent down to assess whether the bride could ride — a hard grey steward with a list, who stood at the foot of her bed and looked at the wasted little princess and the basin and the shuttered window with the air of a man pricing a horse he suspects of being sound.

"The Hand wishes to know," he said, "when the lady may travel."

Gael turned her head on the pillow, slow, as though it weighed a great deal. She let her eyes go large and wet and unfocused. She was, in her own mind, executing one of the supreme techniques of the cultivation arts — *the pig that eats the tiger,* the master who hides his mountain-moving power inside the body of a dying child — and she performed it for the grey steward with all the art she had, which was considerable, and which was completely unnecessary.

"I want to go north," she whispered. "I want to be a good — I want to do my duty. Tell my grandfather." A small cough, beautifully timed. "I only want to be strong enough not to shame the King upon the road."

The steward looked at her a long moment. Then he wrote something on his list and went away and reported that the bride was genuinely and gravely reduced and would shame no one only by remaining alive to the wedding, and the date slid — a week, then two — and Gael lay back in her pillows the instant the door shut and felt the cold clean satisfaction of a flawless deception, never once suspecting that the steward had believed her before he walked in the room.

She thought it was a masterstroke.

In her frame this was the oldest disguise in the book — *concealing the cultivation, playing the weak pig that eats the tiger.* An immortal who could go over the Holdfast roof in the dark feigning the frailty of a court flower, so flawlessly that not one of these soft mortals suspected the power coiled in her thin little body. She congratulated herself on the performance nightly. She had become, she felt, a master of the hidden base.

She fooled no one, because no one was looking. That was the joke the reader got and she didn't: the realm believed her delicate not because her act was good but because the realm had written her delicate years ago — the strange quiet scholar-princess, *always* sickly, *always* odd, everyone said so. Her cover was not a thing she'd built. It was a story Westeros had told about her since the cradle, and she stepped into it like a coat already cut to her size, and called the perfect fit her own genius.

She looked at the court from her sickbed with new clarity in those weeks, and what she saw steeled her for the leaving. A sect of soft mortals, she thought. No cultivators among them, not one — only frightened short-lived people clutching at land and each other, and a few tamed spirit-beasts on the island that they had the gall to think made them strong. Nothing here to stay for. Nothing here at all but the door, and the date on it, and the furnace racing the date.

⁂

*To the Conclave, from the maester Lorren, in haste —*

*The bride is gravely reduced. I have seen her keep her color through a fever that emptied half this castle and I have seen her, this fortnight, fade in a draught, and I cannot reconcile the two girls, and have stopped trying. The Hand presses for the wedding regardless; he wants her north and married with a force I find, frankly, disproportionate to one delicate child, as though her continued presence in this Keep were itself a wound he means to close. He has men everywhere now. The cells are not quiet. I do not ask after what fills them. I have learned, in nine years near this child, that the questions one does not ask are the only ones that let a man sleep.*

⁂

The Hand's certainty had become a machine, and the machine needed fuel.

He was sure now — sure of the maid, sure that the maid led to the dark, sure that the dark led to the missing men and the missing money and the daughter the King wanted gone — and a sure man with the cells at his disposal does not wait for proof; he manufactures the conditions in which proof appears. So the watch went down into the lower city in earnest, and pulled in the operators it could find, and the smallfolk who had stood near the Quiet Coin's money — the table-fronts, the runners, the clever commoners the buyers thought they dealt with — went down to the black cells to be asked who they worked for.

Some of them did not know. The triple-blind that protected Gael protected nothing of theirs; they had been told to take coin and ask no names, and they had, and now they could give no names, and a man who cannot give a name to an inquisitor in a hurry is a man who stays below. The cells filled. Pella, who had run a faro table, went down and did not come up. Nona, who had carried purses, went down and did not come up. A half-dozen more whose only crime was that a child upstairs had needed hands and the realm had needed someone to blame. They went down into the dark, and the ones who came up came up changed, walking strangely, flinching at doors, and the ones who did not come up were spoken of, after a while, in the past tense, by people who learned not to speak of them at all.

Marra came up.

That was the thing that would matter, though no one could have known it yet. They took her down with the rest, the quick decent woman who'd fronted a table and brought the Greenhand's threat up the line, and they asked her who she worked for, and she did not know, truly did not know, only that a kind man came to her in the cells — not a gaoler, a gentle grey-bearded man who sat with her and gave her water and spoke softly — and told her she'd been used by something wicked, a demon's creature, a maid called Wylla who served a bewitched princess, and that Marra could help save the poor maid and herself both if she only watched, and remembered, and told him where the maid went. And Marra, who had been three days in the dark and wanted only to be out of it and to be on the side of the kind man instead of the gaolers, said yes, and meant it, and came up into the light believing with her whole grateful heart that she was helping. The kind man's name was Otto. She would feed him the maid's movements for a season, and never once think herself a traitor, because no one had told her she was one.

And the girl who had used them all, who had reached down through three layers of paper to make a market out of their hands, lay in a heap of pillows playing dying, two clocks crossing over her head — the hull crawling up behind, the wedding rushing down — and felt, between performances, only a serene and rising certainty that her timing would hold.

## III.5 · The Tempering

A letter came up from Dragonstone that week, in her mother's own hand, which Gael read once and set aside and Wylla read twice and could not look at.

⁂

*My darling girl —*

*They tell me you are too unwell to travel and my heart is in my throat, but the maester swears it is only a passing weakness and that you will mend, and I choose to believe him because I cannot bear the other thing. I send you this — your grandmother's grey cloak, the warm one, lined, for the cold roads north — and a little book of prayers, and dried plums, which you loved as a babe. Wear the cloak on the journey. Promise me you will keep warm. I think of you on that long road every hour and I am so very proud of how brave you are being, how dutiful, how you go to your wedding without a word of complaint. You were always such a strange brave quiet child. Keep warm, my love. I will see you at the wedding if the Seven are kind, and if they are not, know that no mother ever loved a daughter more. — Your mother.*

⁂

Gael had glanced at the cloak, judged it serviceable against sea-cold, and folded it into the ship-stores. She did not read the rest twice. There was work to do, and the work, that week, was Wylla.

⁂

The days before the rite were the strangest of Wylla's life, which was saying a great deal, given the life.

The ship was days from ready and the wedding was days from arriving and the two facts should have made her princess frantic, and instead Gael had gone calm — gone deep and quiet and busy in a way Wylla had only seen at her meditations, as though the closing of the trap had simplified everything down to a few clean tasks. They packed in the dark hours. Salt-meat, water-casks, oil, grain, the reagent-map, the bestiary. *Voyage-prep,* Gael called it, and there was a great deal of it, and most of it made sense — a body needs feeding on a long crossing, a hull needs provisioning — and then there was the other thing, the thing Gael spoke of in the flat technical voice she used for the parts of her work that frightened Wylla most.

"You're not provisioned," she'd said one night, looking at Wylla the way she looked at a cask she suspected of leaking. "The ship is. You're not. An untempered body is a liability at sea. We'll see to it before we sail."

And Wylla, who had learned by now that there was no asking *what do you mean,* had only nodded, and gone cold, and waited for the night Gael would *see to it,* and dreaded it the way you dread a thing you've already decided to let happen because the alternative is leaving the only person you have. She had crossed, somewhere in this last year, from a woman things were done *to* into a woman who chose. She did not feel saved by the crossing. She felt, mostly, that she had gone somewhere she could not come back from, and that the girl who'd taken her there was asleep most afternoons now like an ordinary tired child, and that she would follow her onto the sea regardless, and that this was either love or madness and there was no longer any way to tell the two apart.

⁂

It was Wylla who knew it was love, though she would never have used the word, and Gael never would have permitted it.

She had been summoned to the still-room after dark — *bring the long-handled spoons, tell no one* — and she had gone, the way she always went now, with the small old fear and the larger new faith folded together so tight she could no longer find the seam. The still-room was warm. The brazier was lit. On the bench Gael had laid out a mix Wylla didn't know, dark and resinous and faintly sweet, and beside it her own thin hands, scrubbed pink, sleeves pushed up past the elbow like a woman about to do real work.

"Sit," Gael said. "Drink this. All of it. It will be unpleasant; it is meant to be. Then be still and don't fight me, whatever you feel. Fighting it makes it hurt."

Wylla drank it. It was unpleasant. She sat, and Gael came and stood behind her and set both small hands flat between her shoulder-blades, and said something low that Wylla didn't catch — *opening the meridians,* it might have been, or some other phrase out of the strange country her princess lived in — and then the warmth came.

It came in through her back like the brazier had reached out a hand. It went down her spine and out along her arms and into her legs, warm and then hot and then a deep terrible *full* feeling, as though her body were a cup that had always been half-empty and was being poured into past its brim. For one whole bright moment Wylla was afraid — purely, animally afraid, the old horror surging up clean and new — because something was *changing* in her, under the skin, in the meat and the blood, and the thing changing her was a nine-year-old girl with her hands flat on her back and her breath going ragged, and Wylla understood with total clarity that her princess was not a girl at all and never had been and that she had bound her whole life to something she would never, ever be able to name.

And then it passed, the way the worst of the sea passes, and left her — warm. Glowing. Steadier than her own body had ever felt. And Gael's hands came away, and Gael sat down hard on the bench like all her strings had been cut, gray-faced, breathing as though she'd run up the Holdfast stair, smaller than Wylla had ever seen her.

Wylla turned and looked at her princess spent on the bench and understood, in the slow way you understand the thing you're forbidden to know, exactly what had happened. She didn't have the cultivator's words for it. She had the human ones. *She poured part of herself into me. She made herself less, to make me more. She has never once made herself less for anything.*

"Don't," Gael said, before Wylla could speak, reading her face and refusing it. "Don't make it a *thing.*"

"What did you —"

"An investment." Gael's voice was thin but the contempt for sentiment was perfectly intact. "You're an untempered mortal. Mortals die at sea — the salt, the cold, the long bad water, the fevers a ship breeds. A liability. I can't crew an immortal vessel with a body that breaks the first hard month. So I've tempered you. Opened your channels, set my own qi running in them to season the flesh. A master-to-disciple transmission. You'll fever-proof now, and sleep less, and last the voyage. That is all this is. Don't look at me like that."

She believed it. That was the wonder of it, and Wylla saw the wonder and held her tongue. Gael genuinely, serenely believed she had performed a cold technical procedure on a piece of equipment she meant to keep — *investing in the asset,* she'd think later, logging it; she logged everything — and had no idea, none, that she had just made the one choice her entire creed forbade. The cultivator's whole law was *accumulate, never spend; ascend, owe nothing; the dao is taken alone.* And she had reached into her own hard-won self and given a measure of it away, on purpose, knowing the cost, to keep one frightened mortal alive across an ocean. The genre had a word for the rite and no word at all for the love, and so she'd filed it under the rite, and the cover was exactly what let her do it. She would have died before she did it for any reason she could name.

Wylla sat in her new warm steady body and looked at the girl who'd given her a piece of herself and called it accounting, and felt the last of the horror burn off her for good, and something take its place that she also would not name, because some doors you don't open. She'd been raising the small flags lately without quite meaning to — *after we're away,* she'd catch herself saying, *when there's the two of us and the sect and the open water, after* — talking about a future the way you do when you've started, against all your sense, to believe in one.

She did not know that Gael heard those flags and dreaded them. That her princess, certain past all argument that everyone who spoke of *after* and *when this is over* and *the future* was a marked soul the grim world would collect — had watched Wylla start to speak of the future and felt the old cold fear close on the one person she'd kept, and had reached, the way she always reached, for the genre's logic to fix it. You could not unsay a death-flag. But a master could *temper* a disciple past the danger the flag foretold. So she'd tempered her. To clear the flag. To save her from an omen.

It was the one spend a cultivator's whole logic forbade, and the genre had handed her the one rationalization that let her make it. *Clearing a flag.* She could not have given a piece of herself away for love — love was an attachment, a weakness, the red dust the dao required her to sever — but she could perform a *transmission rite* on a junior sister to ward off a narrative omen, and so she had, and the thing she would not let herself want she got anyway, smuggled in under cover of cosmology. The most human act of her life, filed as the coldest. She would have died before she did it for any reason she could name.

She was right, in the end, for entirely the wrong reasons, which was the shape of her whole life. The tempering did save Wylla — not from the omen the flag foretold, but from the omen made flesh. The fever-proofing held; the maid would cross the long bad water and not sicken, exactly as promised. But that was the small saving. The large one came at the dock, weeks off, and it ran the other way: the strength Gael had poured into her maid's back to keep her *alive* was the same strength that would let that maid's hands stay steady on a knife and her nerve hold past the hesitation — would let her cross the worst threshold a person crosses, and not break, and not falter long, and do the killing that kept them both free. Gael had set out to clear a death-flag from the one person she loved. What she had actually done was make her a killer. The gift that saved Wylla's life was the gift that took whatever Wylla had been before, and the girl who poured it in would never understand either half of the bargain, because she had filed the whole transaction under *investing in the asset* and closed the book.

⁂

## III.6 · The Narrow Escape

The wedding party came down the Kingsroad on the morning the hull came ready, and the two things met at the water, which was the only fitting place for them to meet.

She had it timed to the hour, or nearly. The shipwright's agent had sent the word two nights before — *she swims; she's provisioned; she'll catch the morning tide* — and Gael had read it the way a disciple reads the sign that the furnace has gone quiet and the treasure is forged: the long cycle complete, the vessel true. She'd had the dice-coin's last river run out to fill the holds. Salt-meat and water and oil and grain enough for two girls and a lifetime, because an immortal provisions *forever;* she'd never thought past *forever* to the smaller truer thing, that she'd packed a child's idea of how long two people need to be fed, which was: always.

It came down to a single morning, the way these things do — the two clocks that had run the whole book finally striking the same hour.

The Stark party rode in under banners at first light, a long line of grey and white come a thousand miles to collect a bride, lords and horses and a wheelhouse hung with furs for the cold roads north, and they clattered up to the Keep in the grey before dawn full of the small grumbling cheer of men at the end of a long ride, ready to be fed and to feast and to carry their lord's new wife away. The King's herald went to wake the princess for her wedding journey. He knocked, and got no answer, and knocked again, and went in, and found a sickbed cold and a window standing open and a girl gone — gone in the dark hours before, over the roofs, the way the ghost of the Red Keep was said to go. He stood in the empty room with the cold sheets in his hand and could not, for a moment, make his mind say the thing his eyes were telling it, because the thing was not possible: brides did not simply *leave.*

And down at the waterline, in the same grey light, a little way along the harbor from where the Stark banners would never think to look, the great forged treasure rode at her mooring with the morning tide just beginning to pull, and a child came down to the water to board her and rise.

Gael had crossed the city one last time in the dark and come down to the docks light as a leaf, Wylla a step behind her carrying the one bag that held the reagent-map and the bestiary and nothing else that mattered, and the harbor was just waking — a few fishermen, a gull or two, the smell of tar and salt and morning. The boat to carry them out to the ship was waiting. Twenty yards of wet planking, and then the sea, and then forever.

She had a vessel and a tide and a crew bought clean. She had, for the first time in three books, *won* — and was a step from the boat, already an immortal in her own mind, already watching the lower realm recede.

Otto's man was already at the dock.

He was there because Otto, for once, had been right — exactly, precisely right, the one clean deduction of a year of wrong ones. He had pulled the thread from the maid, and the thread had not vanished, and where every other lead dissolved into the smoke of a phantom Coin, this one had held, because this one ran through people who were real. A rival Gael had spared in the spring — the Greenhand man she'd judged not worth the spending, met on the page, let live — had sold the Coin's movements to the King's men for his own skin when the cells started filling. And Marra. Marra, the quick decent smallfolk woman Gael had clocked turning, months back, and had *spared* — looked at, weighed, and let live, because killing her had seemed like effort and she'd seemed harmless and the ship was nearly done — Marra had been feeding Otto the maid's movements for a season, terrified into it and then, worse, *won* into it, because Otto had told her she was helping save a poor bewitched maid from a demon, and Marra had believed him, and kept believing him, and had told his men, just last night, that the maid had asked after the morning tide.

The loose ends she could never close, and the one she had *chosen* not to, came together at the waterline and put Otto's man on the dock with a long knife and orders to take the maid alive and the demon-child dead, and he was a competent man, and he was waiting in the right place, and for one cold moment the whole five years of furnace and paper and rooftop nearly came apart on the last yard of dock.

Gael saw him a half-second late. She was carrying the reagent-map and her own small certainty and was already half into the boat in her mind, already gone, already an immortal on the deck of her vessel watching the lower realm recede — and so she was slow, the only time in three books she was slow, and the man came off the piling fast with the knife low and there was no height to climb to and no paper to hide behind, only the wet boards and the dark water and a competent grown man between a nine-year-old and the sea.

Wylla killed him.

She hesitated first. That was the thing — that was the whole of who she now was, gathered into one half-instant on a dock. She was between him and Gael with the boat-line still in her hand, and her tempered body was fast and her tempered nerves were steady and she *could,* she knew she could, the strength was in her like a held breath — and still she stopped. For the length of a heartbeat the maid who had begun this story fainting at the sight of blood stood at the edge of the worst thing a person can do and felt the full horror of it open under her, clear-eyed, no genre to gloss it, *this is a man and I am about to end him,* the door wide open and her standing in it.

And then she looked at Gael — small, slow, surprised, the only person in the world she had left or wanted — and she crossed.

She did it with the boat-knife, close, the way you do a thing you've decided, and it was not clean and it was not reflexive and it was not a master's stroke; it was a frightened devoted woman committing the whole of herself in one motion because the alternative was a world without the girl who'd poured a piece of herself into her back and called it stock. The man went down between the pilings and the dark water took him the way it had taken the suitor's party, the way the Keep took its people, corpseless and quiet, and Wylla stood over the place breathing hard with her princess's gift running steady and terrible in her arms, and she had become, in that half-second, the last thing the convert's road had left to become.

For a long moment neither of them moved. The harbor went on around them as if nothing had happened — a gull cried, the tide slapped the pilings, somewhere a fisherman called to another fisherman about a net — and a man was gone into the dark water and two girls stood on the wet planking and the world did not so much as pause.

"Get in the boat," Gael said. Her voice was not quite even. She had seen it — had seen the maid she'd made falter at the threshold and then cross it, had seen the whole human shape of the thing she usually never had to look at, the cost up close and wearing a face she loved. She would not say what she'd seen. She filed it somewhere she did not keep a register for, and looked away, and said it again, gentler. "Get in the boat, Wylla. It's done. We're going."

They got in the boat.

The little craft took them out across the brightening water to the vessel riding at her mooring, and the crew Gael had bought clean and questionless hauled them aboard without a word — they had been paid not to have words — and the lines came off the bollards one by one, and the sail came down and filled, and the morning tide took the hull the way the manuals promised it would, the way she had known since the cradle it someday must: slow, then sure, then certain. Out past the anchored ships. Out past the breakwater. Out toward the open grey water, the great forged treasure swimming at last, five years in the furnace and a lifetime in the wanting, and the city falling away behind them course by course like a wall taken down stone by stone.

And as the harbor slid back, Gael made herself look at what she was leaving, because a master should look once upon the lower realm before he rises out of it.

The shore held everything her freedom had cost, if she had only had the eyes to count it. The Keep stood grey on its hill, and under it the black cells were full of people who had touched her money and could not name the hand that paid it — Pella, who had run a faro table and gone down into the dark and not come up; Nona, who had carried purses and gone down and not come up; a dozen more whose only crime was that a child upstairs had needed hands. Out in the lower city the cook's mother still went to a gate each morning and was not let in, and the door-man's girl had stopped saving for a wedding, and the jeweler's washerwoman's sister had learned not to ask. The watch had hunted a ghost across the rooftops all winter and, finding no ghost to take, had taken the powerless instead, and the whole human wreckage of it — the questioned, the changed, the vanished, the ones spoken of now only in the past tense — lay strewn along the coast behind her like the long bright wake a ship leaves on the water, widening, fading, gone. Marra was down there too, somewhere, still believing she had helped, still believing in a kind grey man who had told her she was saving someone. They had paid for Gael twice over, the smallfolk — used by her market, then swept up by the realm's terror for crimes she had committed and they had not — and they would go on paying after she was a speck on the horizon, because the Keep would need someone to blame for a princess who simply vanished, and it would never, ever blame the princess.

Gael looked at all of it and did not see one piece of it. She saw a lower realm receding. She saw the red dust she was severing to rise. She filed the whole grim shore under *attachments left behind* — serene, cold, oblivious to the very last — and turned her face to the open sea.

She had one tie left to sever, and she reached for it, because the dao is thorough.

Her mother. Alicent, away on Dragonstone, who had given her the egg in the cradle and smothered her with love for nine years and was, this morning, being told that her daughter was simply gone. Gael stood at the rail with the continent going small behind her and reached for the gloss to file it, the way she'd filed every cost of every chapter, the cool clean cultivator's frame that made the unbearable bookkeeping —

*Mother: an attachment severed. The dao requires that one feel nothing, cutting the red dust. The disciple ascends alone, and owes —*

And it didn't come.

There was a blank where the register should have been. She reached again and the words were not there; the frame that had caught everything, that had turned a dead hatchling and a drained dragon and a vanished suitor and a poisoned factor each into a clean line she could carry — found nothing to write here, closed on nothing, came up empty. She stood at the rail with her hand out for a sentence that would not finish itself, and felt the place where it should have been, the small cold draught of it.

She did not name it. She looked at the water.

She went on.

Beside her, Wylla saw her princess go quiet at the rail, go still in a way that was not meditation, and did not ask. Some doors you don't open. She put her tempered, terrible, steady hand on the cold wood near Gael's, not touching, just near, and the two of them watched the grey water and the continent getting smaller, and the south ahead getting warmer, and neither of them said anything at all.

## Epilogue

⁂

Wylla stood at the rail on the fourth day and watched the water and tried to find the place where she ended.

The continent was long gone. The water was warmer now, and bluer than the grey northern sea she'd been born to, and the wind was kind, and below decks her princess was asleep — actually asleep, spent and recovering, breathing like an ordinary tired child, which she did more often now that the chase was behind them and which Wylla had learned to watch for and not mention. There was meat enough in the hold for a lifetime, and water, and oil. There was a girl who had given her a piece of her own self and would deny it with her last breath. There was the open sea, and the warm south, and a sect of two that would never have a third.

She turned her hands over and looked at them. They were steady. They had been steady since the dock. The gift held; the warmth ran clean in her still, fever-proof, sea-durable, exactly as promised. The same hands had done up a princess's hair, and fronted a criminal empire, and held a dying woman's secret, and, four days ago, on a wet dock at dawn, put a knife into a man and not shaken.

She was a noble girl who had become a maid who had become a manager who had become a believer who had become, four days ago, a killer. She knew all the words for the road behind her. She did not have one for where it had ended. *What am I now,* she thought, watching the water, and found that she could turn the question over and over and never make it sting — that it sat in her, open, unanswered, the way the warm horizon sat ahead of them, neither a promise nor a threat, only a thing she had chosen and would go on choosing, eyes open, because the alternative was a world without the girl asleep below.

She did not ask it aloud. There was no one to ask, and no answer she needed. She watched the water. It was very blue. She found, to her own quiet surprise, that she was not afraid.

⁂

The realm, for its part, never did work out what had happened, which suited everyone.

There had been a princess, and then there had not, and a Keep cannot abide a hole that size in its own account of itself, so it filled the hole with whatever came to hand. The official version was that the lady Gael, ever delicate, had been lost — drowned, most said, in a boating mishap on the eve of her northern wedding, a tragedy, the Seven keep her. The unofficial versions were better. A foreign prince had carried her off; she'd been seen on a Tyroshi galley with her hair dyed and a lover at the rail. A god had taken her, the way the gods took the too-beautiful and the too-strange. The septas favored the prophecy — she had been the chosen of some scripture all along, gathered up to her purpose, the maegi-princess ascended, and Aldith wept at the wonder of having tended a saint and never knowing it. Septon Barth, the one mind in the realm that had ever come within a hand's breadth of the truth, set the contradictions side by side in his careful way — the missing men, the missing money, the missing girl — and felt the shape of an answer move just under the surface of them, and was called away to the King's war before he could close his hand on it, and never came back to it, and died with the theory one fact short.

The Hand never closed his case. He had been right — gloriously, uniquely right, his one true deduction in a year of false ones — and his man had been in the right place at the right hour, and still the girl was gone and his man was gone with her, vanished off a dock at dawn into the same nothing that had swallowed the suitor's party a year before. He had won every move. He had lost the game. He filed the matter, in the end, as an open disappearance, a wound that would not close, and went back to the King's business, and told himself the girl had drowned, because the alternative was that a child had beaten him, and that was not a thing a man like Otto could afford to have believed, least of all by himself.

⁂

*To the Conclave, from the maester Lorren, the last he wrote upon the matter —*

*My lords, I will trouble you no further with the princess Gael, who is drowned, the court is agreed, and whose strangeness I have catalogued these nine years to no profit and considerable cost to my sleep. I record only, for the honesty of the archive, that I do not believe she drowned. I believe nothing in particular instead; I have no theory; I have learned the danger of theories where this child is concerned. I note merely that the girl who could not be poisoned and would not weaken and walked, some swore, upon the air, has gone out of the world by water on the one morning the water could carry her, and that I feel, writing it, not grief but a queer cold relief, as a man feels when a storm he has watched gather all season finally breaks over someone else's roof. May the Seven keep her, wherever she is. I find I hope, against all sense, that she is well. I will not write of her again.*

⁂

For not one of them, not the King nor the Hand nor the Conclave nor the singers who would make a sad pretty song of the drowned princess, could reach the only explanation that fit: that a girl had wanted to leave, and had built a way, and had taken it. A foreign prince they could imagine. A god, a prophecy, a conspiracy of unseen men — all of these the realm could hold. A princess with the will and the patience and the cold clear agency to author her own escape from the story written for her was the one thing Westeros could not picture — could not even reach the edge of, because the realm had spent her whole life telling itself she was delicate, useless, a marriage-piece, a strange quiet girl to be placed and forgotten — and so it never did, and the truth sailed south unimagined while the legend sealed shut behind it like water over a stone. The last and best of the realm's cheat codes, the one that had hidden her since the cradle, held to the end: no one looked for a girl with agency, because no one believed there could be one.

Far out on Dragonstone, where she had gone to keep her grief away from a court she could not bear, Alicent was told that her daughter was gone, and got, at the last, her terminal *what the hell* — not a fret this time, not a misread, just a mother standing at a high cold window with the news in her hand and nothing in her at all but the size of the absence. She would mourn the wrong girl forever, the delicate strange beloved daughter who had drowned; she would never learn the true one, never know that the daughter had lived and chosen and sailed, never know that her own smothering, blundering, lifelong love had been the one shield that kept the cold father and the hunting grandfather off the child long enough for the child to grow her way free. She had been the wall Gael never saw she stood behind. She would die not knowing it. She looked out at the same grey water her daughter had crossed and did not know she was looking the right way, and after a while she went in, and the door closed, and the sea went on without comment.

Two girls on a ship, the continent behind them, the warm south ahead. One of them slept; one of them watched the water. Both of them had got exactly what they'd wanted, which is the most dangerous thing that can happen to anyone in any story, and one of them was now a killer, and the sea did not care, and the wind was kind, and whether it was freedom they were sailing into or only a prettier cage of their own making was a question the water would not answer and the girls would not ask.

They sailed on. The south was warm. The door was open.
