# BOOK I — *the cage*

## I.1 · Dragonbane

The egg would not stop humming at her, so Gael, who was four days old and bored to the seams of her small wet existence, did the only sensible thing a cultivator could do with a radiant spirit-relic left in her crib: she got her gums around it and bit down.

The shell was warm. It had been warm since they laid it beside her, a black-and-scarlet thing the length of her forearm, and it had been singing to her the whole time in a register that sat just under hearing, the way a struck bell sings after the sound is gone. Nobody else in the room seemed to hear it. Gael had decided some hours ago that this was significant. She had been dead, after all, and now she was not, and there was a relic humming in her crib, and a person who had read four hundred cultivation novels and died face-down in the four-hundred-and-first knew a spirit-treasure when one was placed at her elbow.

So she bit it. The shell took a chip of her gum with it, a bright clean pain, and her mouth filled with blood, and into the blood came the other thing — the warmth, the bell-note, pouring off the broken edge of the egg and into her like water finding a low place. She swallowed the blood and the warmth both.

*Qi*, she thought, with enormous satisfaction. *There it is.*

So. Isekai'd. She'd suspected it since the ceiling — no ceiling in the afterlife had any business being that water-stained — and here was the confirmation, sweet as a lozenge. A lesser disciple might have panicked at being four days old and unable to lift her own head. Gael simply began, methodically, to draw. Nine revolutions, the way the manuals said, warmth into the belly, hold, release. She had a cauldron to forge. She had, she reflected, all the time in the world. She intended to make it considerably longer.

Above her a wet-nurse leaned in, saw the blood on her lips and the egg in her two fat fists, and made a sound like a kettle.

Gael filed the sound under *ambient sect activity* and kept drawing.

The egg disagreed.

She felt it the way you feel a held breath about to break — the bell-note climbing, the warmth in the shell turning frantic, pushing back against her pull. *Resisting cultivation,* she thought, scandalized. *A treasure with a will of its own.* This was, she knew, a known phenomenon; the high-grade relics always fought you; you had to subdue them. She set her tiny jaw and pulled harder, all nine revolutions at once, greedy.

The egg split down its long axis with a wet structural crack.

What came out of it was not warmth. It was teeth. It was a hand's-length of wet black hide and a head all jaw, blind and steaming, mewling, hauling itself up over the wreck of its own shell toward the only heat in the cradle, which was her. Its claws were already out. They were the size of grass-seeds and they were the sharpest things she had ever felt, and one of them opened a line down her cheek before she got her arm up.

*Trial-beast,* Gael thought, with the clean joy of a reader who has reached the part she was promised. *First kill. Power from the first kill — textbook.* The genre did not lie about everything. She got both hands around the small thrashing throat — her grip strength was an abomination, four days old or not, the warmth had seen to that — and she squeezed and bore down and put her whole tiny weight into it, and the hatchling raked her arms to ribbons and screamed, and she did not let go.

It took longer than the novels implied. That was the only complaint she would later file. In the novels the first kill was a paragraph; nobody ever mentioned that a paragraph could go on this long, that a thing could take this much killing, that her own arms would shake. She made a note of the discrepancy. *Two stars for accuracy,* she thought, somewhere under the effort. *The genre oversells the ease and undersells the mess.* She would learn, over the years, that it always did.

When it stopped, she lay panting in a cradle gone red, the dead thing across her chest, and she did the methodical thing: she got a claw loose from its slack foot, and she ate it. Tougher than the shell. Worth it. The warmth that came up out of it was a different colour entirely, deeper, older, and she drew it down into her belly, nine revolutions, and felt her dantian — she was sure now she had a dantian — settle and bank like a covered fire.

She lay there a while afterward in the cooling red, taking stock with the satisfaction of a clerk closing good accounts. She had been alive four days and she had already survived a culling, banked her foundation, and made her first kill. By the arithmetic of every novel she'd ever read, she was ahead of schedule. The discomfort — the trembling, the long bad minute when the thing wouldn't die, the wet weight of it now stiffening on her chest — she set aside as a cost already paid and therefore not worth feeling. She had read four hundred of these books. She knew the shape of a beginning when she was lying in one.

She was still drawing it down when the nanny came in, and screamed, and dropped the basin, and ran.

*To the Conclave, from the Citadel's man at the Red Keep:*

*The princess Gael, fourth living child of King Jaehaerys and Queen Alysanne, four days delivered, presents a trance-state I can compare to nothing in my training save deep study or a greyscale stupor — yet she is in rude health, gaining weight, taking the breast (when a nurse can be found who will hold her). I have requested more parchment.*

⁂

It was the wet-nurse who put the word out, and the word was *cursed*. A babe that never cried. A babe that lay still and open-eyed half the day "like a little corpse practising." She would not take the night shift; nor would the next one; nor the one after, once she'd seen the dried blood crusted in the seams of the cradle and heard about the dead dragon. Two nurses gave notice in a sennight. In the guardroom a practical man with a spear said, "Good. One fewer dragon for the stables to feed," and was hushed, but not before he'd grinned.

None of this reached Gael, who had problems of her own. She was, she had concluded, in an evil sect.

She had not wanted to conclude this. She'd held out hope. But she was a fair-minded soul, and the evidence was the evidence: *what righteous order drops a lizard-monster into a cradle with a four-day-old disciple?* That was a culling. That was a trial designed to weed out the weak before they cost the sect anything. The warm presence that had laid the egg here — she could feel it sometimes, a soft despairing heat that came and bent over her and dripped on her face — must be an inner-sect elder, then, running the cull and grieving the ones who failed it. The grief told her the elder was conflicted. *A reluctant villain*, she thought, charitably. She would remember that.

This raised the obvious question, the only one that mattered, and she turned it over in the long open-eyed hours while the household crept around her crib. *What am I, in this story?* The overlooked reincarnator, plucked from a low place, destined to rise? She liked that. Or — and here she was scrupulous, here she ran the audit she would run a thousand times after — the *villainess*? The reborn schemer everyone roots against, the one whose whole arc is her comeuppance? Because she had, undeniably, just strangled a baby animal with her bare hands in a crib and eaten part of it, and that did read a little villainous, set down plainly.

She cleared herself on a technicality. The thing had attacked *her*. Self-defence. A righteous cultivator was permitted self-defence; it was practically the first rule. She resolved, very seriously, to be one of the good ones, *especially* now that she knew the org she'd been born into was rotten. She would not become the villainess. She would simply get strong, and watch the people above her — there were powers in this place, she could feel them moving, comings and goings that pressed on her senses with a weight that meant authority, and one of them in particular, cold and very high, that her gut filed at once as *sect-master, final boss* — and she would keep a list, and she would learn the shape of the cage before she so much as rattled it.

She lacked a young master. The genre promised one — the arrogant disciple, the rival who dies early to teach the protagonist that the world has stakes — and she had not yet located him. She settled provisionally on the spearman who'd grinned in the guardroom, on the grounds that he had a stupid face and a loud voice, which was most of the qualification. It was thin evidence. She knew it was thin. She filed him anyway, because an unfilled role made her anxious, and moved on.

She watched for the death flags, too. She'd started watching for them the moment she'd worked out where she was, because the death flag was the cheapest information the genre gave you and the most useful: a character speaks fondly of going home, of the wedding they'll have when this is all over, of the child they're saving coin for — and then they die, every time, the omen as reliable as a struck bell. In a world full of murder, a person who could read the flags lived longer than a person who couldn't. So she listened, in the cradle, to the nurses and the guards passing the door, and she catalogued who spoke of futures and who didn't, and resolved to keep clear of the ones who did. It was, she'd find, the single piece of genre wisdom that grimdark Westeros never once let her down on. The realm killed flag-raisers with a thoroughness no novelist would have dared. She would be right about the death flags, again and again, for entirely the wrong reasons, for the rest of her life.

Her mother came in the dark and lifted her out of the bad crib and held her against the soft despairing heat and wept into her hair and whispered that she was strong, she was *strong*, look how strong, her brave clever girl who'd killed a dragon in her cradle and lived — and Gael, drawing nine slow revolutions of warmth out of the woman's pulse without the least idea she was doing anything unkind, agreed completely.

She had unlocked her first title. The nurses had supplied it, hissing it in the corridors, and she'd caught it through the door and turned it over and found it good.

*Dragonbane.*

She had been alive four days. She was off to a frankly excellent start.

## I.2 · The Heist

The lock was a problem of leverage, and Gael, who was two and a half and could now reach the latch if she stood on the slop-bucket, had been studying leverage for some weeks.

She had plateaued. This was the shameful truth at the bottom of her, the thing she examined in the long meditative hours and could not fix: she had eaten her crib-monster, banked the warmth, drawn the egg dry months ago — and then nothing. No new fuel. You could not forge a cauldron on memories. A cultivator without reagents was a forge without ore, and the Red Keep, for all its grimness, did not leave spirit-treasures lying about in the cradles of toddlers a second time. She had been reduced to drawing thin warmth off people, off the cat, off a brazier once until a nurse snatched her back — subsistence cultivation, beneath her dignity.

But she had ears, and she paid attention, and she had heard the maester's still-room described in a hushed voice as a place of "poisons that would drop a horse." Poisons. Concentrated essences. *Reagents.* A righteous disciple did not steal, of course. But she had reasoned her way around that weeks ago: a sect that fed monsters to infants had no moral claim on its storeroom, and besides, she was only borrowing, and besides that, *the strong take what they need to grow* was a load-bearing axiom of every novel she'd ever read. She'd run the self-audit. She'd cleared herself. She was, she had decided, simply doing a heist, and a heist was a respectable thing for a cultivator to do.

She had a method now. She was proud of the method. *Sight, plan, implement, reward.* She'd seen the door (left ajar by an acolyte hauling water; the man never latched it twice running). She'd planned the route (under the long table while the acolyte's back was turned, behind the curing-rack, up the shelf by the gaps between jars, which she'd practised on the nursery shelving until her grip held). She implemented it now, in the grey hour after the noon meal, a small grave child going hand over hand up a wall of crockery with the focus of a master ascending a sword-peak.

The shelf was a wonderland. Forty jars, fifty, labelled in a hand she couldn't read, and she didn't need to read them, because she could *feel* them — most of them dull, inert, dead mineral, but here and there a jar that hummed the way the egg had hummed, that warmth-under-hearing, that *yes*. She passed over the loud-smelling ones. She passed over a powder that made her eyes sting. She put her hands flat on the jars one by one until she found the one that sang loudest — a squat black-glazed pot near the back, sealed in wax — and she worked the wax with her thumbnails, patient, and got it open, and dipped a finger, and tasted.

It was, in fact, the one jar in the room that would drop a horse.

It went down her throat like a hot wire, and her whole small body seized, and for a heartbeat she thought, with genuine interest, *ah, so this is how I die the second time* — and then the warmth came up through the burn, enormous, the most she'd taken since the egg, and her body took the poison and *ate* it. Turned it. Banked it. She sat back on the shelf among the jars with her finger still in her mouth, flushed and glowing faintly and entirely well, and after a moment she dipped her finger again, because waste was a sin.

It had been, she reflected later, almost insultingly easy. And that was the thing that troubled her, walking the long way back to the nursery with the warmth purring in her belly. It troubled her for days.

Because a *protagonist struggles*. That was the rule under all the other rules. The reincarnated hero claws up a mountain of suffering; every reagent is paid for in blood and cunning and the bones of rivals. And her life was — frictionless. She had walked into a poison vault that a child could open, picked the single lethal jar by instinct, drunk a horse's death, and improved. No guard. No trial. No rival lunging from the dark. Even her *power* had come easy, handed to her in a cradle. That was not a hero's data. That was — she turned it over, and didn't like it, and made herself look at it squarely — *set dressing*.

She wasn't the protagonist. She saw it clearly, sitting in the truth of it. She was the *prize*. The kept jade-beauty in the deep palace of the evil sect, the dangling reward, the thing the *real* hero would someday fight his way in to claim. That was why it was all so easy — you don't make the treasure earn its place on the pedestal. You just keep it beautiful and wait for someone to come win it.

She found, examining the feeling, that she was relieved. Heroes had terrible mortality rates. Heroes got tortured in act two. If the realm wanted to file her as the passive reward and stop watching her — well. She had a method, and a poison-vault she could re-open, and a whole evil sect that had just decided she was furniture. A prize that quietly helps itself, she thought, while everyone admires the pedestal, is the most active person in any room. Let them not-win her. She'd be busy.

*To the Citadel, urgent: A jar is short. The black-glazed, the essence-of-monkshood, sealed when I last inventoried it, now broached — and no man dead, no horse dropped, the household entire and hale. I have questioned the still-room boy until he wept. He swears the count was right, swears the levels are down, swears the princess-child glows. The boy is unwell and drinks. I am left with two propositions and I dislike them equally: either this thing cannot poison, or it should not exist. Send a second opinion. Send more parchment.*

⁂

The boy was not believed, of course. Hobb, his name was, the still-room boy, and he kept saying it — *I counted right, I counted twice, the little one was up there, I saw her on the shelf* — and the more he said it the more they pitied him and reached for the wineskin on his behalf. A toddler scaling the still-room. A child glowing. He took to drink in earnest. He was the only person in the whole affair who was completely correct about everything, and it ruined him.

Somebody had to answer for the broached jar, because thieving from the king's own house is the Wall if you're lucky and the block if you're not, and the obvious somebody was the acolyte who'd left the door ajar. He protested. He protested to the floor and the walls and a guardsman who wasn't listening; he had never touched the jar, he didn't even know which jar, he'd only gone for water. None of it weighed anything. Hand of the King set his seal to the sentence — Septon Barth, who at that hour was theorising in his solar that the immune child might be *blessed of the gods, or cursed of them, the symptoms being identical* — and the man was marched north before the moon turned. The Wall took him. The household closed over the gap the way water closes over a stone, and within a fortnight nobody at the Red Keep could have told you his name, and nobody, nobody at all, connected the missing poison to the small grave princess who had begun, that month, to thrive so visibly that her mother wept with relief.

There was a new girl about the maester's rooms in those days — a minor-noble daughter, a few years older than Gael, sent up to court to be aged into a lady-in-waiting, brought through the still-room corridors for her lessons and her health. Wylla, she was called. She was there the day they found the jar short, hovering at the edge of the grown men's anger, and she had — though she could not have said why she was sure — *seen* something. A small shape on the high shelf, the week before, where no small shape should be. She did not say it. The men were already furious; the acolyte was already weeping; saying it would have changed nothing except to put her own self into the centre of a terror she did not understand. So she shut her mouth and watched the acolyte marched out, and felt sick, and buried it. She was eight years old. She had just stood beside the first crime, and chosen, out of fright, to say nothing — which is exactly how every long complicity begins.

Gael never noticed her. Gael, when she noticed her at all in the months after, filed her in the small clay-tablet journal she'd begun to keep — beasts and reagents in a reverent hand she was still mastering, humans by function — under *Asset (hands, legs)*. A useful pair of hands that went where Gael could not yet go. She did not record, because she did not know, that the asset had seen her on the shelf.

Her mother, told that the princess had eaten something that ought to have killed her and instead grown rosy, did not faint or pray. Her mother clasped her hands and declared, radiant, that the child had *a refined palate and a scholar's curiosity* — a connoisseur's stomach, a seeker's mind — and that such gifts must be nourished. And she assigned her daughter a tutor, the most learned man in the Keep, to feed that hungry curiosity.

The maester. The man whose vault she had robbed. Installed now as her personal instructor, with standing leave to be in her company and answer her every question.

Gael accepted the punishment for her crime — which was unlimited supervised access to the storeroom and its keeper — with the grave decorum of a disciple receiving a great and undeserved boon, and added a second title to her quiet collection. The corridors had supplied it, the way they'd supplied the first.

*Poison-Eater.*

Two stars for the genre's plotting, she thought. The prize wasn't supposed to fail upward this hard. But she'd take it.

## I.3 · The Cage

The skulls were enormous and they hummed, and Gael had been edging toward them across the throne-room floor for the better part of an hour, one careful pace at a time, in the spirit-tranced creep of a disciple approaching a forbidden relic.

She was four, perhaps five — she'd lost the thread of it, time being a thing that happened to weaker people — and the great hall of the Red Keep was a forest of dead dragons. They lined the walls, skull upon skull, the small ones the size of hounds and the great ones the size of carriages, Balerion at the head black as a hole in the world, and every one of them *sang*. That under-hearing bell-note, the egg's note, multiplied a hundredfold and old, banked, vast. The whole hall was a treasury and nobody guarded it, which fit her theory of the place exactly: an evil sect leaves its dead spirit-beasts on display, because in an evil sect the display *is* the threat. She did not intend to take anything. Not yet. She only wanted to put her hands on Balerion and *listen*, and learn what a relic that old could teach her about the shape of her own banked fire.

She got within three paces before the guards converged, and then there were large men with their hands under her arms, lifting her away from the black skull, and she went limp and serene in their grip — she'd learned that limp and serene got you set down faster than thrashing — and let them carry her out, having memorised the floor plan, the sightlines, the rotation of the watch, and the precise angle she'd need next time. *Proximity is not access,* she noted, filing the lesson without rancour. *Foiled, observed. Adjust.*

What she had not expected was to be summoned. The sect-master himself. An audience.

She prepared for it the way she prepared for everything, which was thoroughly and on entirely false premises. The patriarch of an evil sect did not call a disciple in for nothing. This would be a *test* — or, better, a bloodline-revelation, the moment the cold elder unbends just far enough to hand the overlooked youngster the forbidden manual that is her birthright. She practised her humble-disciple etiquette in the nursery for two days. She would keep her eyes lowered. She would watch his hands — the manual would pass hand to hand, it always did, a scroll, a jade slip, a pressed seal. She would be ready to receive it.

Jaehaerys received her in a small cold room and looked at her across it for a long time, an old king with a face like a shut door, and then he told her, in a voice with no heat in it at all, that a princess did not climb upon the bones of her forebears. That the dragons of the conquest were not toys. That she would conduct herself, in future, as befit her blood and her House, or there would be consequences, and that he had better things to attend to than the misbehaviour of the youngest of his daughters, of whom — he did not say this, but it stood in the room with them, cold as he was — he expected very little.

Gael lowered her eyes and nodded and said, gravely, that she understood.

She watched his hands the whole time. No manual came. She was, briefly, disappointed.

And then she understood the audience, and her disappointment evaporated, because she'd been reading it as a young disciple and she should have been reading it as a *strategist*. Of course no manual. A manual would have been *too much favour* — lavish, suspect, the kind of attention that gets a rising disciple resented and watched. What the sect-master had given her instead was exactly calibrated: cold, brief, conditional, distant. The precise, measured, deniable acknowledgement that an evil-sect patriarch extends to a disciple he has noticed and means to keep on a long leash. Any warmer would have been a lie; any colder, an insult. This was *patronage*. This was the elder telling her, in the only language his rank permitted, *I see you, I am not done with you, do not embarrass me.* She left the cold room flushed with quiet triumph. Favour secured.

Jaehaerys, watching the small solemn nodding girl led out, noted that she had taken the correction well — docile, chastened, no trouble — and dismissed her from his mind, and never knew that he had just confirmed, to a four-year-old crime-lord, her standing as a made disciple. They both walked out of the cold room satisfied. They were both completely wrong. It was, the room being what it was, almost peaceful.

There had been an empty place at the maester's that whole season — a stool nobody sat on, a name nobody said, a hush where the acolyte had been. Gael had noticed it. She read it correctly as a removal and incorrectly as everything else. *The inner court culled a weak disciple,* she thought; *sect politics, above my station, someone moved against someone.* The lesson she drew was that the sect was more dangerous than she'd reckoned, that powers were moving in the dark, and that she must hide her strength better and grow *colder*, give nothing away, trust no one. She did not connect the empty stool to the broached jar. She did not connect the broached jar to herself. She mistook the wake of her own crime for ambient menace, and let it temper her in precisely the wrong direction, and never once suspected that the reason a man was freezing at the end of the world was four years old and resolving, in his honour, to be more careful with her poisons.

She had also noticed the watcher. The older girl, the one filed *Asset*, was always somewhere at the edge of things now — running errands past her door, eyes on her at the wrong moments, present too often to be chance. Gael, freshly cold, freshly paranoid, drew the obvious conclusion: a monitor-disciple. A spy, planted by some elder to report on the promising youngster. She resolved to feed the spy false impressions, to be boring and pious in the watcher's sightline, and she upgraded the journal entry — *Asset (hands, legs). Surveillance. Manage.* She had correctly identified that the girl mattered. She had inverted, completely, every single thing about *how*. The person she filed as a threat to be deceived was the one who would, in the fullness of a terrible decade, convert and be tempered and kill on her behalf — and the most important relationship of Gael's whole second life began, on her side, as counter-espionage.

She tested the spy once, to be sure. She left a thing where the girl would see it — a scrap of clay-tablet with a few false marks, a meaningless diagram she'd invented to look like a secret — and watched, sidelong, to see whether it would travel up some chain to some elder. It didn't travel anywhere. The girl had glanced at it, and at Gael, and said nothing, and gone on with her sweeping with a face Gael could not read and therefore filed as *trained*. A good spy, she concluded, approving despite herself. Patient. Gives nothing. She would have to be careful with this one. She upgraded the entry a second time — *Asset (hands, legs). Surveillance. Skilled. Manage with care* — and felt the small clean pleasure of an opponent correctly assessed, and was wrong, root and branch, about every part of it. The girl had said nothing because the girl was frightened, and loyal to no one, and already, without a word for it, half in orbit around the strange child she'd been set to watch.

What she did not see — what the chapter was, underneath, entirely about — was that the watcher was caged too.

⁂

*To the Citadel: the doubled lessons. I had thought to weary her into ordinary childhood — septa in the mornings, scripture and stitchery and the duties of a princess; myself in the afternoons, that she might tire of questions. She tires of nothing. She accepts double the burden of any child her age without a flicker of complaint. The septa calls it piety and is unsettled by it. I would call it appetite, were it directed at anything I could name. She studies the cage we have built for her — Faith, marriage, House — with the calm of a clerk taking inventory, and I cannot for my life decide whether she is the most dutiful child in the realm or merely the most patient. More parchment.*

⁂

The thing the maester half-saw and could not name, Gael had seen entire, sitting through the septa's morning lessons with her hands folded and her face still: the *real* cage. Not the guards, not the skulls she couldn't reach — those were nothing, those were a floor plan. The cage was the lessons themselves. Septa Aldith, doubling the hours and crediting herself loudly for the girl's blossoming devotion, was unknowingly teaching her the bars: that a princess was a piece on a board, that her body was a treaty, that there was a marriage coming someday with her name on it and her wishes nowhere in it, that the Faith and the dynasty and the simple fact of being a girl were three walls of a room she had not agreed to enter. *Outward, yes,* Gael concluded, surveying it with that clerk's calm. *Inward, absolutely not.* She rated the Faith's worldbuilding poorly — derivative cosmology, seven gods, no cultivation in it anywhere, a righteous-sect antagonist with very dull doctrine — and she filed the marriage-clause the way she filed everything she could not yet defeat: as a problem of leverage, to be studied, to be routed around, in time, by method.

And Wylla, eleven now and grown quiet, ran the small errands and watched the strange small princess at her serene lessons and said nothing — about the still-room, about the shelf, about any of it, to anyone. Sitting in the cold of the corridor between tasks, she knew without the words for it that her own road was drawn for her too: a daughter of a small house, posted to court to be useful, her marriage and her place arranged in rooms she would never be admitted to. She had no name yet for the kinship she felt with the eerie child she'd been set to wait upon. She only knew they were both being moved by larger hands, and that the princess didn't seem to know it, and that this somehow frightened her more than if she had.

## I.4 · Valyrian Beauty

The sword on the wall of the lower gallery had been singing to Gael for a month, and tonight, at six years old and barefoot in the dark, she had finally come to eat it.

It was Valyrian steel — she didn't have the word, but she had the *note*, and the note was unmistakable: dragon-kin, the same banked-bell hum as the skulls and the egg, folded into a blade. A minor lord's heirloom, hung in a draughty gallery nobody walked at night, guarded by a sleeping man she could step around like a piece of furniture. *Dragon-steel,* she thought, reverent. *Distilled, forged, concentrated. The finest reagent in the Keep.* She had no idea how to eat a sword. She would solve that. She had eaten harder things than they looked.

She got it off the pegs — heavier than it had any right to be, she had to drag it — and she set the edge against her teeth and bit, and her cultivated jaw, which had filed through bone before, met the steel and *did not slip*. It gave. A curl of it came away into her mouth like a shaving of cold dark sugar, and the warmth that came up off it was not warmth at all but a roar, a flood, the biggest single draught since the cradle and the poison put together, and she sat down hard in the dark with the sword across her knees and *drew*.

The breakthrough took her like a wave takes a swimmer. She felt the banked fire in her belly catch and roar and climb, felt some wall inside her she'd been pressing against for two years simply *go*, felt the whole architecture of herself re-settle into a higher shape. Her senses bloomed — she could hear the sleeping guard's heart three rooms off, could see the grain of the floorboards in the dark. And her body changed under her hands as she sat there. She stopped feeling tired. She would, she'd find over the coming weeks, stop *sleeping* much at all — two hours, an hour, none — and never flag. She would find she could go up a sheer wall now with the quiet certainty of a cat, that she could fall and not bruise, that nothing wore her down and very little hurt her. Not a fighter — she had no interest in fighting, fighting was for protagonists — but a child the world could simply no longer get a grip on. *Body refinement,* she thought, glowing in the dark with a Valyrian sword half-eaten in her lap. *Post-breakthrough. Impurities expelled.* She was, frankly, proud of the readout.

She finished as much of the blade as she could carry inside her, propped the ruined rest behind a chest where it might not be found for a year, and went back to bed to consolidate.

The consolidation took weeks, and they were the best weeks of her second life. She'd lie in the dark — she barely needed the bed anymore, sleep had become a thing she visited rather than required — and feel the new realm settling into her, the way a house settles after it's built, small ticks and creaks of her own body reorganising itself around the dragon-steel. She tested the gifts as they came in. She climbed the nursery wall to the ceiling and clung there upside down for an hour out of sheer scientific interest, watching the room from the angle of a spider, and felt no strain and no fear of falling. She held her hand in a candle-flame longer than a hand should go and felt the heat as information rather than pain. She went a full day and night without food and noticed only that she'd forgotten to be hungry. *Tireless,* she catalogued, pleased. *Sleepless. Hard to mark. Hard to wear down.* Not a fighter's gifts — she had no use for those, fighters were protagonists and protagonists died in act two — but a survivor's, a thief's, the gifts of a thing the world could no longer quite get its hands on. She added them to the journal in the reverent hand, under her own name, the only human entry she would ever write with any warmth, and even that was really an entry about a reagent's effects.

The trouble started when people began to look at her.

⁂

*To the Citadel, in some distress: I have a contradiction I cannot dissolve and it is keeping me from sleep. Valyrian steel cannot be destroyed by any forge in the world; it cannot be reworked save by arts we no longer possess; it certainly cannot be — I write the word and feel a fool — eaten. A blade is missing from the lower gallery. The princess was seen, by a guard since transferred for his nerves, "biting at jewellery." These two facts cannot both bear weight. Either the steel was not consumed — and then where is it? — or it was — and then everything I know of the substance is wrong. I find I cannot hold both halves of the thought at once. The framework I have built across six years to contain this child has just, very quietly, come apart in my hands. I have stopped requesting more parchment. I am requesting leave.*

⁂

The court could not stop talking about how beautiful the princess had become.

It was true, and it was uncanny. Six years old and the breakthrough had remade her — skin like the inside of a shell, hair pale as poured silver, a stillness and a symmetry to her that made grown courtiers trail off mid-sentence and forget what they'd been saying. The most beautiful child in the realm, they said, and meant it, and felt obscurely frightened as they said it, because it was *too much*, it was Valyrian perfection past anything the line had bred in three generations. Her mother was, for the first time, simply and uncomplicatedly *proud* — a beautiful daughter, a normal thing, a thing a mother could enjoy without dread — and Alicent let herself enjoy it for almost a whole season before the curdle set in.

Gael, naturally, misread the attention entirely. She knew the breakthrough had remade her body; she'd done it on purpose; the beauty was just the surface readout of a refined core. What worried her was *concealment*. A leap that big put out a signature; a rival cultivator would feel it, would come sniffing for the disciple who'd jumped a realm overnight. She watched the court for the rival, armed against cultivation-espionage, and entirely failed to identify the actual attention she was drawing, which was not a rival sensing qi. It was a man.

Prince Daemon had begun to circle.

He was much older and he was charming and he found reasons to be where the beautiful small princess was — a hand on her shoulder that stayed a beat too long, a smile pitched just for her, a habit of telling her how *special* she was, how rare, how the blood they shared set them apart from common things. To anyone watching with clear eyes it was plain and it was sick, an older man's regard settling on a child, and the court watched with clear eyes and looked away and said nothing, because he was a prince, and the realm did not see fit to make trouble over the way a prince looked at a girl.

Gael watched him with cultivation-poisoned eyes and saw a sect weirdo.

She had run the obvious reading and discarded it. *Romance?* — she nearly laughed. Enemy-to-lovers, with a *cousin*, two of the most overused tropes in the genre stacked on top of each other; absolutely not; she had a do-not-do list and that was at the top of it. So she crossed off romance and was left with a residue she had no clean trope for — a creepy, hovering, over-fond elder who kept cornering her to murmur about bloodline — and an evil sect, she reasoned, was simply *full* of weirdos. Half the elders in any decent novel were unsettling. She filed him under harmless background cult-flavour and stopped watching him as a threat.

Worse — and this is the part the court would not have believed of a child, and the reader could not unsee — she started *using* him. An indulgent, important elder who sought out her company was access. Was cover. Was a door held open. She leveraged his interest with the bland competence of a junior employee managing a senior stakeholder, smiling up at the man circling her, asking her small clever questions, letting him feel special so that he'd keep being useful, and never once — not for an instant — clocking what she was actually standing inside of. A six-year-old cheerfully exploiting a predator's regard as a resource, because she'd filed the predator under *sect weirdo* and moved on.

And it paid. Charmed, indulgent, he began to bring her things — a string of small gifts across the months, old Valyrian trinkets, a worn finger-ring, a melted-looking pendant, a hair-pin black and dense. Baubles to him. To her: *reagents*. Every one of them sang the dragon-note; every one was a compatible source she could draw down at her leisure; one made a fine focus for meditation. She pocketed them, delighted, and angled for more, reading a grooming pattern as a standing supply line and the supply line as patronage — *the favour of the evil sect, the gifts a rising disciple attracts.* Exactly like the king's audience two years gone, she was pleased and not wary. The same lens. The same beautiful blind spot. The sect-weirdo was a free material stream, and she meant to keep him producing.

The whispered name had spread, too — *Dragonbane*, on more lips now, in lower voices — and she misheard that exactly as she misheard everything. *My dao-name,* she thought, a little pleased; *my cultivation reputation precedes me; a fearsome epithet, well earned.* She added it to the collection with the others and never caught the thing the realm actually meant by it, the dread under it, the way they were not calling her a feared cultivator at all but something else, something they whispered in temples, a word she'd have rejected on genre principle if she'd ever heard it spoken plain.

The only person who'd reported the truth — the guard who'd seen her gnawing the heirloom, who'd said plainly that the princess was *biting the jewellery* — had been laughed at, and then, when he wouldn't stop saying it, quietly transferred somewhere his nerves wouldn't trouble the household. Two for two, the Cassandra. Right again. Believed by no one again. He went where they sent him, and the framework that should have caught a child eating Valyrian steel walked out the gate behind him, and the maester upstairs put his face in his hands and requested leave.

## I.5 · The Bond-Breaker

The dagger took the better part of a night to file down, and Gael had nearly finished — the king's heirloom blade reduced to a glittering pile of dragon-steel shavings she'd swallowed one pinch at a time — when Prince Daemon found her alone in the empty gallery and gave her, at last, a real lesson.

Some servants would answer for the dagger. She knew that abstractly, the way she knew the sun would rise; a thing went missing from the king's rooms, a thing always answered for it, and it was never her. She'd stopped finding this remarkable. She filed the dagger under *consumed* and the eventual blamed servants under nothing at all, and was sweeping the last shavings into her palm when she heard him in the doorway and turned, and there he was, the sect-weirdo, the supply line, smiling down at her with a light in his eyes she'd never bothered to read.

He'd been drinking. He came in close — too close, in her space, crouching down to her level so that she could smell the wine — and he started in on the old refrain, but warmer tonight, hungrier, his voice gone low and intimate. How *special* she was. How rare. How the blood of old Valyria ran in the two of them and in almost no one else now, how the dragonlords had kept that blood pure and close *for a reason*, how a thing as rare as what they shared should not be wasted on common notions of — and his hand was on her arm, and his face was very near hers, and what he was doing was unmistakable to anyone in the world except the one person it was happening to.

Gael's genre-brain lit up like a struck match.

*Bloodline-resonant partners. Shared rare blood. Circulating together.* He was — she could barely believe her luck — he was offering her *dual cultivation*. The real thing, the technique from the higher manuals: two compatible cores, blood-matched, drawing and circulating qi between them to break through faster than either could alone. Platonic, of course — she had no interest in the romance-trope nonsense and this plainly wasn't that, this was *technique*, an elder finally sharing the advanced art she'd earned through patient patronage. She had been waiting for this since the cold king failed to hand her a manual. The sect-weirdo, of all people, was coming through.

She was so delighted she ran it then and there.

She reached for his core the way the higher manuals described — found the resonant thing in him, the bright living current that matched her own dragon-note, the thing she'd been feeling off him for months and mistaking for the source of his trinkets' power — and she opened herself and *drew*, the way you draw on a willing partner, nine revolutions, take and circulate and give back.

She took. She did not give back. There was nothing in her built to give back; she had only ever learned to draw.

What she had her grip on was not a cultivation core. It was his bond — the living tether that ran out of the man and across the city to the great red dragon that was half his soul — and she pulled on it the way she pulled on everything, hard, greedy, all at once, and it did not circulate. It *tore*.

It came loose into her like a riptide. Years of another life poured up the broken tether and into her belly in the space of a few breaths, an infusion so vast it dwarfed the sword, dwarfed the poison, dwarfed everything, and the wall inside her went again, a second breakthrough on the heels of the first, and she sat down in it, glowing, *roaring* inside, the richest she had ever been.

Daemon made a sound she didn't have a category for and folded down onto the gallery floor.

And across the Keep, with no wound and no warning, a half-mile from any living soul, Caraxes went mad.

She glimpsed it through the gallery's high window, drawn by the noise — the great red dragon thrashing on its chain-yard far below, screaming, beating its head against the stone, the dragonkeepers swarming out with chains and goads and terror in every line of them, hauling the maddened thing down by main force as it raked the air and shrieked at nothing. It was a horror. It would not stop. They would chain it in the dark before the night was out, and it would never be ridden again, and the men who'd raised it from the egg would lie awake the rest of their lives over the hour their sweet beast simply turned.

Gael watched it for a moment through the glass, mildly.

*A kindred spirit-beast,* she filed, *lost to a qi-deviation.* Pity, in a clinical sort of way — spirit-beasts were kin, worth a grave bestiary entry, and she'd give it one in the reverent hand she reserved for beasts and reagents. Then she looked down at Daemon, slack and hollowed on the floor, breathing but gone somehow behind the eyes, and she filed *him* too, in the flat plain hand she reserved for people: *an elder who lost his cultivation base.* A stat-change. Recoverable, in-genre, more or less. She felt a detached, distant pity, and underneath it — she audited herself honestly and found it there — a small clean relief, because the predator she'd never named had stopped circling. She gave herself a ⑥ credit for restraint: she'd only run the technique the once.

She did not flinch. There was nothing in her that flinched. The reader, watching her sit in a maddened dragon's screaming and a gutted man's silence and feel *neither*, carried all of it, the whole weight, alone — which is precisely how the gallery was built.

She had a problem, and she addressed it with characteristic focus. *Was that the demonic path, or not?* She genuinely could not tell. The technique had worked — gloriously, enormously, she was rich beyond reckoning now — but it had *cost* the partner everything, which was the textbook signature of the demonic drain, the forbidden art that steals what it cannot share. She did not feel guilty. Guilt did not enter. She felt a methodological uncertainty, and she resolved it the way she resolved everything: she booked a flat rule. *That may have been demonic. Therefore I will not use that technique again, on principle.* The line went where it always went — at the *method*, never the *harm*. She had not done a wrong thing; she had possibly used a wrong tool, and a righteous cultivator did not use wrong tools, and so by declining to do it twice she filed herself, with perfect serenity, among the good ones. She did not give back the gains. One did not give back gains. She'd keep the power and swear off the path that bought it, and call the keeping clean — the doctrine the whole rest of her life would run on, drawn flat and white in the gallery floor while a man breathed shallowly beside her and a dragon screamed itself hoarse across the city.

⁂

*To the Citadel — and I no longer expect to be believed, so I write for the record only. Caraxes is mad. No wound. No fever. No mark on him the keepers can find. Sweet-tempered an hour before; gone wild and chained within it; he will not be ridden again. The same hour, in the same Keep, the prince Daemon was found insensible on a gallery floor and is now near bed-ridden, hollowed, his fire gone out of him, no physician able to say why. And the same hour the princess Gael — "Dragonbane," the smallfolk are calling her now, and they do not mean it kindly, and I am beginning to wonder whether they are simply right — was alone in that gallery and is entirely well. I cannot prove a thing. I cannot even shape an accusation; there is no blade, no poison, no wound, nothing a man could put in a writ. I have asked that she be confined to her chambers. I told the Queen it was for the child's protection. I told myself it was to study her. The truth is I am afraid of what walks loose between her and a dragon, and I want a locked door between it and the rest of us until I understand it. I will not understand it. More parchment.*

⁂

The realm, which could not make a writ, made a story instead.

It made several. A locked-room theft with no thief, no blade, a timeline that seemed to *shrink* the harder they looked at it, drove the investigators to a phantom master-criminal of impossible subtlety; they hunted a ghost. The dragonkeepers, finding no illness and no injury in a beast that had simply *turned*, chained Caraxes in the deep dark and slept badly and theorised about bad water, bad meat, a hidden wasting sickness, anything. The court at large preferred a conspiracy worthy of the catastrophe — a rival House, a slow poison, a Faceless Man bought with Lannister gold — anything but the truth, which was four feet tall and confined to her chambers and mildly, exclusively preoccupied with whether the technique she'd run, *specifically the technique*, had been demonic. Not the dragon. Certainly not the man. Two stars, she thought of the dagger investigation, distantly offended at how sloppy it was, how she hadn't even had to engineer the cover-up. The scandal-burying machinery of a great House laundered the whole night down into *the beast took ill* before the week was out, and the executed servants who answered for the filed-down dagger went into the dark and never troubled her journal at all.

The confinement, she received as a gift.

She'd resolved on closed-door meditation to diagnose herself — to sit with the enormous new fire and work out, at leisure, whether she'd deviated onto the demonic path — and the maester, terrified, walked up and *granted her exactly that*, locking her in her own chambers for fear of a contagion that turned dragons feral. She read the locked door as the caring sect granting a promising disciple secluded-cultivation space to recover from a qi-deviation; more evil-sect favour, exactly when she needed it. He read the locked door as a quarantine on something that drove great beasts mad. They got, both of them, precisely what they wanted, and were both, as ever, completely wrong about the other.

And shut in, for the first time in her life, she found she had company she could not avoid.

The maids. Wylla chief among them — fourteen now, sent to tend the strange confined princess through her "illness," carrying the trays, covering the long hours of silent meditation, asking no questions about why the girl never seemed to sleep or tire. And Gael, with for once nothing to steal and nowhere to climb and no heist to run, *attended* to her. Found, to her own faint surprise, that the asset was a person, dry and watchful and brave, who smuggled in the things Gael was starved for — harbour gossip, ship-talk, what a coin did down at the docks, the names of the captains and the cargoes. The enforced closeness warmed the bond hard, in a way no scheme of Gael's would ever have allowed; she'd never have *chosen* to sit still long enough to like someone. The chambermaids who waited on her began, in those shut-in weeks, to orbit her too, half from duty and half from the strange pull of her. The first real fuel went onto the slow fire that would, in the end, make Wylla hers entirely.

Gael, of course, did not know she was being warmed toward. She knew she was reclassifying an asset. The journal, opened on her knee in the locked room, recorded a grave and reverent entry for Caraxes — *kindred, deviated, lost* — and not one word for the prince she'd hollowed, who'd brought her trinkets across a winter and pressed his special blood on her and been *literalised into ruin* for it, his supply line and his menace both ended in an afternoon by a child who had never once read him as a man. The favour was repaid in full. She had no idea there had been a favour, or a debt, or a man.

She closed the journal. She sat down in the centre of the warm fire that another life had paid for. She drew nine slow revolutions, and addressed herself, with serene scholarly diligence, to the only question in the world that interested her: whether she might, possibly, have done something a little bit demonic.

She concluded, after long meditation, that on a technicality she had not.

## Interlude · Alicent

Alicent had stopped going into her daughter's chambers without warning, and the morning she understood *why* she had stopped was the morning she finally let herself be afraid.

She'd meant only to look in. The maester's quarantine was three weeks old; the locked door had become a thing the whole Keep stepped quietly past; and a mother had a right to look in on a sick child. So she'd had it opened, soft, expecting to find the girl asleep, or feverish, or fretful — expecting, she realised afterward, to find a *child*. And instead Gael had been sitting bolt upright in the centre of the cold floor in the grey before dawn, open-eyed, perfectly still, breathing so slow and shallow that for one stopped heartbeat Alicent had thought her dead — and the girl had turned her head, and her eyes had not quite tracked, had taken a half-second too long to find her mother's face, and in that half-second Alicent had looked at her own daughter and not known what she was looking at.

She'd smiled, and said something about breakfast, and gone out, and stood in the corridor with her hand against the cold stone until her heart slowed.

*What is my daughter?*

She had been asking it for six years and dressing it up each time as something gentler. *She's contemplative.* (A babe who lay open-eyed like a little corpse practising; she'd shouted at the nurse who said it and then dreamed it for a month.) *A refined palate, a scholar's curiosity.* (A child who ate a poison that should have killed a horse and grew rosy on it.) *She's strong* — oh, she'd loved saying *she's strong*, she'd built a whole tenderness on it, her brave clever girl who killed a dragon in her cradle and lived. She had armed that cradle herself. That was the thing she could not get past, in the corridor, with her hand on the stone. *She* had fought the King for the egg. Jaehaerys had said no — no dragon for that one, a girl, the youngest, not worth the dragonkeepers' trouble — and Alicent had wept and argued and won, had put the gift in the cradle with her own hands because she could not bear her daughter to be the one princess without a dragon. And four days later the hatchling was dead and the babe was changed, and somewhere in the marrow of her, in a place with no words, Alicent had known ever since that *she had done it.* That whatever her daughter was becoming, she, the loving mother, had handed it the first rung.

So she loved her, and she was losing her to something she could not name, and she did the only thing left to a woman who could neither understand the danger nor turn from the child: she stood between them. She made herself a shield, and she fought a quiet war on two fronts that her daughter would never know was being fought at all.

The first front was her husband.

Jaehaerys had filed Gael *worthless* years ago and had not revised it since — a strange, cold, troublesome girl, no use to the House, a thing to be disposed of cleanly — and now, after the maddened dragon and the gutted prince, a worse word had begun to move behind his teeth. He did not say it to Alicent. He didn't have to. She heard it in the new flatness of his voice when the child came up, in the way his eyes went to her and away: *kinslayer.* A blighter of the blood. A thing that turned dragons feral and left princes hollow and might, left to grow, turn its hand to worse. And the King's answer to a thing he could neither use nor trust was the King's answer to everything — *price it, sell it, be rid of it.* He meant to wring her one last worth before he let her go: parade the uncanny jewel a while, inflate the legend, drive up the match, and then marry her off, far away, to whoever paid most and lived furthest, and call the realm well rid. A betrothal was coming. Alicent could feel the clock of it starting. She worked him — a wife's whole leverage spent across a hundred small conversations — not to *stop* it, she could not stop it, but to keep the sentence a *marriage* and not the colder thing the new word in his mouth implied. She bought her daughter's life one room at a time, and Gael, behind her locked door, drawing her serene revolutions, never knew her mother was bargaining for it.

The second front was the realm's mind.

Septon Barth — Hand of the King, the cleverest man in Westeros, the one mind in all the world equipped to look at the evidence and *theorise* what her daughter truly was — had been circling closer for years, half-charmed and half-troubled, reading the maester's increasingly frantic letters with a seriousness no one else granted them. He was one fact short. He was always one fact short; the substance that could not be eaten, the wound that left no mark, the immunity that read identical to a blessing — every theory he built collapsed at the edge, and Alicent labored to keep it collapsing. She redirected his curiosity into safer channels. She let the maester's wildest letters go *astray* before they climbed the chain to him. She made her daughter *boring on parchment* — a pious, sickly, studious girl, nothing to write home about — so that the one man who might have caught her would stay forever a fact short of the truth.

But the thing she guarded hardest, the thing she spent herself most to keep out, was not the King's word or the Hand's theory. It was the *whispers*.

Because the realm had made up its own mind, and its mind was the strangest thing of all. The smallfolk and the septons and the old women in the temples had taken the same facts the King filed as *kinslayer* and the maester filed as *contagion*, and read them backward into wonder. A dragon driven mad with no wound and chained in the dark. A prince hollowed and never the same. The most beautiful child the Valyrian line had bred in living memory. A name — *Dragonbane* — that they did not say as a curse anymore but as an *omen*. And out of all of it the realm had quietly, gobsmackedly crowned her: not a blighter but a *promised one*, blood-of-the-dragon incarnate, a prophesied figure whispered about in temples, an omen-child come for some great turning of the wheel. The court reeled at the spectacle of a King throwing away — selling cheap to a northern nobody — a girl the realm was beginning, in the same breath, to revere and to dread.

Alicent knew about the whispers. She heard them everywhere now, under the ordinary noise of the Keep, and she made it her whole work that they would never, ever reach her daughter.

Half of it was protection, and she knew the cold arithmetic of it exactly. A *revered* child is a *used* child — a banner, a weapon, a thing factions kill over and kill for. A *feared* child is a *dead* child, the moment fear outweighs awe. Either way the whisper that reached Gael was the whisper that ended her, and so the whisper would not reach her; Alicent would see to it; she would stand in the door of every rumour and turn it back. And half of it — she was honest with herself, in the grey dawns, because there was no one else to be honest with — half of it was not protection at all. Half of it was that she could not *bear* it. Could not bear for her strange still daughter to hear the realm call her a legend while her own mother still, desperately, needed her to be a *daughter*. A daughter you could fail to understand and love anyway. A legend you could only lose.

So she sealed it. All of it. The three readings of her daughter drifted past one another in the Keep that winter and never once touched — Gael behind her door filing herself a side-character prize quietly plotting not to be won; the King filing her worthless and pricing the sale; the realm haloing her as the prophesied one — three castings, every one of them wrong, and the woman who stood at the center of all three the only person alive who saw all three at once and could correct none of them. She was the seal. The asymmetry held because she held it. The legend grew without the girl, the King's contempt grew without the girl, and the girl grew on inside her own delusion, serene and oblivious and dangerous, building a world with no room in it for any of them.

Alicent won her small war. She would go on winning it, room by room, for years. The betrothal stayed a betrothal and not the colder thing. Barth stayed a fact short. The new word stayed behind the King's teeth. She kept the noise out and the door shut and the legend sealed, and she did it for love, knowing — half-knowing, the way she half-knew everything about this child — that the thing she was shielding so fiercely was becoming something the realm would one day be right to fear.

And she lost the only thing that had ever been the point. Because the daughter was already gone. Not to the betrothal, not yet — to the locked room, the slow revolutions, the world being built behind those eyes that took a half-second too long to find her face. Pulling inward, sealing herself the way her mother was sealing the realm out, leaving no room. Alicent would shield her all the way to the end. She would spend herself to the last and never be thanked, never be known, never once be seen as the shield — and when Gael finally went, slipped the cage and sailed out of the story written for her, she would go without ever learning that the one camera in all the world that loved her most, and saw her most clearly, was the one that had stood in the door her whole life and kept the truth from reaching her.

The mother looked in clear-eyed and saw a monster, and loved it anyway, and shut the door softly so as not to wake it.

"She's only contemplative," Alicent told the maester in the corridor, with a smile she had practised for six years. "A philosopher. You'll see."

She believed it the way you believe a thing you have decided to die rather than stop believing.

Behind the door, the philosopher drew nine slow revolutions, and grew.
