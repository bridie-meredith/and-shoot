# BOOK II — *the blind machine*

## II.1 · The Recluse

<p align="center">⁂</p>

Wylla had been told the princess wanted harbor records, which was the first strange thing, and that she wanted them brought to the solar and not the library, which was the second, and that she wanted them at dawn, which Wylla privately thought was a courtesy to no one. She carried the rolls up the cold stair with her breath smoking and her arms aching and found the strange one already awake, sitting cross-legged on the floor in a square of grey light, spine very straight, doing nothing at all.

"You can put them there," said Gael, without opening her eyes.

Wylla put them there. She was sixteen and the princess was six, and by every law of the world this should have been the simplest arrangement in the Keep — a bedmaid for a small girl — except that the small girl frightened the older maids so badly they had begun trading away their shifts, and the duty had landed, the way unwanted duties do, on the one with the least standing to refuse it. Wylla's father was a landed knight three days' ride from anywhere that mattered. She had been sent to court to be useful and to be seen, and instead she had been given a child who collected the customs-manifests of Lannisport the way other children collected ribbons, and who was looking at her now with the flat, patient interest of a much older person deciding whether you were going to be a problem.

"Sit," said Gael. "I'll explain the realm to you. You've earned it."

Wylla had not asked to have the realm explained to her. She sat.

What followed she would never afterward be able to repeat, though she tried, once, to a septa, and got halfway through *spirit-roots* and *the nine revolutions* and *the demonic sect that runs this court* before the septa's face closed like a door and Wylla learned not to. The princess spoke of the King's court as an evil cultivation order that had reincarnated her — Wylla did not know that word and Gael did not slow to teach it — into the body of its least-favored disciple, and of the egg she had eaten as a relic, and of a thing called *qi* that she drew into her belly in the mornings, nine turns, which was the doing-nothing Wylla had walked in on. She said all of it pleasantly, the way you'd describe the weather to a guest, and none of it as though she expected to be believed, which somehow made it worse.

"The cells beneath us," Gael said, pointing down through the floor with one small finger, "are where they take the disciples who fail. The black cells. A righteous sect would have a discipline hall and a redemption trial. This one just has a hole." She said it with the dispassionate approval of a critic noting a competent if uninspired choice. "Derivative, but it sets the tone. Two stars. You'll have seen them take people down there."

Wylla had. A scullion last winter, for a theft he had not done. He had not come back up. She had not, until this moment, let herself put the matter so plainly, and she found that hearing a six-year-old say it aloud was not the same as thinking it, and was much worse.

"Yes," Wylla said. Her voice did something strange in the middle of the word.

"Don't fret over it." Gael had noticed the strange thing the voice did and misread it, the way she misread everything, as squeamishness rather than grief. "Mortals are always going down the hole. It's the whole point of a hole. The trick is to not be a mortal." She tilted her head, considering Wylla with that flat patient interest. "I haven't decided about you yet."

*She is mad,* Wylla thought, with great clarity, *and she is mine, and there is no one I can tell.*

And then the princess opened her eyes, and they were that pale violet the Valyrians had, the color the whole court had started lowering its voice around, and she said, "You don't believe a word. That's correct. You shouldn't. You're a mortal; this is above your station. But you're *my* mortal now, and I keep my people fed." She said it kindly. Wylla felt the bottom drop very gently out of her stomach, because no one had ever offered to keep her, and she understood in that moment, with a horror she could not have named, that she was going to be loyal to this child, and that it was already too late to choose otherwise.

<p align="center">⁂</p>

The court assembled to look at her, and Gael let them.

She had been instructed — by Otto, through the King, through a chamberlain who would not meet her eye — to stand upon the dais, to be silent, to say nothing whatever, and above all to remain still. She found the instruction delightful. A whole hall of the evil sect's elders had been gathered to honor their prized jade-disciple, and they had even, thoughtfully, forbidden her to break her meditation by speaking. She drew the morning's warmth down into her belly, nine revolutions, standing perfectly upright in cloth-of-silver with her hands folded, and was so visibly serene that a knight near the doors crossed himself.

This, she understood, was a *display*. The sect was advertising her. She did not begrudge it. A radiant disciple was a credit to her order, and an order that had spent six years and a Valyrian egg on her cultivation was entitled to show off the return. She knew herself beautiful now — the glow-up had come on in the last year, the silver hair, the unsettling skin, the eyes — and she read the beauty exactly as she read everything: a power-level readout, a number gone up. She preened at it the way you'd preen at a clean cauldron. Then she remembered none of it mattered, because she was leaving — bailing out of this whole derivative court-intrigue plot before its true protagonist arrived and slaughtered the lot of them, the way protagonists did — and the preening folded back into boredom, and the boredom looked, to the watching hall, exactly like the contempt of something not quite human.

The procession went by her in slow tides. Lords she would never learn the names of bowed to the depth their rank required and watched her over the bow for any flicker of warmth, and found none, and went away murmuring. A Reach girl her own age was brought up to stand near her a moment, for contrast or for luck, and stared at Gael's eyes until her nurse drew her back. A septon blessed the air a careful arm's length from her. None of it reached her. She had set herself a standing meditation — feet rooted, weight even, the warmth coming down nine turns and pooling low — and the hall full of staring elders served only to deepen it, because it is easier to sit still inside a held breath than inside an empty room, and the silence-rule meant no one would jar her out of the count. *A gift,* she thought. *They've given me a hall to cultivate in and called it a duty. Considerate, for a wicked sect.*

The King regarded his daughter from the high seat and saw, with cold satisfaction, a jewel that would price out very well in the North.

And then there was a commotion near the western arch, and a man's voice, too loud, denouncing her.

Gael did not, properly, know Prince Daemon, except as a sect-weirdo with useful gifts who had once gotten too close and come away maddened — she had filed the incident flat and kept the power and never thought of it again. She was therefore mildly interested to see him now, thinner than she remembered and shouting, while the lords on either side of him stepped not toward him but very slightly away, the way you step away from a man who has begun to smell.

"*Demon,*" Daemon was saying, pointing at her with a hand that was not quite steady. "Look at it and tell me you don't see it. Maegi. Sorceress. That is no child of this house — "

A title.

Gael received it the way a dry sponge receives water. *Maegi.* She turned it over. *Sorceress.* She had collected *Dragonbane* and *Poison-Eater,* and they were fine, they were respectable, but this — *Maegi* had a *dao* in it, a whole secret-arts lineage, the kind of name an immortal earned over centuries and a mortal only got to fear. A diminished elder of the sect, evidently jealous, had just elevated her in front of the whole order, and from the way the others were laughing behind their hands at *him* and not at *her,* the elevation had landed. She inclined her head a precise quarter-inch in his direction — *gratitude, junior to senior* — which made him scream something else, and she entered *Maegi / Sorceress* under earned titles, current rank, and went back to her revolutions.

Across the hall her mother watched the whole thing and felt the floor of her composure crack.

Alicent had told herself the parade was harmless. She had told herself the reading and the harbor-rolls were a mercy — *she's taken up scholarship, thank the Mother, a seemly thing, a quiet thing* — and had believed it for almost a week. But she watched the court drink her daughter in, watched the very reverence she had spent six years smothering swell up again off the dais like heat off a stone, and she understood two things at once and could fix neither. The first was that the King was not protecting Gael by showing her; he was *selling* her, and the showing was the price-tag. The second was worse, and arrived as she was already turning to go: that she could not take her daughter away from any of it. She had dreamed, lately, of Dragonstone — of the salt and the quiet and her strange girl safe behind black walls. But a princess belonged to the King, not the Queen. The one door out had been a painted door all along, and the wall behind it did not move.

By nightfall the kitchens had it three different ways. *The mad prince called her a demon to the King's own face,* a scullion told a potboy, *and she only smiled.* — *Smiled?* — *Smiled, and bowed to him, like he'd paid her a compliment. That's the wrong of it. A natural child would weep. She thanked him.* The potboy crossed himself with a greasy hand, and word of the smile and the bow went out through the under-Keep faster than the parade had gone through the hall, and where the lords had seen a discredited man embarrass himself, the servants — who were never wrong about these things, and were wrong about this — saw a girl receive the word *demon* as her due. The maegi-rumor, which Daemon had meant as a weapon, set in the city's mouth like a tooth, and began to do, quietly, for free, the intimidating that Gael would otherwise have had to do herself.

Daemon was being escorted from the hall, still talking. Gael, eyes shut, drew the warmth down nine more turns and decided that the new title would look very well in the bestiary.

## II.2 · The House

The counting-house of the man they called Hobb the Salt — no relation to her Hobb, the still-room boy, though Gael did not know either of them by surname and would have cared about neither — had three guards on the door, a dog in the yard, and a strongbox sunk in the floor under the moneylender's own bed, and Gael walked out of it before dawn with the better part of its contents and an opinion that the formation-array protecting the vault had been, frankly, derivative.

She had drugged the dog from the well-bucket and the guards from the wineskin they shared at the changing of the watch, both doses out of the still-room, both measured to read the next morning as ordinary heavy sleep — the way the manuals never bothered to teach you, because the manuals assumed you'd just be strong. A six-year-old in a servant's brown shift was the most invisible thing in King's Landing; she had walked in through a coal-hatch a grown thief could not have used and would never have thought to look for, which was, she reflected, rather the point. The novels were full of master infiltrators who were always somehow *large.* It had never occurred to any of them to simply be a child.

She had read the strongbox's wards — the false bottom, the trip-bar, the second box inside the first — as a third-rate sealing-formation laid down by a competent but uninspired array-master, and she had taken it apart kneeling on the floor in the dark with a pin and a wire and the patience of someone who had four hundred lifetimes of bad novels and one of real chemistry behind her eyes. The trip-bar she found by the faint wrongness in how the lid sat; the false bottom by tapping; the inner box she lifted out whole and opened later, at her leisure, in her own rooms, because there was no reason to do delicate work in a dead man's bedroom when you could do it at a desk. The poor disciple robs the wicked sect to fund her ascent. It was practically a *requirement* of the genre. That the wicked sect in this instance was a crime syndicate, the only one in King's Landing organized enough to be worth the trouble, and that a robbed smuggler-boss could no more report his losses than fly, she registered only as the sect being conveniently weak in its mortal protections. That she had, in the same stroke, decapitated her only competition before she had even named her enterprise — that was not a thing she thought about, because she did not think she had competition. She had a *Dao.*

The Faith's coffers she took a week later, and felt even less. She had known the septons and septas all her life as the court's tiresome rival order — an orthodox sect, righteous-pathed, forever pressing its inferior scriptures on better cultivators — and reclaiming a war-chest from an inferior-path rival raised no flag in the moral self-audit she ran, carefully, the way she ran all of them. *Am I robbing the innocent?* No: a rival sect. *Am I taking from the weak?* No: from an order with more gold than the crown. She cleared herself on both counts and was mildly gratified, as she always was, to find herself blameless. The Faith, for its part, discovered the theft, calculated what it would cost in standing to admit that the Seven had let themselves be robbed, and buried the loss so deep that no inquisitor was ever sent. Gael had counted on exactly this and called it the sect's pride. The realm called it nothing, because the realm never knew.

With the founding capital in hand she transmitted the Dao of Games and Fortune.

There was, she had noticed, a *vacuum* in King's Landing — drink, whores, the bear-pits, and nothing for a man with coin who wanted his blood up and his purse at risk in a clean, repeatable way. The city was simply too young to have invented the gaming-hall, and Gael, sole living heir to the half-remembered formats of four hundred read-and-forgotten novels, brought them down out of the air like a patriarch transmitting a lost art. She set the house-edge herself, with the only real mathematics anyone in Westeros had ever applied to the problem, and she explained it to Wylla once, gravely, as a cosmological law: *the house is fate, and fate does not lose.* She built a stable of fighting-beasts and doped and conditioned them out of the still-room, reading each animal's vigor the way she read a reagent, and she set the chambermaids — Pella, Nona, the timid pious one, Marra — to walk the floor and take the coin, because a princess could not, and a noble lady-in-waiting could not, but a clever commoner could, and the buyers would think they dealt with the commoner and never look higher.

It worked the way she said fate worked. The house took its edge off every wager, patiently, the way the sea takes a cliff, and within two moons the converted cellar under a dead-end wynd in Flea Bottom was turning more coin in a night than the bear-pits turned in a week, and men who had never in their lives had a clean way to ruin themselves found one, and were grateful. Gael watched the numbers come up the channel to her in Wylla's careful hand and felt the deep contentment of a law confirmed by experiment. It did not lose.

And then there was the matter of the skimmer.

His name was not important and Gael did not learn it. He was a man Wylla had taken on to keep the dice-tables honest, and he had been quietly bleeding the count for a month, a little off each night, the way a man does when he has decided the operation is too strange and too profitable to ever come looking. Wylla brought it to the princess the way she brought everything now, in a low voice in the solar, expecting — she realized later she did not know what she had been expecting. A reprimand for the man. A docking. A frightening word.

Gael listened. She was sitting at the window with a chart across her knees, and she did not look up from it.

"How much," she said.

Wylla told her — it was not, in the scheme of the take, very much, and she said so, half hoping the smallness of it would make the thing go away. "A few stags a night. He thinks no one counts. I only caught it because the table totals ran sweet against the door totals three weeks running."

"You count beautifully," said Gael, with genuine warmth, and Wylla felt the compliment land and was ashamed of how much she wanted it. "That's exactly right. The door total is fate; the table total is the man's hand in the bowl. Good." She made a small mark on her chart. "Outer disciples need discipline. Don't trouble yourself further with it."

"What does that — " Wylla began, and then did not finish, because she had already learned, somewhere underneath where she let herself know things, that she did not want the sentence completed. "Yes, princess."

There was one man in the Keep who saw the shape of it and said so, and was, as ever, not believed. Hobb — the still-room boy from the old days, a watchman now, who had twice seen the truth of the princess and twice been called a liar for it — caught sight of her one grey dawn coming back in through the coal-hatch in a servant's brown shift, small and certain and entirely where no princess should be, and he stood very still in the shadow of the buttery and did not call out, because he had learned what calling out cost him. He told one man, later, a friend, over ale: *I saw the princess crawl out of a coal-hole at dawn like a thief.* The friend laughed at him. *The boy who cried demon,* the friend said, not unkindly, and bought him another cup, and that was the end of it. Hobb stopped telling people things. It was the only lesson the Keep had ever managed to teach him.

The man was dead within three days. The wrong tea, taken at the wrong table; a soft word from a smallfolk girl who did not know what she was carrying or why. He simply stopped coming.

<p align="center">⁂</p>

It was Wylla who confirmed it, and Wylla who carried the confirming up to the solar, and stood in the doorway afterward and understood that she was looking at a corpse.

Not a threat. Not a rival cowed, not an enforcer leaned on and sent away grey — those she had begun, God help her, to find ordinary. A *body.* A grown man who three days ago had been a few stags a night in a ledger and was now nothing at all, gone out of the world, and the child who had ended him had not raised her voice or her hand or even, visibly, her interest. The princess had looked up from her chart when Wylla said it, with mild distraction, and remarked only that she hoped the body had not been left somewhere troublesome — the novels never told you what to do with the bodies, she said, and she found the logistics tedious — and gone back to her work. *Put out by the mess.* Wylla felt the horror go through her clean and cold, and underneath it, worse, the first dreadful drop of understanding: this was why the floor obeyed. This was why grown men took orders from a maid half their age. The unseen thing behind the maid would, if you crossed it, simply remove you from the world, blithely, between one line of accounts and the next, and be mildly annoyed about the tidying. *Ask nicely* worked because the alternative had now been demonstrated.

"Was that necessary," Wylla heard herself say, before she could stop the words.

Gael considered it as a genuine question, which Wylla found, somehow, more frightening than if she had brushed it off. "He was stealing," the princess said, reasonably. "It's Tuesday. Bring me the Braavos manifest when you've a moment — the one with the bent corner. I think the road south is opening." And she smiled, the small private smile she gave no one else in the world, the one that meant *junior sister, inner disciple, mine.* And Wylla — to her own lasting horror, kneeling that night by her cot trying to pray and finding the words would not come — discovered that it still warmed her, that the smile of a child who had a man poisoned over a few stags still reached into her and turned something over, and that whatever was happening to her, whatever this girl was making of her, it was already, quietly, much too late to undo.

<p align="center">⁂</p>

Gael, for her part, did not give the man another thought, and brought her own attention happily back to the road south.

The whole structure ran, Gael had arranged without quite intending to, on three people being wrong about who was in charge. The men who came to wager and lost thought they dealt with a clever commoner — one of the chambermaids, Pella or Nona, the sharp-eyed girls who walked the floor and took the coin and seemed, to a drunk losing his purse, plainly to be the brains of so slick an operation. Wylla, the noble lady-in-waiting who never showed her face below-stairs, thought herself the keeper of the cleverest mind in Westeros, and quietly held the apparatus together in service of it. And Gael thought she was running a sect's tribute-branch, the mortal limb of a great immortal order, attended by an inner disciple and a swarm of outer-disciple servants beneath her notice. Every one of them was wrong about who ran it, and the not-knowing was the strongest wall in the whole edifice, and it printed money. A noble lady cannot work a gaming-floor and a princess cannot be seen near one, and so the smallfolk were the hands and the lady was the manager and the child was the invisible source, and no man who lost his house at the dice would ever, in a thousand years, raise his eyes high enough to find her.

Across the city, in a gallery above the gaming-floor where he had no business being, a man Otto Hightower paid to watch things watched the chambermaids take the coin, and could not for his life make the count come out, and went away to report that the strangeness around the little princess had a shape now, even if he could not yet say what it was — not the coin itself, he told his master, but the *girl,* there was something wrong about the girl, and his master, who had been waiting years for someone to say exactly that, set down his cup and asked him to go on.

## II.3 · The Betrothal

The Stark envoy was a broad grey man named Cregan-of-some-holdfast — Gael did not retain the holdfast — and he stood in the King's solar with snow still melting off his shoulders and looked at her the way a smith looks at iron, and she liked him better than anyone she had met at court, because he at least understood that this was a transaction.

"The Warden's heir," Jaehaerys was saying, "is a man of two-and-twenty, sound in body, blooded against wildlings — "

"We've heard the tales of her," the northman said, over the King, which no one did, and which made the chamberlain by the door go still. He was looking at Gael, not at Jaehaerys. "The dead dragon. The bonded prince unmade. The lady throttled — that's newer; word reached us on the road." He did not say it as an objection. He said it the way a buyer recites the points of a horse he has decided to take. "Soft houses run from it. We don't run from hard weather, in the North. We breed it back in." He inclined his head a precise degree toward the King, and a precise degree more toward Gael, and Gael, meditating, inclined hers exactly the same — *senior to junior, between allied sects* — which the northman took, correctly, for the only honest greeting he had gotten in the south, and which warmed him to her, fatally, for both their sakes.

Gael, forbidden again to speak, meditated.

She had assembled the situation from the harbor-rolls and the gossip and the maps before anyone troubled to tell her. The sect was selling her north. The North was the frozen enemy ice-sect — Winterfell its mountain stronghold, the Wall its great formation, and beyond the Wall a demon-horde the southern orders only whispered of — and they meant to hand her over as a sacrifice-bride to seal an alliance, a stock arrangement she had read a hundred times and found, in execution, beneath the dignity of the trope. The groom did not interest her. He was a destination, not a person: a cold place that would snuff her furnace, where the radiant matter she needed could not be sourced, where she would be watched by a whole rival order. *Hard pass.* She was not afraid of him or of the wedding, because she had read enough to know exactly how this went wrong — you did not *jilt* a great sect, did not make a senior order *lose face,* or you became the villain of their hundred-year revenge arc. You vanished clean. You let them think you'd died or fled or been taken by a spirit, and went to cultivate outside the plot entirely, where no one's face was at stake. The wedding, to her, had quietly become a delivery deadline.

Her mother was not so calm.

Alicent had come to fight the match and discovered there was nothing to fight. She could not say a Great House was beneath them; it was not. She could not say the North was unsafe; the North was, by the realm's lights, the safest place a difficult daughter could be sent. Every objection she reached for dissolved into the one thing she could not say in the King's solar: *she is mine and I am losing her and I do not even understand what I am losing anymore.* So her objection collapsed, in the end, into grief, and grief has no standing at a betrothal.

She withdrew to Dragonstone. There was nothing else to withdraw to. Defeated on the match and defeated, more terribly, on the daughter she could no longer read, she chose at least to be defeated behind black walls and the sound of the sea, alone — the refuge she had dreamed of bringing Gael to, fled to without her, because a princess belongs to the King.

They had their goodbye in the solar Alicent had used as a girl.

"I won't be far," Alicent lied, holding her daughter's face in both hands, and her daughter looked back at her with that patient violet calm, and Alicent searched it for the child she had armed with an egg and could not find her. "You'll write. You'll tell me you're — you'll tell me how you fare. Promise me. Whatever happens with — " She could not finish. *With the marriage. With the North. With whatever you are.* "Promise me, my love."

"Of course," said Gael, who was already three-quarters into the reagent-map behind her eyes, and found this sentimental and overlong, the way send-offs in the novels always were — a senior leaving the sect for seclusion, a tedious obligatory beat before the plot resumed. She let her mother hold her face. It cost nothing and seemed to be wanted. *Braavos for the resin,* she was thinking. *The Summer Isles for the bark. The road south, the warm road, glows the whole way down.*

"You were always — " Alicent's voice failed and tried again. "When you were small, before any of it, before the egg, you would grip my finger so hard. As though you knew me. I have wondered since whether — " She stopped. There was no end to the sentence that she could bear to reach, and her daughter was watching her with that calm, kind, bottomless attention that gave her nothing back, and Alicent understood, finally, fully, that she was saying goodbye to someone who had already left, years ago, in the cradle, and left no forwarding word. She pressed her lips to Gael's forehead. The skin was cool and faintly wrong under her mouth, as it had been for years, and she had taught herself not to notice, and noticed now. "Be safe. Whatever you are. Be safe, and let me believe you're safe, that's all I — that's all."

"Travel safely, Mother," said Gael, and meant it, in the way one means a wish for a colleague one will not see again; and the moment her mother released her face she was back inside the chart, tracing the warm road south.

Alicent left for Dragonstone the next morning and wept the length of the bay, and Gael, who had filed the entire goodbye under *elder departs for closed-door seclusion, low priority,* did not see her go.

Behind her, the table she had lit did not stay one table.

She had built a gaming-hall and stepped back, and the vacuum and the seed-capital and the house-edge no rival could match had taken it and *run* — dice into protection, protection into territory, territory into laundries and loan-sharks and a quiet retainer paid to the right men of the watch. Factors recruited factors three tiers below her sight. King's Landing, which had never had a true crime-lord, acquired one without noticing, and the crime-lord was a seven-year-old who registered the whole sprawling racket only as a vague administrative *largeness,* the worldly branch rendering more tribute than was interesting. It ran behind a faceless name the men themselves invented to fear — the Quiet Coin — and the coin poured off the books, three layers of paper deep, toward reagents in the south and a hull not yet ordered.

And because a lone lady-in-waiting will not be taken seriously by violent men who smell weakness, the master came down to back her. Gael slipped out, more than once, to stand at Wylla's shoulder in rooms where men had begun to test her — and made them stop. No ceremony, no qi, no raised voice: a poison hinted at, a debt called, the maegi-legend doing half the work before she opened her mouth. A challenger came in certain and went out grey, and never could say afterward what had been threatened, only that he would not be going back. It was, Wylla understood — watching her tiny mistress discuss the maiming of an enforcer in the same voice she used to order the laundry — a rehearsal. By the time real danger reached Wylla herself, removal would already be comfortable. Casual. She held the whole apparatus in her two hands and felt the size of it on her shoulders, and felt how much of it now rested on her alone, and was afraid in a way she could no longer afford to be.

And out in the gangs and the gold cloaks, from the bodies that did not appear and the rivals who simply stopped and the money that moved like smoke, men assembled a portrait of the power behind it all — a brilliant, invisible, untouchable crime-lord, patient and bloodless, who left no trace and made no demand and could not be found. They feared the Quiet Coin accurately and entirely, and it was a seven-year-old and her maid, and the very wrongness of the legend made it a perfect decoy, a monstrous shape they would chase forever in exactly the wrong direction, nowhere near a dais.

Out in the city, too, the first real human wreckage surfaced — a lord ruined at the tables past his means and turned out of his own hall, a debtor who borrowed against the next harvest and then did not come home, men sunk into a grip on the doped fighting-pits they could not name and could not climb out of. Gael caught only the faint edge of it: a souring in the city's air, the first thin whisper of a *curse,* which she read, in passing, as some far-off evil-sect aura beginning to gather — a thing to watch, later, when she had the time. She filed it and thought no more of it. She did not connect it to herself. It had not yet occurred to her, and would not for a while, that there was anything to connect.

The maester, two floors below, wrote a letter.

<p align="center">⁂</p>

*— to the Conclave, with my continued perplexity. The Princess Gael, lately betrothed to the North, has of her own will requested charts of the southern trade — Braavos, Lys, the Summer Isles, the Asshai'i routes — and studies them, her septa assures me, for hours. I am told it is geography, a young lady improving her mind ahead of a long journey. I find I am charmed. And yet I write at this hour, and cannot say why, save that a child betrothed to Winterfell ought, surely, to be studying the snows of the North, and is instead tracing with her small finger every warm port that lies in the opposite direction. It is nothing. I am tired. I remain your servant, &c.*

## II.4 · The Overheard Word

She had not meant to pass the music-room at all; the corridor to the still-room ran the other way, and she took the long route only because she had heard there was a new shipment of Myrish glass in the western stores and wanted to see whether any of it was the clear kind that took an etched array. So it was chance — pure, stupid, genre-flavored chance, the kind the novels used to spring exactly this sort of thing — that put her at the door while the ladies were talking.

There were four of them at their needlework, talking about her the way the court always talked about her: at a careful one remove, not cruel, not warm, the cadence of women discussing a thing rather than a person. The strange princess. The one no one quite warmed to. They had moved on to her hopelessness at the courtly graces — the dancing-master had despaired of her, it seemed, the way the harp-master had despaired of her, the way her embroidery would shame a girl half her age — and a lady Gael knew, a recurring face named Lady Jeyne Rosby who had pinched her cheek once and called her *poppet,* sighed over her stitching and said, half-laughing, not unkindly, *"No talent there, that one. None at all. Talentless."*

Gael caught one word of it.

She had come up the corridor hearing nothing, and arrived in the doorway in time for *talentless,* fixed to her name, in a woman's idle voice. And the word did not mean *dancing* to her, because Gael did not have the frame in which it could mean dancing. *No talent* was the one verdict her cosmology could not absorb. It meant *no spiritual root.* It meant a void where a foundation should be. It meant the negation of her entire path, the single unsurvivable sentence a cultivator could be handed, the one thing that was not fearsome and so could not be reframed as a title — only as erasure, the claim that she was *nothing,* that her four hundred lives and her egg and her revolutions and her radiant climbing self were a void someone could simply name in passing over needlework.

She crossed the room in the time it took Lady Rosby to draw her next breath.

There was no warning and no heat in it. Her body was tempered and it moved fast and flat, and her small hands were at the lady's throat before anyone in the room had registered that the princess was in the room, and she bore down with a strength no eight-year-old has, a cold mechanical correction of an intolerable error, and she did not stop in time. She did not, in truth, stop at all; she was *stopped* — by the woman going limp, by the wet ruined sound, by the matter being, to her, simply over. She let go. Lady Rosby slid to the floor with her hands at her own neck and a noise coming out of her that would never again be a voice, the cartilage crushed, the throat ruined for the rest of her life, and the other three ladies were screaming now, and Gael straightened her sleeves.

The matter was closed. The error was corrected. She turned and walked out, already back inside the reagent-ledger, mildly puzzled at the noise behind her — the novels were always so loud about these things — and gave it no further thought, because nothing unusual had happened. Someone had spoken an impossibility over her root, and she had answered it, proportionately, and moved on.

The reader holds four things at once, and none of them touch each other: that the ladies had meant a dancing-lesson; that Gael had heard a death-sentence on her soul; that she had half-killed a familiar, harmless woman over a trifle; and that she had no idea, walking placidly back to her glass, that anything had occurred.

Lady Rosby kept her ruined throat. She was at court still, after, a marked face the whole Keep learned to fall silent around — the woman the maegi-princess had nearly killed for no reason any of them could find. They invented reasons. A curse spoken. An ill omen given. The truth — that there had been no reason they could see, because the trigger was buried in a cosmology none of them shared — was unbearable, so the *maegi* rumor hardened another notch instead, which suited everyone except Lady Rosby, who could no longer say so.

And the King's grandfather, who had been waiting for exactly such an opening, took it.

Otto Hightower could not name what was wrong with the princess, but he knew an unwomanly, dangerous child who throttled ladies at court was a problem with a price, and with the Queen gone to Dragonstone there was no one left to shield her. He went to the King with the language of correction. *The girl wants discipline. She wants the dangerous notions taken out of her.* And he opened, as such men do, with the easiest lever to hand. Her allowance was cut. The gift her mother had given her, now undefended, was simply trimmed away, a princess's pocket-coin docked to teach her humility.

It was the funniest thing that had happened to Gael in a year, though she did not laugh, because she rarely did. They had docked the pocket-money of a girl who privately bankrolled the city's underworld and was three layers of paper deep into buying a *ship.* She noted the cut with faint, genuine amusement: that they imagined a princess's power was the coin a man chose to grant her, that they thought they could starve a river by capping a cup. *They can only conceive my strength as my allowance.* She let them take it. It cost her nothing she needed, and a humility-trial endured serenely only burnished her, as the next one would.

The clampdown had, in fact, only begun. But that was for later.

Three nights after the music-room, a man came over her windowsill with a knife.

She did not wake, because she did not need to. Daemon's coin had bought a competent killer — a real one, a man who had done this work before and would have done it cleanly on any other child in the Seven Kingdoms. He came down the wall on a knotted line in the dead black before dawn, eased the shutter, crossed the floor without a sound, and stood over the sleeping princess and found, as such men are trained to find, the soft place beneath the ribs where one push of good steel ends a small life. He drove the blade down with a professional's economy, his whole weight behind it.

The point met her skin and stopped.

He felt it the way you feel a chisel meet a knot — the give that should have come and did not — and he leaned harder, baffled, and the steel skated sideways off something that was not bone and not armor and not anything a sleeping eight-year-old had any business being, and took a bright notch out of its own edge, and the girl breathed slow and even and did not so much as turn over. The man crouched in the dark over a ruined knife and a child who had not woken, and his nerve, which had carried him over the wall, simply left him. He went back up the line and over the wall and was three streets away before he let himself shake. He never told Daemon the truth of it, only that the thing was done or could not be, he was never sure which he said. In the morning Gael found a long scratch on her window-frame and a thread of grey wool snagged on the latch, frowned at it for the space of one breath, and filed it — if she filed it at all — under a clumsy servant and a careless ladder, and went down to her revolutions. She never knew there had been an attempt. The discredited prince's voice had made him a joke; his knife made him a villain; and a girl who never once looked his way absorbed the villainy as serenely as the joke, because both of them, in the end, simply slid off.

Word of the throttling traveled north, and landed *well.* A girl who answered an idle word with cold and instant lethality was hard stock, dangerous stock, exactly the iron the North respected. The flash that would have repelled any southern suitor confirmed, for Winterfell, the asset. And the King, hearing of both the maiming and the failed murder in the same week, resolved with fresh urgency to marry the trouble far away, and fast — the sale he had closed becoming, by the day, a disposal he wanted *closed.*

Wylla had seen it from the doorway. She had been bringing a copied manifest up the corridor and had arrived in time for the worst of it — the crossing of the room that was too fast to be a child's, the small hands, the sound, the slow boneless slide of Lady Rosby to the floor with that ruined noise coming out of her. And then the princess had straightened her sleeves and walked out, and her eyes had passed over Wylla in the doorway with no more weight than they passed over the wall, already gone back into the ledger, mildly puzzled at the screaming. *Was that one of ours?* the look seemed to ask, of the woman dying on the floor. *No. Then.*

Wylla went and was sick in the privy, quietly, and knelt there a while after with her forehead against the cold stone, doing the arithmetic she could not stop doing now. The lady had said a word about *dancing.* That was all. There had been no warning and no quarrel and no debt and no theft; there had only been a word, and the word had been the wrong word, and a familiar woman who had once called the princess *poppet* would carry a crushed throat to her grave for it. Wylla understood, kneeling there, that she now lived inside a thing she could not predict — that all her careful management of the operation's *real* dangers was beside the point, because the true danger had no rule she could learn, only a single buried tripwire no one could see, and her survival, and the survival of everyone below her, depended on never once stepping where she could not tell the wire was laid. She got up. She rinsed her mouth. She went back to work, because there was no one she could tell, and the manifests still needed sorting, and the dice still needed counting, and the strange terrible child she had bound her life to would want the Braavos figures before the bells.

## II.5 · The Compromise

The face in the gallery had begun to repeat.

Gael noticed it the way she noticed everything she was not meant to — a man she did not know appearing twice in places a man like that had no reason to be, and then, more interestingly, Marra. The timid pious chambermaid had gone strange and skittish, would not meet her eye, flinched at the solar door; and the small things in Gael's rooms had begun, very slightly, to move. A journal a finger's width from where she'd left it. The harbor-rolls re-stacked by a hand that was not Wylla's. She was being watched, closely, by someone patient, and the watching had reached inside.

She had been waiting for this for two years, and her whole genre-poisoned heart rose up to meet it.

"It's a *hidden test,*" she told Wylla, with shining certainty, pacing the solar. "A reclusive master — an expert, a true immortal, someone who has *sensed* my talent — has set an unseen trial. This is the part where they watch in secret to see if I'm worthy. The diligence, the discretion, the dao-heart under pressure. And if I pass — Wylla, if I pass — there's a legacy. A secret discipleship. A fated encounter. I've read this a *hundred* times." Her eyes were luminous. "I have to *perform.*"

Wylla put her face in her hands.

She had watched the operation's real danger gather all book — the exposed chambermaids, the notoriety, the wreckage in the city, the watchers Gael was now mistaking for *destiny* — and had no way to say any of it that the princess would hear, because the princess had a frame for everything and the frame was always wrong and always serene. *It is not a hidden master,* Wylla wanted to say. *It is a real man with real malice and he has gotten to one of the girls, and you are about to perform for him.* She said none of it. She had learned that the frame did not bend.

And Gael performed.

She did not hide from the surveillance; she *auditioned* for it. She rose earlier, sat longer, walked the corridors with her eyes lowered in a study of modest diligence she had read described in a dozen novels and reproduced now with a craftsman's care. She dressed a decoy diary as her audition piece — pious reflections, dutiful copywork, *Today I considered the virtue of patience; a disciple's first foundation is her stillness* — and left it where a watcher's hand would find it, and felt rather pleased with the touch. "He'll read humility there," she told Wylla, tapping the decoy. "Masters always test for humility first. It's the gate before the real teaching."

"And if it is not a master," Wylla said, very carefully, because she had decided to try, once, plainly, while there was still time. "If it is only a man. A real man, with a knife or a writ, who has gotten to one of the girls and is reading everything you set out."

Gael considered this with the indulgent patience she reserved for Wylla's frights. "Then he's a fool, and he'll learn nothing, and the master who *is* watching will see how I handle a fool. Either way I pass." She smiled. "Stop worrying. This is the good part. This is the part the whole arc is *for.*"

There was nothing to say to that, and so Wylla said nothing, and watched her tiny mistress, as the fatal flourish, leave her *real* journal proudly on display. The bestiary. Because she had never once, in eight years, conceived of it as a vulnerability. It was *proof.* It was the evidence of her merit, the record of her ascent, exactly the thing a hidden master would want to read before extending a legacy, and she wanted it read. She would never hide the one document that told the truth about her, because in her mind it told only the truth that she was magnificent.

The hand that moved Gael's papers belonged to Marra, and Marra did not know she was a thief. Otto had not threatened her — he was far too clever for that. He had come to her as a kindly patron, a great lord taking a fatherly interest, and had sat with the timid pious girl and spoken gently of his worry for the young princess: so strange, so friendless, surrounded by people who might wish her ill, in need of someone watchful to keep her from harm. And he had persuaded Marra, who loved the princess in her frightened dutiful way, that to tell him what the household did was not betrayal but *protection* — for the princess's own safety, for the good of the marriage to come. So Marra told him things, and went home each night believing she had done a small kindness, and slept easier for it, and never once understood that she was the leak, or that the leak would in time doom her — a smallfolk hand set to hold a risk that would be spent first and spent whole. The kindly lord had a gift for finding the one frightened soul in any room and making her the instrument of her own ruin, and calling it care, and being thanked for it.

It was through Marra's dutiful, loving, doomed hands that Otto's man got the bestiary, and took it down to Otto.

And Otto did not recoil. Where Alicent would have broken — would have found, partway down, *Mother: sentimental, useful for egg-procurement, low threat* — and where Barth, had he gotten it, would have brushed a cosmology and missed the girl by one unreadable step, Otto read the bestiary the way he read everything: for the weapon in it. He did not care that humans were logged by function. He cared that one of them, a single entry, had climbed. *Asset (hands)* crossed out, climbing, line over line, to a *name.*

Wylla.

He understood it instantly and coldly: that this name was the one lever that moved an unmovable girl. And more — that this was the noble who held the secret things, the lady-in-waiting who tied the whole invisible apparatus together, the trade and the off-books vice and the Quiet Coin, the single human seam in a nameless empire. The one crack. He had been digging for dirt on a wildcard princess to bank with the heir-faction — with Baelon and young Viserys, who already blamed the maegi for the maddened dragon — and instead he had found the load-bearing point of something far larger than he had imagined, and the maid who was its keystone, and he began, patiently, to plan.

Gael, meanwhile, was *enjoying* her test.

Her own exposure she shrugged off entirely. When she gathered, from the pattern of the intrusions, that the watcher had read her journal, she was briefly, genuinely *offended* — they'd filed her a mere *poisoner,* apparently, when she was so obviously a *cultivator* — and then she set the offense aside, because the examiner's misjudgment was part of the trial. She kept performing. She felt the test was going well. She never once clocked Marra as the channel, because she was not hunting a turned maid; she was hunting a phantom hidden-master, and a phantom does not have a face you can catch.

But the instant the lever resolved into Wylla, the breeze stopped.

It came to her sideways, the way real things did. She was working through the pattern of the intrusions — which papers had been touched, which left, in what order — and somewhere in the middle of it she understood not a fact about herself but a fact about Wylla: that anyone who had read the bestiary now knew there was exactly one entry in it that was a name, and that a careful enemy would draw from one name the one conclusion that happened to be true. That Wylla was the lever. That there was, in the whole nameless invisible empire, a single human seam, and that someone had just found it, and that the someone could now reach the one thing in her flat-filed life that she could not file flat and walk away from.

She set the papers down. She did not know yet that the watcher was Otto, did not know it was Marra, did not know the shape of the threat at all — only its target. And something in her, which had been breezy through betrothal and clampdown and a knife she had never woken for, went quiet. Cold. Precise. She was not afraid — she was never afraid; fear was a mortal's reflex and she had ascended past it — but the wide attention she carried over everything narrowed, all at once, to one hard point, and stayed there. She told herself, carefully, in the language she trusted, that this was a *compromised channel that must be closed.* A logistics problem. A vulnerability in the apparatus, to be excised before it could be turned. She believed it as she thought it. The reader saw what she could not: that this was the single thing in the world she was not carefree about, that the cold was not the cold of accounting but the cold of a creature whose one soft place has just been touched, and that she had, calmly, between two thoughts, in the language of logistics, *decided.*

The decision was made there. The deed would wait. But it was made.

Out in the city the curse she could not see was thickening — the ruined lord, the vanished debtor, the addicts, a faceless power *draining* the realm — and Gael heard every word of it and read, with grave concern, a rival demonic sect at its soul-work, some dark formation laid over King's Landing that she ought to guard the people against. She ran the self-audit. *Am I the villain doing this? No — surely some other evil sect.* She hunted herself across the city with real diligence and never caught the scent, because the scent was her own. The notoriety that frightened her was the same notoriety that had drawn Otto's eye; her blind spot and his opening were one fact, seen from two sides.

And then the cage closed the rest of the way.

The allowance had gone at II.4; now Otto finished the work. With the Queen at Dragonstone and no shield left, he convinced the King to *correct* the dangerous child in full: her tutors dismissed, her library stripped — no more unwomanly books, no more correspondence — and septa training imposed in their place, proper piety, proper accomplishments. The realm meant it as discipline. It landed two ways, neither intended. The book-and-tutor strip was the cut that actually stung, because the library *was* her intelligence service — the harbor records, the trade logs, the raw feed of the reagent-map — and she had to reroute the whole flow of information through other hands. But the septa indoctrination she reframed whole: the Faith was a stuffy rival orthodox sect pressing its righteous dogma, and the dreary catechism was *obviously* part of the hidden master's test, a humility-trial to prove her dao-heart's steadiness. So she sat in Septa Aldith's bare cold cell with her hands folded and her spine straight and let the woman read her the Seven-Pointed Star by the hour, and asked, in her grave clear voice, exactly the questions a worthy prospect would ask under examination. "And the Crone carries the lantern," she said once, "to light the path through the dark. That is the wisdom-aspect, then — the elder who tests the disciple's heart before granting the lamp." Septa Aldith, who had braced herself for a demon and been handed a catechumen of frightening focus, felt her heart lift with a vanity she would have called holy joy. The princess collected the scripture the way she collected everything — an inferior-path text to be studied and dismissed, the dogma of a rival order filed for tactical reference — and hid her real gut-revulsion so completely behind the reframe that Aldith never once glimpsed it. The septa watched the maegi-child sit so meekly under instruction, hour upon hour, and took it for a miracle of her own working — *I have turned the demon to the Light* — claimed the credit up and down the Keep, and let the *saintly princess* rumor swell up beside the *demon child* one until the court could no longer tell which of the two it feared, only that it ought to keep feeling both at once.

The correction meant to humble her had burnished her legend instead, on both faces at once. And underneath all of it, where no audit reached, sat the one cold decided thing about Wylla, waiting for the deed.

## II.6 · The Yield

The squeeze that was meant to break her closed on nothing, because there had never been anything there to break.

This was the thing Otto could not have guessed and would never learn. He had built the clampdown as a siege — cut the supply, starve the strange power of whatever fed it, and watch the dangerous child weaken into something a man could manage. It was a sound plan against any creature that ran on what it took in. It simply did not describe Gael. Throttling her allowance and stripping her stores and choking her court-supply changed nothing she needed to *live,* because she did not run on what came in. The egg had been a reactor, not a battery. She had generated her own qi since the cradle, a slow inexhaustible warmth she drew down nine turns each morning, the well refilling itself faster than any morning could empty it, and no man capping her purse could touch the furnace under her ribs. The siege closed on a fortress with its own spring inside the walls.

The empire had never been about survival. This was the part she clocked only as a faint, footnoted inefficiency, and the reader was left to hold the weight of: that she could have sat in a stripped bare room with every book and coin and reagent in the realm taken from her, and gone on existing, indefinitely, faintly bored, never an hour from death no matter what they did. The treasury raid, the Faith's coffers, the doped beasts and the ruined lords and the man with the wrong tea and the five who were coming — none of it had ever stood between her and dying. None of it was need. It was all *advancement,* every theft and every body bought to climb a wall she could have chosen never to climb, and to buy a door out of a cage that could not, in the end, have killed her if she'd stayed.

The empire was about *advancement.* And here, at last, it paid.

The trade and the off-books vice-coin finally reached past the Keep, past the city, past the realm — and brought back the far-sourced radiant matter the court could never source. The packages came up the channel disguised as anything but what they were: a Lysene resin in a dye-merchant's crate, a Summer-Isle bark folded into a bolt of cloth, and the prize, last and smallest, a black stone the size of a thumb-joint that had come off an Asshai'i hull through six hands and that hummed against her palm, when she finally held it alone at midnight, the way the egg had hummed in the crib.

She prepared the yield over three nights in the still-room with the door barred — grinding, decocting, layering the three sources the way the manuals laid out the forging of a foundation-pill, the real chemistry of her last life braided with the genre-craft of the four hundred before it. On the third night she swallowed it and sat down cross-legged on the cold stone floor and began the revolutions, and somewhere past the hundredth a wall she had been pressing against for a year, patiently, with nothing to widen it, simply *gave.* She felt her foundation spread outward into new room. She felt the whole apparatus of crime and theft and risk *vindicated* in a single bright turn — every robbery, every leaned-on man, every body in a ledger had bought *real power,* measurable, hers, climbing — and the same coin would buy the ship, the road paid for at last, advancement and escape both purchased with one long outflow of stolen gold. She sat in the dark with the new room ringing in her and was, for a moment, perfectly happy.

And yet — she clocked it only as a faint inefficiency, a footnote she did not read — none of it had ever been *needed.* The whole vice-network, the crimes, the bodies had bought her advancement and her exit and nothing she required to keep breathing. The dark note did not fall on the racket's dead men; they were furniture, and she would no more mourn a spent minion than a spent candle. It fell where she could not see it fall: on the one mortal she had drawn into all of it, the maid pulled into a criminal empire and pointed, though neither of them yet knew it, toward blood — for a survival-panic that had never had any ground at all.

Then, with the first true surplus, she did the thing that gave her away, and did not notice she had done it.

She spent it on Wylla.

<p align="center">⁂</p>

Wylla had not been told what was in the cup, only that she should drink it, that it would be unpleasant, and that the princess would stay with her through the night. She had learned, by now, the precise weight of *the princess will stay with her* — it was the weight of a thing being done that mattered, because Gael did not sit up with people for trifles — and so she drank, and the warmth went down into her like a swallowed coal, and spread.

For a while it was only strange. Heat under the skin, a glow she could see faintly through her own wrist when she held it to the candle, a feeling of her body being *read* and slowly, carefully *rewritten,* line by line, the way Gael read everything. And then, past midnight, it went wrong. The warmth became a fever; the fever became a furnace; she was burning from the inside and shaking and her bones felt as though they were being unmade, and she heard her own voice making sounds she did not choose, and she thought, with strange clarity, through the white roar of it, *I am going to die, and she will write a clean line in the book and find someone new.*

She did not die. Somewhere in the worst of it there were small cool hands on her face and a voice — flat, certain, not frightened, *never* frightened — saying numbers, counting revolutions, talking her body down off the edge it had climbed to, and the furnace banked, and the fever broke, and toward dawn Wylla surfaced into a body that was not the one she had lain down in. Stronger. Quieter. A heat banked low under everything. She lay in the grey light and looked at the ceiling and understood, the way she understood most true things now, sideways and without permission, that her princess had poured something into her that should not exist, had nearly killed her with it, and had stayed the whole night with cool hands to make sure she lived.

Some doors you do not open. Wylla did not ask what the cup had been. *A tonic for the voyage,* Gael had said, lightly, when the worst was past, and Wylla let it be a tonic for the voyage. She noted, in the days after, that she did not tire the way she had, that a winter fever swept the Keep and felled half the household and never touched her, that she slept less and saw more. She noted, too, the things she had been not-noticing for years — that the princess herself never ate much, never tired, sailed through every sickness untouched, was simply *other* in some way she had decided long ago not to look at directly. She decided, again, not to look. She had been nearly unmade and held together by the same two hands in a single night, and whatever that made the princess, it had also, unmistakably, been *for her,* and Wylla did not have it in her anymore to be only afraid.

<p align="center">⁂</p>

Gael, for her part, filed it under *fortifying the asset.*

She had a useful junior-sister, an inner disciple who held the apparatus together, and it was simple prudence to harden a valuable mortal against the wear of the work — an investment in a reliable instrument, a line between the resin and the bark. She ran the self-audit and cleared herself instantly: a master strengthens a worthy disciple; there is nothing here to examine. She did not register, because she had no frame in which she *could* register it, that the tempering had cost her real, hard-won, far-sourced power; that she had taken the first surplus of her ascent — the thing the whole bloody empire existed to produce — and spent it, not on her own advancement, not on the ship, but on a person. That she had sat up all night with cool hands counting another body's revolutions, frightened — she would have denied the word — that it might stop.

She saw a line in the ledger. The reader saw the rehearsal for the gift that would, one day, "save" Wylla, and the cost of it, and the one warm thing in the whole cold machine, filed as procurement.

Two floors down, the maester read the household accounts and the sick-lists by candle, and reached a conclusion he could neither prove nor say aloud: that the court had cut the strange princess off from every source that fed an ordinary body, and the princess had only grown stronger; that she did not run, could not be running, on what fed everyone else. He sat with it a long time. Then he drew a fresh sheet to the candle, and wrote, because writing was the one thing he could still do with a truth no one would believe.

<p align="center">⁂</p>

*— to Septon Barth, privately, and not for the Conclave. My lord, I will set down a thing and trust your discretion with it, for I can trust it to no one nearer. The Princess Gael has these six months past been cut off, by the King's own order, from her tutors, her library, her correspondence, and the better part of her allowance. A girl so stripped should pine. The pox that took half the household this winter — I buried four of the under-servants — never touched her; she has not had so much as a chill. She eats, by the kitchens' account, almost nothing. She does not tire. I have weighed her on the apothecary's scale against her own measure of a year past and she has not lost an ounce. My lord, I am a maester and I deal in causes, and I can find no cause. A body that is fed less and works the same does not grow stronger. Hers does. I do not write you a theory. I write you a fact I cannot place, and the cold feeling it gives me at this hour, and the request that you tell no one I wrote it. Your servant, &c.*

<p align="center">⁂</p>

He sealed it, and held the candle a moment longer over the small flame of his own dread, and then put it out, and went, at last, to bed.

## II.7 · The Vise

The wedding-clock, which had been a distant thing on the far side of two years, jumped — and Wylla was the one who brought Gael the news, because Wylla was the one who heard everything first now, and her hands were not quite steady on the letter.

"They've moved the date," she said. "The Stark party rides south in the spring. There's to be a — they're calling it a presentation, before. Midsummer." She set the letter down on the chart. "Princess. That's not two years. That's not even one."

Otto had turned the lever he had found. This was the elegance of it, from his side: having located the maid, he no longer needed to understand the empire at all. He had never made the count come out and he had stopped trying. He did not need to know what the strange princess was building, or how, or with what; he needed only to know that everything she did ran through a single noble girl, and that a single noble girl could be pressed — and the fastest, hardest press in his reach was the wedding itself, which leaned on the maid and the operation and the cage all at once, with the full weight of the crown behind it. He had gone to the King with the maiming and the failed murder and the maegi-rumors all bundled into one urgent argument — *close the disposal, marry the trouble north before it ripens into something the crown cannot answer for* — and the King, who wanted exactly this, accelerated the match. The distant deadline became a near one. The pressure came down the channel all at once. There were new faces watching the gaming-floor that the chambermaids did not know. A factor three tiers down was questioned by men who showed no badge and would not say who sent them. A bill that should have cleared in Braavos sat, unaccountably, *held.* It was the cage drawing tight from every side at once, and a betrothal Gael had filed as a delivery date for a delivery she did not mean to make was suddenly bearing down on her with months instead of years to run.

She clocked the cage. She did it minimally, breezily, without one gram of earned epiphany — sitting at the window with the held bill in one hand and the moved date in the other, while Wylla stood waiting for the storm and got, instead, a small thoughtful nod.

That the empire was hers in no way that counted — that any of it could be cut off, leaned on, leaked. That her body and her fate had been sold north and were not, in any sense the world recognized, her own. That even her secrets had leaked, that the bestiary had been read, that nothing inside these walls belonged to her. She registered all of it as *typical evil-sect business.* A master owns everything and lets a disciple keep nothing; that was simply how a wicked order ran, flat and unremarkable, no felt loss in it anywhere. She had read this exact arc a hundred times — the disciple who realizes, too late, that her cultivation resources and her freedom and her very person were never hers to begin with, that the sect lent them to her the way a moneylender lends, against a debt called in at the worst moment. The difference, she thought, with the mild satisfaction of a reader who has guessed the twist early, was that those disciples always *despaired* here, and she had read ahead, and knew the move that came next. And beneath the court she clocked the matching genre-fact: that the Targaryens were not, in truth, strong — strip away the dragons and you had a hall of soft mortals with a few tamed spirit-beasts and nothing here, *nothing,* worth a cultivator's staying for.

To *own* a thing — she reasoned it straight through, the way she reasoned the house-edge — you had to be *beyond reach.* This was the move the despairing disciples in the novels never made, because they were written by authors who needed them to suffer; they raged against the sect, or schemed within it, or knelt, and were crushed, when the obvious play had been in front of them the whole time. You did not win the master's game. You did not lose it either. You *left the board.* And in the leaving, the two desires that had pulled against each other since the cradle — the hunger for endless radiant supply, and the hunger for a solitude no hand could reach into — which she had carried as a contradiction, two roads she could not walk at once, suddenly were not a contradiction at all. A single object in the world was both roads. The answer arrived whole and obvious and aligned at last, and she nearly laughed, which she almost never did:

A ship.

A ship gave her infinite supply, the warm road south unrolling port by port forever, *and* absolute, unclaimable solitude, a hull no King and no sect and no grandfather could put a hand on. She stood up in the solar with the whole thing assembled behind her eyes, and the resolution — being Gael — arrived as comedy.

"I will forge a vessel," she announced to the empty afternoon and to Wylla, who was the only one there, "to bear me beyond the reach of this mortal realm."

Wylla put her face in her hands.

"A *ship,* Wylla. Don't you see?" She had stopped pacing and was holding the held Braavos bill up between two fingers like a specimen. "They can sell my body, they can cut my coin, they can read my journal, they can wall me into a hall full of weak mortals and marry me to an ice-sect — but they cannot own a thing that *isn't there.* You can't cut the allowance of a girl who isn't in the realm. You can't hold the bill of a buyer at sea. You can't marry a bride who has no shore. I will be *beyond reach.* Infinite supply, perfect solitude, the road south the whole way down. It is the *answer.* It has always been the answer; I simply hadn't drawn the formation correctly." She was luminous with it, eight years old and declaiming like a patriarch announcing his closed-door breakthrough. "We commission the finest vessel coin can buy. Off the books. Through you, three layers deep, course set south. And we sail out of the entire plot before the wedding bells finish ringing."

Wylla took her face out of her hands. "Princess. A ship is the single most-watched purchase a person can make. They have rolls of every keel laid in every yard from here to Braavos. The harbormaster's men climb aboard before the paint dries. You cannot — a *child* cannot quietly buy a *ship.*"

"Which is why," said Gael, serenely, "I have you." And she smiled, the small private one. "Three layers of paper, a phantom buyer, the Quiet Coin's own men, and a course filed for somewhere I am not going. The same way we hid a treasury and a syndicate and a curse over the whole city. We've done harder things than buy a boat, Wylla. We've done harder things this *month.*"

"They are moving the wedding *up,*" Wylla said, faintly.

"Then we build faster."

It was, Wylla thought, the most insane sentence she had ever heard, and she also knew, with the sinking certainty that had become the texture of her life, that they would in fact build faster, and that it would in fact work, because everything this child decided to do worked, blithely, against all the laws of the possible world. She went, the same afternoon, to find a shipwright through enough intermediaries that no man in the chain could ever say whose coin he had taken or where the hull was bound. Because the maid-squeeze obstructed at every turn now — Otto's pressure came down on the very channel she had to commission the ship *through,* the new faces, the held bills, the questioned factor — and the two countdowns, the hull and the wedding, had begun to run hard against each other, and somewhere in the closing gap between them, Wylla understood, walking the cold corridor with the commission already taking shape in her head, a thing was going to have to give. She did not yet let herself think about what.

It gave, in the end, the way Wylla had feared it would, and sooner. The pressure that came down through the channel did not stay on bills and watchers. Within a fortnight of the moved date the suitor's own people began to arrive — an advance party, ahead of the spring, to smooth the path for a wedding that could not be allowed to slip — and they were not careful men, and the smoothing of a path, to such men, meant finding whatever stood in it and leaning until it moved. Wylla felt them circling the operation, and then circling closer, and then — but that was the next chapter, and she did not yet know it would be her.

The household noted, in the days after Gael's strange declaration, that the princess had become eerily *calm* about her rushed wedding. She did not weep. She did not rage. She did not, as a difficult girl ought, have to be coaxed and corrected toward her duty; she had become, overnight, the most tractable bride in the Keep, sitting through the talk of dates and dowries and northern customs with a faraway attention that the matchmakers took for acceptance and the septa took for the final flowering of her piety and Alicent, in a letter from Dragonstone, took for a broken heart bravely borne. It was the eeriest misread of all of them. They were watching a girl be calm about a future, and not one of them guessed that the calm was the calm of someone who had read the future, found it not to her taste, and quietly decided not to attend it.

## II.8 · The Disappearance

They were five, and they were men, and they put their hands on Wylla in the lower passage where the gaming-coin came up through the household, and that was the mistake from which nothing else could be recalled.

They were the suitor's advance party — Otto-faction men, sent ahead of the rushed wedding to lean on the obstruction around it, and the obstruction, in their reading, was a lady-in-waiting who could be leaned on. So they leaned. A hand on her arm, a hand at her jaw, a voice telling her what would happen to her and to the people she answered to if the difficulties did not resolve themselves before the bells. Wylla went very still and very white and said nothing, and afterward could not have said how she got back up the stair, only that she did, and that she stood in the solar doorway with the marks already darkening on her arm and told the princess, in a flat careful voice, what had happened.

A prince's oblivious endangerment of a servant — that, Gael had no answer for; you cannot poison a man for failing to notice a maid. But this was different. These men had *meant* it, had reached deliberately for the one thing in the whole serene apparatus of her life that she could not file flat. These were addressable. So she addressed them.

Two of them had raised their flags without knowing it. The suitor himself, a heavy confident man, had said in the great hall, where a chambermaid heard it and it traveled up the channel to Gael, that *after the wedding's done I'll send for my own people and settle proper, a man wants his household about him.* And a sycophant in his train, a thin anxious factor doing the leaning he was told to do, had begged off a third night of it with the truest sentence a man can say: *I've only a family to feed, you understand, that's the whole of it, that's all I am.* Gael, who tracked such omens with religious care, noted both, filed both, and was not surprised when neither man was saved — the realm was a meat-grinder and proved her right about death-flags as reliably as it proved her right about everything else she was wrong about. She did not connect the omens to her own intention. She observed that flag-raisers died, and then made it so, and saw no seam between the watching and the doing.

It is not shown. The reader is given the before and the after and nothing in between: that the men were in the Keep, leaning, certain of their leverage; and then, across a handful of days, that they were not in the Keep, nor anywhere, that the suitor and his whole party of five-and-more had simply gone out of the world and left no body and no thread and no thief behind them. There was no drain, no qi, no demonic path — only paper, and the maester's own supply chain, and the still-room: a poison that read as a natural death, a bill of exchange that sent a man somewhere he never arrived, a quiet untraceable subtraction of human beings from the rolls of the living. They were here, and then they were not, and the hole they left was bigger than the men, and into it Otto would pour the rest of his year and find nothing.

The hole was bigger than the men in the other way too, the way no one in the Keep would ever add up. The thin factor's family did not know they had become a family with no one to feed them; the news of his vanishing reached them as a wage that stopped and a winter that came on regardless. The suitor's own household, a hundred leagues off, waited through the spring for a man who had gone south to seal a marriage and would now simply, inexplicably, never write again. The ruin spread out from the five of them in rings, into people Gael had never seen and would never count, dependents of the furniture she had quietly removed — and none of it appeared on any ledger she kept, because to her the matter had one line and the line was closed.

It was colder than the Daemon drain, because the Daemon drain had been a reflex and this was a plan. This she had decided at II.5 and built toward and executed on purpose, cleanly, the first death in eight years that she meant — not the casual swat of the skimmer she had never bothered to count, but a chosen, planned removal of human beings, undertaken with care, *for Wylla.* It was R2, and she did not feel it move under her at all.

She drew the line where she always drew it: at the technique, never the harm. Poison and paper, not the demonic path, so to her hands were *clean.* A channel closed. An asset protected. The books balanced. She told herself it was for the apparatus, for the operation, for the cold logistics of an empire that had grown a vulnerability and needed it excised. The reader, holding the ledger she would not, saw the truth she had filed away under a heading she would never read again: her first cold murders, planned and carried through, committed entirely to protect the one person in the world she would never, ever name as hers.

<p align="center">⁂</p>

Wylla knew.

No one told her. She did not need telling. She had brought the princess five men's names and the bruise on her own arm, and then the men were gone, and she understood the arithmetic of it the way she understood everything now, sideways and complete. She had not lifted a hand. She had not moved a body — she would not have known how, and was not asked, and some animal part of her was grateful for the not-asking. She had crossed only into silence. Into knowing that five men were dead, and why, and by whose small careful hands, and saying nothing to anyone, ever.

She sat alone in the dark of the room she shared with no one and worked out what she was doing, because she found she could not do it without watching herself do it. She was choosing. That was the thing. It was not that she had been kept from telling, not that fear had stopped her mouth — she could have gone to a septa, to the maester, to the gold cloaks, could have ruined the whole impossible machine in an hour and walked free of it. The men had threatened her; she would have been believed. She did not go. She sat in the dark and felt the shape of the silence she was choosing, and she knew it was murder she was keeping, five men into the ground for laying hands on her, and she folded it carefully into herself and resolved to carry it, and the thing that made her able to was not fear.

It was that the princess had done it for *her.*

That was the horror Wylla could not get past and did not, in the end, want to get past. Not that her mistress was a killer — she had known that since the skimmer and the tea. But that her mistress, who did not love, who filed her own mother under *low threat,* who spent power on nothing but her own ascent, had spent murder on *Wylla,* coldly and immediately and without being asked, the moment Wylla was touched. No one had ever protected her in her life. Her father had sent her to court to be useful. And this strange terrible child had reached out and taken five men out of the world for putting a hand on her arm, and called it logistics, and would never once say it was love, and Wylla — God forgive her, she thought; God forgive her, there is no one to tell and I would not tell them if there were — chose the silence, and chose the child, and chose, in the dark, to stay.

She did not yet have the word for what she had become. She would find it in Book III, on a shore, with a knife. For now she only sat with it, and was quiet, and in the morning sorted the manifests, because the work went on.

<p align="center">⁂</p>

With the obstruction gone, the commission was placed.

Through Wylla, paid out of the off-books dice-coin, three layers of paper deep, its course the reagent-map's warm road south — a hull ordered through enough intermediaries that no man who touched the coin could say whose it was or where the ship would carry her. *Nothing here is mine,* Gael had decided at the vise, and the sentence carried its one silent footnote, the thing she would have denied if anyone had been able to ask: *except Wylla.* The one possession she meant to take beyond the walls, filed, of course, as logistics — *transport of essential asset, voyage south.*

The cruel fold she never saw and the reader was left to assemble alone: that the vessel meant to carry Wylla out of all of it was being built through Wylla's own two hands.

Two floors below, Otto Hightower stood over the hole where five men had been and could not make it close. His vise had been meant to yield leverage; it had yielded a corpseless disappearance instead — no bodies, no thief, no demand, no error, only a suitor and his party gone clean out of the world and a wedding-clock now running against a thing he could not name. He probed it and learned nothing, lost his men and gained no ground, and the larger the hole stayed the louder it became, until it was no longer an opening he could exploit but a lead he could not let go — the lead that would become, in the year ahead, a manhunt.

He had wanted to close his hand on a small girl. He had found instead that the hand closed on nothing, and that men who reached into the nothing did not come back, and he resolved, in the cold grandfatherly way of a man who has lost a piece and means to win the game, to find out where they had gone.

Up in the solar, Gael filed the whole affair under a heading and forgot it. *A vendor dispute,* she wrote, in the bestiary, in the small fair hand she used for things that did not matter, *resolved.* And she turned to the chart of the southern routes, and traced with one finger the warm road down, port by port, all the way to the open water where no one's face was at stake and no one's hand could reach, and thought how very *unlucky* it had been, for those men.
