*I had won my last world by becoming a hive of bugs, and I had not meant to wake. I woke anyway in another body — a foreign woman with no name in this city, in King's Landing, in the year before the year before the year the dragons would dance. The flies came with me. Two hundred metres of them, dense as the air. The first thing I decided was that nothing I had done at the end would be done here.*

Three weeks in this ward and I have learned the specific weight of doing nothing. I sleep in the angle of a Hook alley where the drain runs and the air is full of small bodies I refuse to use. I have a count. I hold the count at its edge by the cost of holding it, every hour I am awake. The arrangement is that nobody here knows my name and I know nobody's name, and the arrangement is what I am paying for.

I came here to be useful without taking control. That is the rule I set before any of the rest of it. The rule is what I am holding the flies in for. The rule is older than anything in this city. The rule is what I have left.

The drain water threaded the angle-gap — the Hook, the lane-warren of Flea Bottom, King's Landing's slum-district where transient smallfolk went to be unremarkable — and what Flea Bottom called a ward was a trade household that took in a parentless child for shelter and light work, not warm and not cruel. Morning in the stitch-house lane.

The tallow smoke crossed the stitch-house lane, the smell of it arriving before the light had settled, the lane-ambient giving way to onset. The holding cost a quarter of her at the count's edge, and she paid it on principle every morning. The feet hold the only discipline she brought from before that still does what it was built for — and she held the feet now, at the lip of the angle-wall, still. The insects swelled; the swell was the suppression slipping by a quarter measure, and she had pulled it back before the count completed. The angle-wall narrowed the lane to a single body's width, and she stood in the narrowing and did not move. She exhaled once, slowly, and the count held.

A handcart of the kind the morning fish-trade ran through the Hook from the river-side gate had stopped crosswise in the lane, blocking it full. The ground had carried the child's breath before the crowd had registered the absence of one — a small breath, too shallow, a child on the cobblestones by the cart's wheel. The crowd compressed; she felt it in the cobblestones underfoot before she saw it in the backs of the bodies, the packed texture of flesh pressing against uneven stone shifting into something denser, the pre-compression baseline giving way. She held the feet again, her breath going nowhere. The lane-mouth pressure was already priced — the dozen she would need, and the second dozen she would not. The insects propagated; the propagation went where she had told it to go, and the telling was a thing she would not name. The nearest dozen bodies yielded, not knowing why their legs had stepped aside, or not caring. The gap propagated outward from the child, one body's width opening into two, two into enough.

She moved through the gap and crouched beside the child. She raised her voice.

"Fever. Not the croup."

"She needs air. Stand back."

"Who knows her? Fetch them."

She lifted her hands and held them visible.

After the gap closed, the crowd came apart the way Flea Bottom crowds did. She watched the lane thin from the inside — the fish-cart man had turned to face her, reading her the way they all eventually read her, and two women had turned to face the lane, already moving. The elder had taken the lane-mouth a week before he would have the word for it — the Mudway man, a Hook ward-elder who had stood thirty years on these streets and named what he saw without explaining why he had noticed, stepped to the lane-mouth as the crowd gave way, his hands settling at his apron-front, doing the work a word would do in a week. The child cleared the lane, led by the women who had answered. The gap closed behind her.

She turned her face to the alley-mouth and set her body there, so the stitch-house lane would not need to be registered again.

The tallow smoke layered the lane-floor in the low morning light, pooling at the cobblestones. The chin-lift filed her in a category she recognized the shape of without needing the country's name for it; the chin lifts the way it lifts in the country's older stories when a word has been waiting for a person and the person has finally arrived to fit it. The elder's chin moved, unhurried. He had stood thirty years on these streets naming what he saw.

The stitch-maker's ward — a Hook girl of eleven who watched cloth before she cut it, and watched people the same way — turned her face toward Taylor, her eyes leading her head around before the rest of her moved, finding her across the lane-floor the way she would find a seam in dark cloth, without being able to say how she had known to look.
