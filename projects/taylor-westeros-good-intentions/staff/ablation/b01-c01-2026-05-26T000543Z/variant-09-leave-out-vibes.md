*I had won my last world by becoming a hive of bugs, and I had not meant to wake. I woke anyway in another body — a foreign woman with no name in this city, in King's Landing, in the year before the year before the year the dragons would dance. The flies came with me. Two hundred metres of them, dense as the air. The first thing I decided was that nothing I had done at the end would be done here.*

Three weeks in this ward and I have learned the specific weight of doing nothing. I sleep in the angle of a Hook alley where the drain runs and the air is full of small bodies I refuse to use. I have a count. I hold the count at its edge by the cost of holding it, every hour I am awake. The arrangement is that nobody here knows my name and I know nobody's name, and the arrangement is what I am paying for.

I came here to be useful without taking control. That is the rule I set before any of the rest of it. The rule is what I am holding the flies in for. The rule is older than anything in this city. The rule is what I have left.

The drain water threaded the angle-gap in the stitch-house lane — the Hook, the lane-warren of Flea Bottom, King's Landing's slum-district where transient smallfolk go to be unremarkable — and what Flea Bottom called a ward (a trade household that took in a parentless child for shelter and light work, not warm and not cruel) had its lamp burning at the far end of the lane, and the stitch-house-lamp cast a thin stripe across the cobblestones where the drain-water moved. The holding cost a quarter of her at the count's edge, and she paid it on principle every morning. The feet held the only discipline she brought from before that still does what it was built for. Tallow smoke crossed the stitch-house lane, and she noticed the smell of it — the lane had been clean before, pre-tallow-onset, and now was not. The angle-wall narrowed the lane at the far end where the drain ran, and the insects swelled against the edges of the count. She exhaled.

A handcart of the kind the morning fish-trade ran through the Hook from the river-side gate came crosswise in the lane and blocked it. The ground had carried the child's breath before the crowd had registered the absence of one. The child was down somewhere at the cart's near side, and the crowd that had been moving through the lane closed on itself and compressed — the cobblestones pressed hard through the soles of her shoes where the crowd's weight added to her own, flesh-against-flesh compression from all sides, the uneven angle-wall side of the lane made worse as the bodies packed. She held the feet. The lane-mouth pressure was already priced — the dozen she would need, and the second dozen she would not. The insects propagated, and the propagation went where she had told it to go, and the telling was a thing she would not name. The nearest dozen bodies yielded. The gap propagated outward through the press. She moved to face the child. She raised her voice.

"Fever. Not the croup." She lifted her hands so the nearest faces could see the gesture. "She needs air. Stand back." A beat, watching the calculation cross them. "Who knows her? Fetch them."

After the gap closed, the crowd came apart the way Flea Bottom crowds did. The crowd thinned. The fish-cart man turned to face her, reading something, then decided against whatever he had been about to say and looked away. Two women faced down the lane. Oswyn — the Mudway man, a Hook ward-elder who had stood thirty years on these streets and named what he saw without explaining why he had noticed — took the lane-mouth; the elder had taken the lane-mouth a week before he would have the word for it, and his hands settled at the apron-front. The child cleared the lane. The gap closed. She set the body to the alley-mouth so the stitch-house lane would not need to be registered again.

Tallow smoke layered the floor of the lane. Oswyn's chin-lift was the kind she recognized the shape of — the chin-lift filed her in a category she recognized the shape of without needing the country's name for it, and that category assembled itself the way it assembled in the country's older stories when a word had been waiting for a person and the person had finally arrived to fit it. Oswyn lifted his chin. Across the lane, the stitch-maker's ward — a Hook girl of eleven who watched cloth before she cut it, and watched people the same way — faced her, her eyes leading her head toward Taylor; she filed the registration in the same fold where she kept the things that did not enter the ledger.
