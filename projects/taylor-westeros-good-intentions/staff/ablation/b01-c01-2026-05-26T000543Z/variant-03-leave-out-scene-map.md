*I had won my last world by becoming a hive of bugs, and I had not meant to wake. I woke anyway in another body — a foreign woman with no name in this city, in King's Landing, in the year before the year before the year the dragons would dance. The flies came with me. Two hundred metres of them, dense as the air. The first thing I decided was that nothing I had done at the end would be done here.*

Three weeks in this ward and I have learned the specific weight of doing nothing. I sleep in the angle of a Hook alley where the drain runs and the air is full of small bodies I refuse to use. I have a count. I hold the count at its edge by the cost of holding it, every hour I am awake. The arrangement is that nobody here knows my name and I know nobody's name, and the arrangement is what I am paying for.

I came here to be useful without taking control. That is the rule I set before any of the rest of it. The rule is what I am holding the flies in for. The rule is older than anything in this city. The rule is what I have left.

The drain water threaded the angle-gap where the cobblestones pinched closest to the wall, in the Hook — the lane-warren of Flea Bottom, King's Landing's slum-district where transient smallfolk go to be unremarkable — and the stitch-house lamp was still burning at the far end of the lane. Morning. The drain trickle was audible if you stood at the right place along the angle-wall, which I did, which was not a coincidence (what Flea Bottom called a ward — a trade household that took in a parentless child for shelter and light work, not warm and not cruel — ran behind the stitch-house two lanes over, and the ward's working hours started before the lamp went out). The holding cost a quarter of her at the count's edge, and she paid it on principle every morning. The feet hold the only discipline she brought from before that still does what it was built for.

The tallow smoke crossed the stitch-house lane — smell of it arriving before the cart that carried it would have, the lane going from pre-smoke ambient to tallow-smoke-onset between one breath and the next. I held the feet. The count was at its edge. The swell was the suppression slipping by a quarter measure; she had pulled it back before the count completed.

The angle-wall narrowed the lane at the far end, and I exhaled.

The fish-cart blocked the lane — a handcart of the kind the morning fish-trade ran through the Hook from the river-side gate — crosswise, one wheel lodged in the drain-rut. The ground had carried the child's breath before the crowd had registered the absence of one. The crowd compressed around the blockage, the cobblestones going from uneven-at-angle-wall to crowd-compression underfoot, bodies pressing into the tactile fact of other bodies, the lane not wide enough for the cart crosswise and the people trying to move around it.

I held the feet. The lane-mouth pressure was already priced — the dozen she would need, and the second dozen she would not. The insects propagated out from the count's edge into the crowd — the propagation went where she had told it to go, and the telling was a thing she would not name. The nearest dozen bodies yielded, opening a gap. The gap propagated along the lane.

I faced the child. I raised my voice.

"Fever. Not the croup."

"She needs air. Stand back."

"Who knows her? Fetch them."

I lifted my hands.

After the gap closed, the crowd came apart the way Flea Bottom crowds did. The crowd thinned. The fish-cart man faced me across the lane without moving, watching. The two women who had been pressing toward the child's side of the lane turned and faced the lane-mouth instead, giving it their backs. Then the elder took the lane-mouth — the Mudway man, a Hook ward-elder who had stood thirty years on these streets and named what he saw without explaining why he had noticed — and the elder had taken the lane-mouth a week before he would have the word for it, hands settling at the apron-front with the particular stillness of a person who has just finished composing something internal. The child cleared the lane. The gap closed.

I faced the alley-mouth. The tallow smoke layered the lane-floor, heavier now, settling into the cobblestone-hollows where the drain-rut ran. I set the body to the alley-mouth so the stitch-house lane would not need to be registered again.

The elder lifted his chin — the chin-lift filed her in a category she recognized the shape of without needing the country's name for it. The chin lifted the way it lifts in the country's older stories when a word has been waiting for a person and the person has finally arrived to fit it.

Wren faced me — the stitch-maker's ward, a Hook girl of eleven who watched cloth before she cut it, and watched people the same way — her eyes leading her head toward me before the rest of her orientation completed. The ward's face had found hers; she filed the registration in the same fold where she kept the things that did not enter the ledger.
