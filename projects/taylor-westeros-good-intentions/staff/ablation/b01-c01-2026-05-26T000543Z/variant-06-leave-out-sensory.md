*I had won my last world by becoming a hive of bugs, and I had not meant to wake. I woke anyway in another body — a foreign woman with no name in this city, in King's Landing, in the year before the year before the year the dragons would dance. The flies came with me. Two hundred metres of them, dense as the air. The first thing I decided was that nothing I had done at the end would be done here.*

Three weeks in this ward and I have learned the specific weight of doing nothing. I sleep in the angle of a Hook alley where the drain runs and the air is full of small bodies I refuse to use. I have a count. I hold the count at its edge by the cost of holding it, every hour I am awake. The arrangement is that nobody here knows my name and I know nobody's name, and the arrangement is what I am paying for.

I came here to be useful without taking control. That is the rule I set before any of the rest of it. The rule is what I am holding the flies in for. The rule is older than anything in this city. The rule is what I have left.

---

The drain water threaded the angle-gap in the stitch-house lane — the Hook, the lane-warren of Flea Bottom, King's Landing's slum-district where transient smallfolk go to be unremarkable — and through the gap's geometry the morning ran its first tally of itself (what Flea Bottom called a ward: a trade household that took in a parentless child for shelter and light work, not warm and not cruel). The tallow smoke crossed from the stitch-house side of the lane and hung at the level of a standing person's chest.

She held the feet. The holding cost a quarter of her at the count's edge, and she paid it on principle every morning — the feet hold the only discipline she brought from before that still does what it was built for. The angle-wall narrowed the lane at its turning-point, pressing the space closer than the street beyond it. She exhaled. The count stayed where she had put it.

Then the fish-cart blocked the lane — a handcart of the kind the morning fish-trade ran through the Hook from the river-side gate — and the lane stopped moving.

The ground had carried the child's breath before the crowd had registered the absence of one. Down in the press of the lane the child was there, below the level of what the crowd had looked at, and the ground was what knew it first. The crowd compressed into the angle, person against person, the lane-mouth pressing from the far end so there was no direction that opened. The lane-mouth pressure was already priced — the dozen she would need, and the second dozen she would not. She held the feet again. The insects swelled — the swell was the suppression slipping by a quarter measure; she had pulled it back before the count completed. She had not let it go; that was not the word for what happened at the lane-mouth. The propagation went where she had told it to go, and the telling was a thing she would not name. The nearest dozen bodies yielded without being asked. The gap propagated through the lane the way a lack of resistance moves, which is not slowly.

She was facing the child before she had accounted for the steps. She raised her voice.

"Fever. Not the croup."

"She needs air. Stand back."

"Who knows her? Fetch them."

She lifted her hands with the palms out, the universal shape that means I am finished with my part of this.

---

After the gap closed, the crowd came apart the way Flea Bottom crowds did.

The fish-cart man faced her without approaching. The two women at the lane's edge turned toward the mouth without speaking. Then the Mudway man — Oswyn-Mudway, a Hook ward-elder who had stood thirty years on these streets and named what he saw without explaining why he had noticed — took the lane-mouth; the elder had taken the lane-mouth a week before he would have the word for it, and his hands settled at the front of his apron as if whatever he had been holding had been put away. The child cleared the lane behind him, passed from the press into the open space at the street-end. The gap closed.

She set herself toward the alley-mouth — she set the body to the alley-mouth so the stitch-house lane would not need to be registered again. The tallow smoke had come down to the lane-floor while she hadn't been watching the lane. It lay across the cobbles in a flat layer. The Mudway man lifted his chin, and the chin-lift filed her in a category she recognized the shape of without needing the country's name for it; the chin lifts the way it lifts in the country's older stories when a word has been waiting for a person and the person has finally arrived to fit it.

Then there was the stitch-maker's ward — a Hook girl of eleven who watched cloth before she cut it, and watched people the same way — facing the lane. The ward's face had found hers; she filed the registration in the same fold where she kept the things that did not enter the ledger. The girl's eyes had led her head around, the way eyes do when they have already decided what they are looking at.
