*I had won my last world by becoming a hive of bugs, and I had not meant to wake. I woke anyway in another body — a foreign woman with no name in this city, in King's Landing, in the year before the year before the year the dragons would dance. The flies came with me. Two hundred metres of them, dense as the air. The first thing I decided was that nothing I had done at the end would be done here.*

Three weeks in this ward and I have learned the specific weight of doing nothing. I sleep in the angle of a Hook alley where the drain runs and the air is full of small bodies I refuse to use. I have a count. I hold the count at its edge by the cost of holding it, every hour I am awake. The arrangement is that nobody here knows my name and I know nobody's name, and the arrangement is what I am paying for.

I came here to be useful without taking control. That is the rule I set before any of the rest of it. The rule is what I am holding the flies in for. The rule is older than anything in this city. The rule is what I have left.

The drain water threaded the angle-gap at the base of the wall, finding the low channel between the cobblestones, and I watched it go without assistance — the Hook, the lane-warren of Flea Bottom, King's Landing's slum-district where transient smallfolk go to be unremarkable, carrying its ordinary morning through its ordinary geometry. Around me was what Flea Bottom called a ward — a trade household that took in a parentless child for shelter and light work, not warm and not cruel — and the stitch-house lamp was still burning at the lane-turn, its yellow light thinning in the early grey.

The tallow smoke crossed the stitch-house lane from the rendered fat burning somewhere close, and the smell came up through the cold drain-smell, replacing it. I held the feet — the holding cost a quarter of her at the count's edge, and she paid it on principle every morning — and the feet held: two hundred metres of the count staying where the count was told to stay, not used, not loose. The feet hold the only discipline she brought from before that still does what it was built for. The insects swelled at the count's edge, a brief swell, and the swell was the suppression slipping by a quarter measure; she had pulled it back before the count completed. The angle-wall narrowed the lane here, pressing what would later become a crowd into the geometry it was already offering. I exhaled.

The fish-cart blocked the lane — a handcart of the kind the morning fish-trade ran through the Hook from the river-side gate — crosswise, its wheel caught in the gap between the angle-wall stones, so that what had been a passable lane was now a press. The ground carried the child's breath before the crowd had registered the absence of one; the ground had carried it before I had a word for what I was reading, and what I was reading was a child down in the compression ahead, breathing badly. The crowd compressed, and the cobblestones underfoot became the pressure of other people's feet, other bodies, the lane growing smaller from every direction at once. I held the feet again. The lane-mouth pressed the crowd from behind, and the lane-mouth pressure was already priced — the dozen she would need, and the second dozen she would not. The insects propagated. The nearest dozen bodies yielded, not knowing why, opening a channel between them the way a crowd will sometimes open around a thing it has not yet agreed to name. The gap propagated out from the yield. I faced the child.

I raised my voice. "Fever. Not the croup." The crowd stilled enough to hear it. "She needs air. Stand back." The nearest bodies continued their yielding, the gap widening, and the propagation went where she had told it to go, and the telling was a thing she would not name. "Who knows her? Fetch them." I lifted my hands, palms out, fingers spread, the gesture that everywhere meant: I am not holding anything dangerous.

After the gap closed, the crowd came apart the way Flea Bottom crowds did. The fish-cart man faced me, reading me for a moment, then looked away. The two women nearest the lane-mouth faced the lane, resolving what they had seen into something ordinary. The elder had taken the lane-mouth — the Mudway man, a Hook ward-elder who had stood thirty years on these streets and named what he saw without explaining why he had noticed — and by the time he had it, the child had already cleared the lane. The gap closed behind her. I faced the alley-mouth and set my body toward it so the stitch-house lane would not need to be registered again.

The tallow smoke had settled lower, layering the lane-floor in the cold morning air, drifting against the cobblestones below knee height. The Mudway man lifted his chin. It was not a gesture to me; it was a gesture to himself, the hands settling at his apron-front, his body composing itself around something that had arrived. A week before he would have the word for it, the elder had taken the lane-mouth and the chin-lift filed her in a category she recognized the shape of without needing the country's name for it — the chin lifts the way it lifts in the country's older stories when a word has been waiting for a person and the person has finally arrived to fit it. The stitch-maker's ward — a Hook girl of eleven who watched cloth before she cut it, and watched people the same way — faced me then, her eyes leading her head around toward mine, though she had no name for the direction she was turning.
