*I had won my last world by becoming a hive of bugs, and I had not meant to wake. I woke anyway in another body — a foreign woman with no name in this city, in King's Landing, in the year before the year before the year the dragons would dance. The flies came with me. Two hundred metres of them, dense as the air. The first thing I decided was that nothing I had done at the end would be done here.*

*Three weeks in this ward and I have learned the specific weight of doing nothing. I sleep in the angle of a Hook alley where the drain runs and the air is full of small bodies I refuse to use. I have a count. I hold the count at its edge by the cost of holding it, every hour I am awake. The arrangement is that nobody here knows my name and I know nobody's name, and the arrangement is what I am paying for.*

*I came here to be useful without taking control. That is the rule I set before any of the rest of it. The rule is what I am holding the flies in for. The rule is older than anything in this city. The rule is what I have left.*

The drain water threaded the angle-gap where the lane pinched between the two leaning walls and ran on toward the lower courses of the Hook — the lane-warren of Flea Bottom, King's Landing's slum-district where transient smallfolk go to be unremarkable — and I have wondered, since, how a thing as small as a thread of water through stone can hold a morning in place, although that morning held for other reasons, none of which I had yet been required to name. The tallow smoke from the stitch-house two doors up crossed the lane at about the height of my shoulder, which was a thing it had done every morning for three weeks and which I had come to recognize the way I recognized what Flea Bottom called a ward — a trade household that took in a parentless child for shelter and light work, not warm and not cruel — by the small daily evidences that did not ask to be noticed.

I held the feet. The discipline lived there and not anywhere else: in the joint of the ankle and the press of the heel against the cobble, in the keeping-still that was a form of argument with myself, the only argument I had brought from the place before that still did what it was built for. The insects swelled at the count's edge by a quarter measure and I pulled the swell back before the count completed, the way one pulls back a hand that has begun to move toward a door it should not open, and the angle-wall narrowed the lane the way it had narrowed it every morning, indifferent to whether I had noticed it or not.

I exhaled. The exhale was what the holding cost — a quarter of me at the count's edge, paid on principle every morning, paid before I knew the morning would ask for it.

A fish-cart blocked the lane farther down — a handcart of the kind the morning fish-trade ran through the Hook from the river-side gate, sideways and stuck — and before I had walked toward it the ground had already begun to carry what the crowd did not yet know. The ground transmits a child's breath before the crowd registers the absence of one. The insects told me this, although telling is the wrong word; the count thinned at one specific point of the lane and the thinning was a fact about the body of a person small enough to fall and not be caught, and I knew the fact the way I knew the angle of the wall, which is to say I had not asked to know it.

The crowd compressed. The dozen nearest bodies and then the second dozen, the lane-mouth pressing them inward, all of it priced in my count before I had moved, because the dozen I would need and the dozen I would not need were already separate categories in the only ledger I keep. I held the feet. The discipline did what it was for, and held, and held, and the lane-mouth pressed.

Then the insects propagated. The propagation went where I had told it to go, although I had told it nothing in the way the word told meant to anyone who was not me, and the telling was a thing I would not name. The nearest dozen bodies yielded the way water yields at a stone that has appeared underneath it without disturbance, and the gap opened, and the gap propagated outward, and the child was at the end of the gap on the cobblestones with the gray look of a small fever, not the croup, and I was facing her without having moved through the steps it would have taken to face her.

I raised the voice. "Fever," I said. "Not the croup." And then, because the words had arrived already arranged in the order they would need to be in: "She needs air. Stand back." The crowd stood back, which was a thing the crowd did, and I lifted the hands toward the woman whose face had moved to the front of the crowd and said, "Who knows her? Fetch them."

After the gap closed, the crowd came apart the way Flea Bottom crowds did. The fish-cart man faced me and did not say anything that I needed to hear. Two women faced the lane and did not face me. The Mudway man — a Hook ward-elder who had stood thirty years on these streets and named what he saw without explaining why he had noticed — took the lane-mouth, by which I mean he placed his body at the lane-mouth in the way a man places his body when a thing is being recorded, and the elder had taken the lane-mouth a week before he would have had the word for it. The child cleared the lane in someone's arms. The gap closed behind her.

I faced the alley-mouth, by which I mean I set the body to the alley-mouth so the stitch-house lane would not need to be registered again, and the tallow smoke layered the lane-floor at about the level of the ankles, which was lower than it had been at the morning's open and which meant the air had cooled, which meant the morning was older than I had let myself notice.

Oswyn Mudway lifted the chin. The chin lifted the way it lifts in the country's older stories when a word has been waiting for a person and the person has finally arrived to fit it, and the chin-lift filed me in a category I recognized the shape of without needing the country's name for it.

And the stitch-maker's ward — a Hook girl of eleven who watched cloth before she cut it, and watched people the same way — faced me from the stitch-house doorway. She had been there, I understood, for some part of the morning I had not measured. She did not look away. I did not, either.
