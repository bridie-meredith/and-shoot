*I had won my last world by becoming a hive of bugs, and I had not meant to wake. I woke anyway in another body — a foreign woman with no name in this city, in King's Landing, in the year before the year before the year the dragons would dance. The flies came with me. Two hundred metres of them, dense as the air. The first thing I decided was that nothing I had done at the end would be done here.*

*Three weeks in this ward and I have learned the specific weight of doing nothing. I sleep in the angle of a Hook alley where the drain runs and the air is full of small bodies I refuse to use. I have a count. I hold the count at its edge by the cost of holding it, every hour I am awake. The arrangement is that nobody here knows my name and I know nobody's name, and the arrangement is what I am paying for.*

*I came here to be useful without taking control. That is the rule I set before any of the rest of it. The rule is what I am holding the flies in for. The rule is older than anything in this city. The rule is what I have left.*

Drain water threaded the angle-gap where the stitch-house lane pinched against itself, the trickle audible against cobblestone. This was the Hook, the lane-warren of Flea Bottom where transient smallfolk go to be unremarkable. The stitch-house lamp burned. The cobblestone ran uneven on the angle-wall side.

The tallow smoke crossed the lane on the morning air, smell shifting up out of ambient into the burning fat of a household waking.

I held the feet. The feet were the only discipline I had brought from before that still did what it was built for, and the cost of holding them at the count's edge was a quarter of me, paid every morning on principle.

The insects swelled at the range-edge — suppression slipping by a quarter measure. I pulled it back before the count completed.

The angle-wall narrowed the lane.

I exhaled.

A fish-cart blocked the lane, the kind the morning fish-trade ran through the Hook from the river-side gate, set crosswise where the lane bent.

The ground carried the child's breath before the crowd had registered the absence of one.

The crowd compressed, cobblestone going from underfoot to flesh-against-flesh in the narrow throat of the lane.

I held the feet.

The lane-mouth pressed the crowd back upon itself. The pressure was already priced: the dozen I would need, and the second dozen I would not.

The insects propagated. The propagation went where I had told it to go, and the telling was a thing I would not name.

The nearest dozen bodies yielded.

The gap propagated.

I faced the child.

I raised my voice. "Fever. Not the croup." A pause for the next breath. "She needs air. Stand back." Then, into the half-yielded silence: "Who knows her? Fetch them."

I lifted my hands.

After the gap closed, the crowd came apart the way Flea Bottom crowds did.

The fish-cart man faced me.

The two women faced the lane.

The Mudway man took the lane-mouth, a Hook ward-elder who had stood thirty years on these streets and named what he saw without explaining why he had noticed. He had taken it a week before he would have the word for it.

The child cleared the lane.

The gap closed.

I faced the alley-mouth.

The tallow smoke layered along the lane-floor.

Oswyn lifted his chin. The chin lifted the way it lifts in the country's older stories when a word has been waiting for a person and the person has finally arrived to fit it.

The stitch-maker's ward faced me. A Hook girl of eleven, who watched cloth before she cut it and watched people the same way. Her face had found mine; I filed the registration in the fold where I kept the things that did not enter the ledger.
