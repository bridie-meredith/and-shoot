# Cold-read — b01-c09

First-time reader, text only. Blunt.

## 1. EVENTS
Honestly, almost nothing. In order:
- An italic opening monologue tells me (doesn't show me) that some "arrangement" is holding, a count runs the Hook, a stitch-maker named Wren gets fed, and a courier has been filed.
- Morning circuit: the narrator walks a lane south of the Hook, watches Wren walk from her shop toward the water-sellers, and mentally "files" her route as a pattern on a map. No incident. She reaches a corner. That's it.
- Evening circuit: the narrator walks the Dragonpit margin, sees a parked supply cart and a stone-post, then sees a man ("Corwick") standing where he shouldn't be, facing a second man, squaring his shoulders. The narrator decides this means something about factional loyalty, "prices the posture," and closes the entry.
- End of day: the narrator folds a packet, seals it with wax, and the wax dries.

So: two surveillance walks and the sealing of a letter. The entire chapter is one character observing and cataloguing. There is no action, no dialogue, no contact between people. I can name maybe three physical events and all three are "someone walks / stands / seals wax."

## 2. JEOPARDY
Gestured at, never delivered. The text implies the narrator is some kind of surveillance intelligence ("the architecture," "indexed lives at a scale," "the map") that catalogues people, and that this is quietly ominous — Wren is being tracked without her knowledge. The Corwick moment hints at a faction conflict ("a Green-faction gate," "the split's ending"). But nothing is *at risk* on the page. No one is threatened, pursued, or harmed. The menace is entirely atmospheric. **Effectively no jeopardy** within this chapter, only the suggestion that surveillance could become dangerous later.

## 3. CAUSALITY
There are only three beats and they don't cause each other — they're just three sequential observations by the same narrator on the same day. Morning walk doesn't lead to the evening walk; the evening walk doesn't lead to the wax. They're a list, not a chain. I could not tell *why* the narrator does any of this beyond "it's the routine circuit." I could not tell what the sealed packet contains, who it's for, or why it matters — the text deliberately withholds it ("the only thing the channel would ever see"). I could not tell who Corwick is or why I should care.

## 4. PAYOFF
No. The ending is a wax seal drying. It's presented as if it's a weighted, fateful act ("the weight I had already settled"), but I have no idea what was decided, so the gesture is hollow. Nothing was earned because nothing was shown — no decision I could follow, no consequence I could feel. The chapter mistakes withholding for tension.

## 5. CONTINUE?
No — I'm not confused so much as starved; ten lines of someone cataloguing routes and sealing an unexplained letter gave me no person, stake, or question to chase.

## 6. ONE-LINE SUMMARY
A surveillance-minded narrator takes two patrol walks — filing a woman's daily route and clocking a suspicious man — then seals a mysterious letter, and that's the whole chapter.

---
**Where I was confused:** the prose itself. Sentences like "the architecture takes one more set of days and does not distinguish the keeping of a route from the keeping of a person" are abstract to the point of fog. I had to reread to confirm "the feed" / "insect-feed" means the narrator perceives through some distributed sensor network (insects?). The names — Wren, Corwick, Jarvis, the Hook, Dragonpit, Greens — arrive with zero grounding. A first reader gets atmosphere and withholding; almost no concrete event.
