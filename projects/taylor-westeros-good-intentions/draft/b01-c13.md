*In the weeks since she began routing what her insects show her through Jarvis to Otto Hightower's people, Taylor Hebert had handed up the kind of intelligence the arrangement paid for — among it, at one delivery, a list of ward-elders in the lower precinct whose lane-contacts her pattern-reading had marked as Black-faction-adjacent. She delivered it in plain functional description and did not watch where it went. This week she watches.*

---

The blowfly took the crate-ledge at the edge of the loading platform, low and back, where the salt-fish brine off the platform had thickened the morning lane-air into something I could read the room by, and I held it there while the household-agent stood the trestle-table across from the supplier. He was a Green man, the agent, running a Green household's accounts, and he had the table between himself and the salt-fish supplier the way a man keeps a counter between himself and a thing he means to handle. The supplier faced him and said nothing I needed; the geometry said it. The son did the rest. Eleven years old, maybe, the supplier's boy, and he shouldered the wrapped crates up onto the platform and kept his eyes on the wood while he did it, because he had already learned that the looking was the part that cost.

The agent ran the tally. I watched him do it through the fly's flat thousand-faced read of him — the figures going down, the margin pared and pared again, a copper-penny shaving off a load of salt fish that could not have carried more than a few coppers of profit to begin with. A margin that thin was not the point. A man does not work that hard over a few coppers unless the coppers are the instrument and the working is the thing. He was confirming something. The supply chain ran where it ran and bent where it was told to bend, and the agent already knew this, and the tally was how he made the supplier know that he knew it.

The blowfly gripped the ledge. The account closed.

At the close the agent's shoulders dropped — not slumped, not tired, but let down into the body the way weight settles when a thing is finished and finished as wanted. I had the words for him already. What the drop added was only the body confirming a thing that had never actually been in question. The son picked the last empty crate up off the platform and turned it over to stack it, eyes still on the wood. I released the blowfly.

The fly took the ceiling-corner of the rented back room four days later, up under the eave-timber of a chandler's house where the Hook frayed out into the broader ward network, and it read the proceeding from above. The Green-faction clerk had set a document at the table's edge before any of it opened — laid it down flat and squared it and stepped back. The man they brought in took the chair. Aldric, a ward-elder of the lower precinct, one of the men whose name had been on the list, brought before the proceeding on a charge of receiving stolen cord.

The magistrate wrote on the procedural-form before Aldric had finished speaking. I watched the stylus move ahead of the man's mouth — the verdict going down onto the paper while the answer was still being given, the questions asked in a register that did not want answers because the answers were already inscribed. Aldric lifted the cord up off the table to show it, the length of cord he was charged over, holding it out in the way of a man who believes the object will explain him if he only holds it high enough. The cord was not the mechanism. I knew which one was.

The magistrate set the cord aside without looking at it and lifted the document the clerk had squared at the table's edge. It came up into his hand, and he read down it, and the verdict that had already gone onto the form took its authority from the page he was holding. The cord was the pretext. The list was the engine. The document in his hand was the one I had set down in functional description and handed to Jarvis — my own flat sentences about who walked which lanes, lifted now by another hand to decide a man.

The fly gripped the ceiling-corner. His name had been on my list; his hands were flat on the table now. I held the connection where it sat. I did not move it across into the accounting column, did not open the page where that kind of entry would go — only held it, the list and the hands, the two of them in the same look and not yet in the same ledger. Across the room Aldric gripped the table edge, both hands gone white at the knuckle, gripping it the way a man grips a rail when the floor has done something the floor is not supposed to do — not understanding it, only that it had happened and was happening to him. The fly worked its hold on the timber and held the read.

That evening I stopped on the lane.

It was a lane I had walked five hundred times, and I stopped in the middle of it, mid-step, on the ordinary stone in the ordinary evening with the tallow-smoke drifting across the open throat of the street from somebody's rushlight. The two of them came up together and stood in the same accounting — the agent's shoulders going down at the account-close, the magistrate's hand lifting my paper to seal a verdict already written — and a word arrived to meet them, and it arrived in the functional register, which is the only register I keep. The word was contempt. Not resentment. Resentment is diffuse; resentment files as temperament and sits in a column and stays there. This was a verdict about quality, and the evidence was already counted and already true: the copper-margin worked like a tool, the form written before the answer, the ward-elder list I had delivered turned and used the way it was always going to be used. I had had the texture of this since the first time the resentment found an object to take. The word came now and stood outward, against the thing, and under it the colder knowing sat where it had always sat — that the people the apparatus spends are spent the way a coming fire spends what is in its way, as weather, never as a column anyone keeps.

The breath went out and my weight settled back onto the heel that had stopped. I set my jaw and held the word inside it, carried to no channel, set down on no page where Otto's people would ever find it.

Then I walked on. The lane ahead was the lane behind, unchanged before the word and unchanged after it. The contempt did not bend the route by a single stone. It was a finding, and the ledger knows what to do with a finding, which is nothing — file it, hold it, walk on under it. A finding, not a decision.

Two days later Halvard found the water-trough at the lower end of the Hook, where the chandler's row gave out onto the open ground before the fishmongery, and he was filling for somebody — water-carrying on another man's behalf, the way the septons did, in his ordinary circuit and not lying in wait for me at all. I came up to the trough from the lane approach with the stone cool and dry under my reach and the trough-rim cool where my hand found it, and he straightened when he saw me, and he spoke.

"They took up Aldric. You'll have heard. I knew him — knew the lanes he walked and who he talked to on them, and it was not what they put to him. He carried cord. He carried word between two old women who can't walk the hill themselves. That was the worst of him, and they made it the worst of something else. Somebody decided what he was from a long way off, from a street they've never stood in, and they got it wrong. A man who's never set foot in the Hook can't tell you who's a danger in it. He can write a name down. The name is enough for them."

The long way off was me. He did not know it was a mirror he was holding up.

"It won't be slower because I ask it to be," I said. "It won't be gentler, or pick more carefully whose name it writes down. You can be slower than it is. The people it's already deciding on can't wait for you to reach them. I've counted what your way costs and what mine costs — both columns, all the way down. Mine keeps more of them alive. There's no cleaner way that holds the same line for the same price. I know there isn't. I went looking for one."

It was the same counter I had run at this trough before, in other weeks, against the same slower voice that named the wrong and had no colder method to put in the wrong's place. Nothing in the running had ever bent the route, which was why I did not wait for the end of it.

"You can know a street five hundred times over and never know what it weighs to be the name on the page," he said. "I've buried men a wiser hand wrote off. I'll bury Aldric too, like as not. And I'll not have the comfort of telling myself it had to be him."

I left the trough while he was still speaking, crossed past its edge into the open lane-mouth ahead.

I walked the route. The feet stayed on the circuit, one stone and the next, the way they went every day, and the gap his counter would have opened was a gap I did not turn toward — and the not-turning had the shape of walking, had no shape but that, the body going on along the round it always went.

Behind me Halvard worked the water-skin under the trough-spout and let it fill, the sound of the water going into it rising and then closing off as the skin came full, and he set it on his shoulder for the next house. The counter went on existing where I had left it, unanswered, in the mouth of a man who did not know whose hand he was naming, and he carried it on into his circuit the way he carried the water, and neither of us stopped.
