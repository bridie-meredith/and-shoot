# Maester-Chronicler Voice — Coda-Only Archival Register

## Description

Governs the register, scope, placement, and prohibitions of the maester-chronicler witness voice. This voice appears once — at the book's close, as the coda — and constitutes the structural irony engine's revelation moment. It is not a recurring voice. It is not available as a mid-book perspective shift. It arrives at the end as a different document.

## Sensory Impact

The register-break is itself a sensory event for the reader. The coda arrives as a change in texture, rhythm, and intimacy. The body (Taylor's first-person) is close, compressed, present-tense in feel. The coda is distant, past-tense, cold. The register-break should feel like the door opening from a warm room into an unheated archive.

## Duration

Single occurrence at book's close. The coda is not seeded, foreshadowed, or prepared by any other passage in the same voice. It arrives without setup. The absence of setup is load-bearing — the archival voice is a surprise precisely because no narrative preparation has trained the reader to expect it.

## What the Coda Is

**An archival record fragment.** The maester-chronicler is writing for the Citadel's historical archive — the same archive from which Fire & Blood is drawn. The coda is not fiction pretending to be an archive; within the story's world, it is an archive. Its presence at the book's close positions the events Taylor has lived through as history being documented.

**A retroactive revelation.** The coda knows what happened after the story ends. It names outcomes Taylor could not have known at her story's close. This retroactive view is the mechanism through which the bad end is confirmed, the cost-bearer's fate is stated, and the counterfactual is named.

**The irony mechanism.** The gap between what the body showed (Taylor's interpretation, Taylor's logic, Taylor's ledger) and what the coda states (historical fact, outcome, the counterfactual path not taken) is where the tragedy lives. The body earns the reader's sympathy for Taylor's reasoning. The coda reveals the cost of that reasoning in the flat archival register. The reader supplies the grief; the coda does not.

## What the Coda Must Contain

**The explicit counterfactual:** The coda must name, in declarative archival language, what would have happened had Taylor's intervention not occurred. Not implied. Not hedged. Stated. Format: "Had [specific event Taylor prevented or caused] not occurred, [specific downstream consequence] would/would not have followed." The counterfactual is the load-bearing structural requirement — the moment the irony becomes inescapable.

**Nessa's death:** The coda must record Nessa's death as historical fact. Not described with warmth or grief — recorded in the archival register as a documented consequence of the opening violence of the Dance. She is named; her death is placed in its historical context; the prose does not mourn. The coldness of the archival register is itself the register's work.

**Taylor's fate (stated or implied):** The coda confirms that Taylor is no longer operating — dead or expelled by the close. This does not require explicit cause-of-death narration; it requires that the record makes clear her absence from subsequent events.

**Ordering:** The counterfactual is named first. Nessa's death is the final image. This ordering is not reversed. A coda that names Nessa's death before naming the counterfactual is a structural violation requiring revise.

## What the Coda Must Not Contain

**Warmth or grief in the archival register.** The maester-chronicler writes cold. No elegiac phrasing, no language that mourns Taylor or Nessa. The reader's emotional response is their own work; the coda does not guide it.

**Ambiguity in the counterfactual.** "One might speculate" or "it could be argued" or "some historians have suggested" — these are hedges that defuse the irony mechanism. The counterfactual is stated in the declarative. The chronicler is not making an argument; the chronicler is recording what the archive shows.

**Narrative theme-narration.** The coda does not say "thus did good intentions produce ruin" or any equivalent. The theme is in the structure of what the coda reports, not in language that identifies the theme. The chronicler writes fact; the reader reads theme.

**Taylor's interior.** The coda is third-person, past-tense, archival. It does not access Taylor's reasoning, her intentions, or her moral accounting. It records actions and outcomes. Taylor's logic is entirely absent from the coda's register — the logic lived in the body; the coda records only what can be documented from outside.

**Maester sympathy.** The chronicler is not a friendly narrator. He is not hostile either. He is institutional — recording for the archive what can be verified. He would describe Taylor in the same register he would describe a battle or a famine: precisely, without editorializing, with appropriate institutional distance.

## Register Specifications

**Tense:** Past tense throughout. The body is present-tense in feel; the coda is unambiguously retrospective.

**Person:** Third-person. Taylor is referred to by the name the chronicler has for her (likely not "Taylor Hebert" — the chronicler would have whatever name or descriptor KL's records assigned to her: "the foreign woman," "the witch of Flea Bottom," or whatever name she used in this world).

**Sentence structure:** Declarative, formal, periodic. The chronicler's sentences are complete. He does not use fragments. He does not trail. His sentences end and mean what they say.

**Length:** The coda is short. It arrives with the weight of the whole book behind it; it does not need length to land. A paragraph or two — enough to state the counterfactual and record Nessa's death. Nothing more.

**Source attribution:** Optional but in-register. A chronicler may attribute the record to a specific source — eyewitness account, Watch record, household testimony — if this adds archival texture without muddying the declarative clarity.

## Interaction Notes

**With `cond-taylor-pov-behavior`:** That card governs the body's register. This card governs the coda's register. They are designed to produce maximum contrast — the body is close, first-person, present-tense in feel; the coda is distant, third-person, past-tense. The contrast is not a stylistic flourish; it is the structure through which the retroactive revelation lands.

**For auditor use:** Flag any coda that does not explicitly name the counterfactual in declarative language. Flag any coda that names Nessa's death before naming the counterfactual (ordering violation). Flag any warmth or grief in the coda's archival register. Flag any coda that accesses Taylor's interiority or reasoning. Flag any coda longer than approximately two substantial paragraphs — length bloat in the coda dilutes the register-break effect. Flag any coda that appears before the book's final pages (it is not a mid-book tool).
