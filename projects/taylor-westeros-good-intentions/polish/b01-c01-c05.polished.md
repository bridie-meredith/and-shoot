Chapter 1
═══════════════════════════════════════

*I had won my last world by becoming a hive of bugs, and I had not meant to wake. I woke anyway in another body — a foreign woman with no name in this city, in King's Landing, in the year before the year before the year the dragons would dance. The flies came with me. Two hundred metres of them, dense as the air. The first thing I decided was that nothing I had done at the end would be done here.*

*Three weeks in this ward and I have learned the specific weight of doing nothing. The late-summer dust is still on the cobbles at the alley-mouth where the drain runs out, the kind of dust that comes off the city before the first hard rain breaks it. I sleep in the angle of a Hook alley where the drain runs and the air is full of small bodies I refuse to use. I have a count. I hold the count at its edge by the cost of holding it, every hour I am awake. The arrangement is that nobody here knows my name and I know nobody's name, and the arrangement is what I am paying for.*

*I came here to be useful without taking control. That is the rule I set before any of the rest of it. The rule is older than anything in this city. The rule is what I have left.*

---

The drain water threaded the angle-gap of the stitch-house lane in the Hook — the lane-warren of Flea Bottom, King's Landing's slum-district where transient smallfolk go to be unremarkable — the trickle audible at the pinch-point, cobblestone-underfoot uneven at the angle-wall side.

Lane-ambient — then tallow-smoke-onset: the smoke crossed the stitch-house lane.

I held the feet. The feet held the only discipline I'd brought from before that still did what it was built for; the holding cost a quarter of me at the count's edge, and I paid it on principle every morning.

The insects swelled. The swell was the suppression slipping by a quarter measure; I'd pulled it back before the count completed.

I exhaled.

The fish-cart blocked the lane — a handcart of the kind the morning fish-trade ran through the Hook from the river-side gate.

The ground carried the child's breath before the crowd registered the absence of one.

The crowd compressed.

I kept the feet.

The lane-mouth pressed the crowd — the dozen I'd need, and the second dozen I wouldn't.

The insects propagated where I'd told them to go, and the telling was a thing I wouldn't name.

The nearest dozen bodies yielded.

The gap propagated outward.

I faced the child.

I raised my voice.

"Fever. Not the croup," I said. "She needs air. Stand back. Who knows her? Fetch them."

I lifted my hands.

After the gap closed, the crowd came apart the way Flea Bottom crowds did.

The fish-cart man faced me. Two women, side by side at the edge of the press, faced the lane.

Oswyn Mudway took the lane-mouth — the Mudway man, a Hook ward-elder who had stood thirty years on these streets and named what he saw without explaining why he had noticed. His hands settled at his apron-front. The elder had taken the lane-mouth a week before he would have the word for it.

The child cleared the lane.

The gap closed.

I faced the alley-mouth. I set my body to it so the stitch-house lane would not need to be registered again.

The tallow smoke layered the lane-floor.

Oswyn lifted his chin. The chin-lift filed me in a category he recognized the shape of without needing the country's name for it.

The stitch-maker's ward — a Hook girl of eleven who watched cloth before she cut it, and watched people the same way — faced me. Her face found mine, eyes first. I filed the registration in the same fold where I kept the things that did not enter the ledger.

Chapter 2

*The crowd had broken open around the stitch-maker's ward, and the hands had gone up before mine did. After that I went back to the drain angle and did not feed anyone for three weeks. The late-summer ran out somewhere in those three weeks and the Hook started keeping its mornings by the salt-day rhythm — the fishmongers' three-day, the carter-day, the lay-day the salt-cured trade kept between. I held the count at its edge by the cost of holding it. What I did instead was start to listen — at first because subsistence-reading is what survives in the Hook, and then because the listening had a shape I had begun to extend on purpose. The rule was the same rule. Reading was not directing. I had not crossed the line.*

*What I had built I did not yet have a name for. The flies and beetles told me where heat clustered and where foot-traffic knotted. I held a count of bodies in my head — who was sick, who was hungry, which alley junction emptied before dark and which one never did. I called it a coverage map when I was being plain with myself; I called it an accounting when I was not. The instrument was harm-reduction. That was how it stayed permitted.*

---

The flies came back from the alleys carrying heat where the bodies were, and the beetles came back from the thresholds with the weight of who had crossed and how often. I let the readings settle. Then I left the drain angle.

The fever-cluster was the unresolved one — three signatures, two alleys, none mine to price yet.

I sent the rest of the insects out wide. The ward gave me back what it had: heat where I expected it, weight where I expected it. I took the lane-mouth slow, with the wall against my right shoulder.

Four hundred bodies sat in the feed at once. The ceiling.

Then the alley-back went quiet. Not the bodies leaving — the feed leaving the bodies. I had not yet decided to push the range, and the dropout was already the bill for it.

I pushed the range. I drew the line at the ceiling I had just paid for.

The insects spread out over the Hook, alley by alley, finding their count. Foot-traffic thickened where I had known it would thicken — at the ward-junction, a corner in the Hook where three or four alleys gave onto one another under a single precinct-ward's reach.

Days of it, and the pattern came.

The insects returned a body the map had no slot for. A junction-signature where the foot-traffic knotted — neither a sick body nor a hungry one, neither one that stopped at the threshold-stones nor one that did not. The flies and beetles had a category the map had not written yet. The hands kept the count even.

The tallow smoke rolled at floor-level out of the stitch-house east wall and pooled in the lane. The coverage map anchored on the threshold there because the threshold was where the count concentrated — the beetles returned the threshold-crossings.

I took the drain angle. The lane-mouth and the alley beyond went north, forty paces off, and the hands carried me to the gap and set me there the way they had set me there yesterday and the day before that.

The ward-junction body crossed the thresholds. The stitch-maker's ward crossed from the stitch-house's east side into the alley behind it — I yielded the alley-mouth before the yield was a choice. The yield had been priced days back, when the insects first put her in the feed; what arrived at the lane-mouth now was the receipt. The threshold was occupied from both sides at once and I did not look up.

The map assigned the connector-type.

I turned from the alley-mouth. The insects filed the ward-junction contact.

The gesture moved in a hand that knew the motion, from another accounting under another architecture, and the hand kept going. The head was elsewhere, holding the new entry, not signing it.

I took the drain angle again in the afternoon. The light had moved since morning — cutting across the tenement wall at a lower angle, falling slant down the brick on the far side of the gap.

The shadow filled the drain angle, and the gap was the darkest point in the lane.

I ran the map.

The fever-cluster corner returned its three bodies above ambient. The dark-junction corner returned the silence-pattern. The map returned the bodies — all of them, in their slots.

The accounting closed the count, reached the ward-junction entry, returned the junction-lane void.

The count stalled. I held the breath I had just taken.

The line was the same line I had drawn at the alley-mouth and it came up under the same hand. I did not look at it. The hand drew where it had drawn before — same hand, same motion, an hour and an angle of light away from the first drawing — and the shape of the line was what a watching street would find a word for, and I did not give it the word.

I closed against the drain angle. The body closed before the decision did — chest into shoulder, shoulder into the brick, the weight on the rear foot as if that were the foot that had been priced. The drain ran below the heel.

The ledger closed the fever-cluster, the dark-junction, the ward-junction contact. The last one cost more than the others. The cost did not appear in the closing.

I exhaled and went on holding the angle against the dusk.

Chapter 3

*By the end of that day I had a map and no name to put on it. The flies told me the streets and the streets told me the count and the count closed where the ward-junction body crossed the threshold of the stitch-house and the dark-junction corner went silent in the same hour. I did not feed anyone. I did not direct. The rule was still the rule. What I had built I had not yet shown to anyone, and that was the second rule — the one I had not stated to myself in those words.*

*The morning market was where the streets condensed. It was a salt-day, the second of the fishmongers' three, which meant the stalls would run heavy until the third bell and thin sharp after — the rhythm the Hook kept its weeks by, fixed and unwritten. I walked it because the count needed the foot-traffic densities the flies could not give me at distance, and because moving through the stalls was the cheapest way to refresh the map without giving the map a watcher.*

---

The morning market filled the Hook shoulder-to-shoulder, tallow-wax and fish-salt thick in the foot-traffic, and I moved through the stalls without stopping, reading the densities through the feed. The feed returned the stitch-maker's ward at the salt-fish row a beat before the eyes did — filed alongside the other recurring bodies the market gave back. She did not look up. The count carried her past and went on.

At a salt-fish stall a man stood with his hands at his sides, eyes on me before I had registered him as a presence — Jarvis Coin, a courier of the kind the lower city ran for patrons who did not put their names on the routes, transactional in register, paid by the delivery.

The feed had him three seconds before the eyes did. Stillness against the market grain — the cost-tell.

I slowed my pace by one step, hands loose, eyes on the next stall rather than on him.

He spoke.

"The foreign woman who cleared the crowd off Butcher's Lane the morning the Mudway child took fever. That's you."

I went still, weight on my back foot, both hands visible at my sides. Hands visible was the cheaper answer. The back foot was the one I would not show him.

"You walk the same six streets between dawn and the third bell. The sick cluster where you cluster. Three alley-mouths off Pisswater Bend you check twice before the second bell, and no one else on that route looks at them once."

He had not guessed — he had counted. The count was the size of the leverage he was offering not to use, and it had a shape I had once spoken into another architecture from the side that did the counting.

The feed registered the distance between us as the distance of a street-side negotiation, unremarkable to anyone passing.

"There is a man who would like to put a question to you. The cooper's yard off Eel Alley, third bell, day after tomorrow."

The rescue I had run was already read. Him naming the alley confirmed the tether closed.

"The cooper's yard. Third bell."

I moved past his stall position without looking back, the feed tracking his departure route.

The cooper's yard off Eel Alley (a lane two cuts north of the river-side stalls, near enough to the Hook for the tidal smell, far enough that the third-bell market did not reach it) held two workers at the near shed and a boy stacking hoops at the far end — no foot traffic past the third bell, no clear sightline from the lane-mouth. The market-crowd noise gave way to a yard-worker murmur.

I reached the yard before him, back to the shed wall, clear line to the yard-mouth, the feed running on the two workers and the boy.

Jarvis entered at the third bell with no document, no letter, no seal, and took a position three steps from the yard-mouth.

"Otto Hightower. The Hand who was. The man who still runs the channels the Small Council does not audit."

Otto Hightower had been Hand of the King — the chief office under the throne, the manager of the realm's business when the king's attention was elsewhere — until his dismissal two years before. He had not stopped working.

I registered the name and did not shift my weight or my eyes from the yard-mouth. The name landed with the precision of a man who had been calibrated; the recognition cost something I had already paid once.

"What he wants is what you already see. Movement in the wards below Rhaenys's Hill. Where the sickness gathers in the months before winter pressures the wells. Which junctions go restless and which go quiet in the weeks the Small Council sits. Frequency you set. Detail you set. Routed through me."

Rhaenys's Hill rose east of the Red Keep, the Dragonpit at its crown — the wards below it ran from the noble-quarter approaches down toward the lower city.

I ran the inventory of wards-below-Rhaenys's-Hill coverage against the feed I already held. The coverage ran through the wards he had named.

"Sera Hightower. Fourteen. A ward of the cadet branch, kept in the Queen Consort's household. Her father's claim has a question in it. If that question reaches the Small Council, she is put out. It goes into the record. The record does not come back out. Three months is all it would take, with the right man to set it there. The patron has kept it from the council twice. He needs the cover to do it again."

She had been placed in Alicent's household for the kind of political advancement minor branches purchased with appropriately-presented daughters.

The wards he had named were already in the feed; I was being asked to sell coverage I was running. A girl of fourteen, put out of the household, the place forfeit, the question set in a record that did not reopen. My hand closed once at the cloth of my dress and opened.

"I have heard you."

The trade-shape sat between us in the tallow-damp air of the cooper's yard: a named ward at a named risk, a named patron, a named product, no answer yet. At the far end the boy lifted a hoop to the stack and did not look over. Jarvis stood without adding to the proposal, hands at his sides, weight distributed for someone who had delivered and was waiting. I held my position with my back to the shed wall and the yard-mouth in clear line.

"A day."

My eyes stayed on the yard-mouth, weight even on both feet. A day was the smallest unit of deferral the trade-shape would accept; I had already counted what asking for it committed.

He considered me for a beat — the beat in which a courier registered that the answer was not refusal.

"Same place. First bell. Day after."

Same place, first bell — the courier's confirm read as the close; I had engaged before the utterance finished.

He left the yard by the lane-mouth with the same unhurried pace he had arrived with.

I did not move from the shed wall until the feed confirmed his exit. The coverage and the open line sat in the accounting the way a chain that had tightened sat inside the choice that tightened it — a known weight inside an already-priced load.

I left the cooper's yard by the lane-mouth, the feed still running on the street beyond it.

*I had one day. The Maiden's name day fell on the third of the fishmongers' three, and a sept-bell would carry it across the Hook at first light whether anyone in the Hook marked it or not. The proposal sat on the night-side of the count the way an unpaid debt sits at the edge of a ledger. I did not decide twice. I decided in the hour the dark thinned and the flies came back inside the count, and what I decided was that the harm I could prevent was the only column the accounting would close — the column whose first name I had been carrying since before there was any coverage to close it against, a child two seasons back the slower way had buried before anyone could route a call in time. The terms I had named for myself I would name to the courier at first bell, in the yard, in the same place he had named the question. The rule I had not stated in those words now had a price tag and a destination both.*

---
The tallow-damp off the lane-caulking reached the shed-wall before the yard itself came into view, and the cooper's-yard workers were holding their smallfolk-hours murmur at the far side of the shed in the way that meant nothing had yet asked them to look up. I crossed into the yard at that register, took the shed-wall at my back, and let the timber put the lane-mouth across open ground in front of me.

The feed had him a span before the lane-mouth did. The insects placed him in the lane and returned him to me, and only then did Jarvis step into the lane-mouth in the body my eyes were waiting on.

My hand closed on the shed-wall behind me. The yes went across the yard-air in the cadence of a settled count, and the modification carried the only parameter the prohibition still held.

"Yes. The terms hold with two changes. You will have patterns — what moves through the streets, what does not, where the seams sit — not raw report of who said what to whom. The interval is mine to set. The volume is mine to set."

I held my feet.

"The man I serve takes those terms," he said. "The volume yours. The interval yours. The substance pattern-reports from the wards you walk. Same place. First bell. Three days."

His cadence carried the receipt without the conditional that would have left the yes open; the receipt-form had settled before my body finished holding for it.

The feed held him through the hook-range as he turned. He exited the lane-mouth. I gave it the breath it required, and then I exited the cooper's yard.

The insect-range went outward one ward. The agitation at the junction-mouth mapped itself out through the feed — three alleys, the middens-cart axis, the bodies working the rendering at the angles the carter-work imposed.

The penny-a-barrel carter pulled the middens cart up at the junction-mouth and set the brake.

The feed returned Oswyn Mudway on the carter's near side — the elder I had met once in the second month, placed by the routing at penny-a-barrel work this hour, recognition arriving along the gait before the eyes were given the face. The body the routing already accounts for, the feed said back, and I did not let the next thought finish.

Pig Tallow Lane returned ward-tier bodies and nothing above ward-tier. At the range's far reach, the stitch-house frames stood at the next ward's edge.

The feed returned Wren Stitch-Maker at the second-ward range. She would not be written down.

Roper's Court came out of the early-morning grey with its sound emptied. The tributary lanes ran off long and dark at their far reaches; the central court held sight-clear to every mouth. A Flea Bottom craft-trade ward turning on rope-making, its lanes linear enough to serve as rope-walks — the geometry would read cleanest under the grey.

The insect-range went outward to its fourth ward. The saturation-cost crossed past the load I had carried before, and it sat at that crossing without resolving. Four wards opened into the feed at once — the Hook, the stitch-house frames, the waste-middens junction, Roper's Court — a single read held across the four footprints together.

I held the four-ward feed and walked the court.

By the time the first bell came up out of the Hook, I was back at the cooper's yard with the shed-wall at my back and a half-yard of open air between the timber and the lane-mouth. Jarvis Coin stepped into the lane-mouth at the count the feed had given him, half a span before the body arrived in the eye.

He raised a folded square of parchment to chest-height between us. The crease ran clean across it, and the paper held in his hand without cover. He did not open it. The display was the receipt — Otto's confirmation that Sera was managed and that the three-month window had closed — and the parchment carried that receipt at the height where a hand at the lane-mouth would have to register it.

I took the sheet from my coat. It was folded twice, the smaller fold inward, and I crossed the yard-air between us and put it into his hand at the height his note had been raised to.

The half-step of yard-air closed around the sheet's last contact with my fingers, and the cost had crossed before my hand had registered it crossing. I held my feet at the close of the step.

He pocketed the sheet into the inner seam of his coat without unfolding it. The coat-cloth settled over the place where it had gone, and his hand returned empty to his side.

What sat in his coat now sat at ward-pattern observation. The sheet held the lanes I had walked — which alleys carried what, which seams ran where the carter-work routed around them, the body-counts the wards held at the hours I had read them — and it did not hold the name of anyone who had spoken to anyone in any of those lanes. Nothing reached above ward-level.

He gave the brim of his cap the small touch the receipt-form required, turned in the lane-mouth, and exited at the gait he had entered.

I let the empty yard hold for the breath it required, then exited on the line away from the lane Jarvis had taken.

The four-ward feed ran outward as I walked back through the Hook — body-counts at the near range, waste-middens junction at the mid, Pig Tallow's rendering-yard angles beside it, Roper's Court at the outer reach.

The four-ward read continued through the return, and the report had already gone the other way in Jarvis's coat. The body that walked through the Hook carried both of them at the same count.

The north-end lane-mouth at the stitch-house frames came up at the edge of the Hook alley. My stride held the four-count past the lamp-frame at the lane-mouth and past the second frame beyond it, and the count did not break for the threshold the lane carried at its north end. I crossed past the stitch-house lane and held my line into the Hook.

The chandler's-storehouse corner came up at the cross-lane. The feed returned the Septon at his corner — Halvard, the Faith's man for this stretch of the Hook, body filed as fixture from the first week, his gait mapped a hundred times against the storehouse wall and the sept-bay shadow. The crossing of our paths returned no surprise to the count. I gave him the brief nod a person gives a fixture they have logged, the nod that costs the same coin every morning, and the corner went past on the right side without breaking my stride.

*I had given the four-ward sheet to the courier in the cooper's yard, and the arrangement that ran through him was now a thing I had named to myself and walked back from in the same hour. The accounting closed. The harm I could prevent was still the only column the accounting would close. The coverage from the four wards held overnight. The first cold had come in two nights back, the kind that doesn't lift off the stone before the bells — the bay-wind turning, the dry summer-dust on the cobbles gone over to a damp that fingers found on every surface they touched. This morning the count reached past the stitch-house lane into a ward where the alleys lean up toward the hill — ground I had not walked before, ground that abuts the lower servant-passages of the Red Keep. The method was the same. The substrate was about to be different.*

*The arrangement bought protection for a fourteen-year-old ward of a Hightower cadet branch placed in Alicent's household. Her name was Sera. Her standing rested on a question about her parentage that Otto's rivals at court could surface to damage him; Otto kept the question quiet by knowing what was moving before they did. The intelligence I routed through the courier was the form that paid for the quiet. I had agreed to this. I read court bodies now.*

---

The hill's stone skirt met the lane-mouth at grade, cool damp under the paving where the overnight had not lifted, the light arriving from the harbor side and stopping at the north face of the junction in shade.

I stepped into the Rushwick — a lane-cluster between the hill's stone skirt and the city's upward lean, alleys for passing not selling, ground abutting the lower Red Keep servant-passages — and held at the junction's edge.

The feed filled the junction. Insect-bodies on lintels, in wall-seams, under cart-shadow; the count settled the way it had always settled, the same instrument bedding in on new ground.

A provisioner-train came through from the south side, four carts and the men walking them, cart-wheels grinding over the paving in the heavy uneven rhythm of stone not yet worn smooth at this turn. I read them the way I had read every body since coverage came up: the forward-lean of men carrying loads under a tight delivery window, no affect required to file them.

The train took the east-lane, the stone narrowing along the hill-skirt as the lane fell off into the morning light past the wall.

A message-runner came across after them, alone, no load, a tighter compressed gait — the body of someone accountable to someone above, walking on the interval he was paid to walk. He took the lane-mouth at the threshold where the coverage I held released him, and the feed let him go past the wall before the next body entered.

Then the next body entered.

He came on at the same threshold the runner had taken, walking the heel-first gait the feed had now flagged three mornings running on the same lane — the courier, a man filed as recurring body, no name attached. The hold I kept at the wall-line was the hold I had used at every junction since coverage came up — the discipline at the body, not at the cognition, the count steady against the building gap where the sightline broke.

The three figures came in from the side.

They entered the side-alley off the east exit, one-person-wide stone passage, the alley-mouth visible from where I held and the interior not, and they took the mouth at full width and held it.

The third struck him.

The side-alley returned the sound. The walls turned the noise of impact against stone inward, and then the alley carried it out at the threshold — the low, effortful sound a body makes against stone when it is trying not to make any sound at all, no cry in it, only the work of force absorbed and the work of not answering the force — and the feed had no field for what the alley delivered.

The courier raised the spine. He found the feet.

I filed the enforcement-record. Three figures, coordinated, side-alley containment, directed at a specific person for a specific reason — not a robbery; the geometry was wrong for a robbery; the body-count and approach-angle filed as enforcement-incident, clean entry, no affect required. I delivered the report into the same form that had held junction-agitation and passage-avoidance: movement-pattern, body-count, approach geometry, resolution. The form took it.

The three figures cleared the alley-mouth, east-lane open behind them, and went east in the order they had entered.

I added the courier to the body-map — recurring-Rushwick-resident, enforcement-incident attached, no ledger entry where his face would have a name. He took the junction-corner forty feet from the wall-line, the corner at the east turn where the sightline ran clean, and the count let him go where it had let the runner go before him.

I ran the Hook-feed first, the morning's coverage of the lane below the Hook brought back through me in the same shape it had gone out, and the feed delivered the count and the body-flags and the gait-records and closed itself out clean, every entry resolved against its return, the lane settling into the form it kept when nothing about it had asked to be remembered. The color that had been on the junction at the morning hour was on the junction at the evening hour, present in the return the way it had been present in the live read, the feed routing the provisioner-train through the same architecture and bringing the architecture back with the train. I ran the pass a second length to see if it was the read and not the feed; the color persisted.

My head tilted toward the held color. I stopped the rushwick-pass.

The stopping was the body's, before I had a word for what the body was doing. The hand I had laid flat on the boards came up off the boards. The feed was an apparatus I had been keeping neutral, an instrument with a use, and the instrument was recognizing the route it carried as the route — not the lane, not the courier, but the architecture the routing went through, and the architecture was the architecture I had not asked the feed to mark. I ran the rushwick flat-read. The provisioner-train held the rushwick-pass; the train re-crossed the junction at the morning hour and the architecture came back with it. The pass would not run as the Hook had run, would not close out clean against its return. The door I had turned did not open at the turn.

The courier-walk held the rushwick-pass.
