## Per-entry seams (non-1 entries only)

**@13 (rated 2):** SEAM — The writer's axis-citation frames the officer's speech as "the formal opening declaration that activates the selection frame for all in the yard," invoking the public-exposure analog. But the rubric's speech-beat default (anti-pattern 2) explicitly states "Speaking is by itself a 1. The dialogue file may be 3-charged, but the *proto-line beat* is 1 unless something else lights." The writer's defense depends entirely on reading the speech as a registration event rather than a speech act — but the proto-line SVO is "the officer speaks to the yard," which is formally a speech beat. Nothing else lights at this beat on its own face: no gaze-fix, no stylus move, no physical commitment. The writer is treating narrative function (the inspection opens here) as on-face charge, which is plot-importance inflation (anti-pattern 4) wearing a stakes-visibility label. If this ships at 2, every subsequent speech-beat in a charged scene-frame can claim the same defense.

**@23 (rated 2):** SEAM — This is a calibration anchor, but the calibration-anchor status is also the attack surface. The rubric's worked example reads "the officer's gaze fixes on taylor at the yard's far end" as a 2 on stakes-visibility (public selection) and reversal-proximity (alignment for next move). The hostile reading: the gaze is the continuation of @20 ("the officer works the line") — the officer was already moving through the yard selecting. If @20 is 1 because it's procedural non-targeting, then @23 is either the legitimate escalation from that procedural motion (supporting the 2) or it is merely the moment the procedural targeting reaches Taylor's position (making it procedural continuation, not alignment-for-turn). The seam is thin but real: reversal-proximity requires that "the next move" be meaningfully distinct, and the rubric's "alignment beat approaching a commit" standard must survive the argument that the officer targeting Taylor's end of the yard is continuous with his working the line.

**@25 (rated 2):** SEAM — The writer cites body-charge ("face held level in direct confrontation") and reversal-proximity ("held before speech-move"). The rubric's body-charge 2 requires "a held position against pressure" with the held-against-what on screen. But @24 already landed (the stylus stopped); the officer's face holding level is now in a post-rupture environment, not a pre-rupture held-against turn. The reversal already occurred. A face held level in the aftermath of a rupture reads as continuation of confrontation, not body-charge against an approaching turn. If the held-against-what (the stylus stop, the procedural commitment) is the past beat rather than this beat, the 2 here may be climax bleed (anti-pattern 3) bleeding forward from @24 rather than genuine current-beat charge.

**@28 (rated 2):** SEAM — The Phase 2 audit explicitly notes that Phase 1 rated @28 as a 3 equivalent to the @38 calibration anchor and that this auditor concurs with the 2. The seam runs the other direction: the writer rates @28 as 2 and the audit defends this as "the approach, not the commit." If @38 is 3 for deliberate exposure and body-charge peak, what separates @28 from the same class? The writer's answer is that @38 is the full commit and @28 is preliminary — but the SVO "presses forward" implies active physical commitment, not positioning. The seam is whether the line between "preliminary letter press" and "letter in the air" is a rung-level distinction or a staging distinction within the same physical commit sequence.

**@29 (rated 2):** SEAM — The writer's axis-citation is stakes-visibility ("credential now publicly visible to officer") and reversal-proximity ("seal-facing forces officer's next move"). But the rubric's stakes-visibility 2 example for a credential beat would require the credential's exposure to be the active event at this beat. Treating @29 as a distinct 2 from @28 requires that the seal-facing is a new beat of charge rather than the downstream consequence of the prior beat's action. The seam: @29 may be a continuation beat (the seal arrived in the officer's field of view as the result of @28) rather than a charged beat on its own face. If @28 is already the stakes-generating beat, @29 is the aftermath, and aftermath beats are 1 under the continuation-is-1 principle.

**@30 (rated 2):** SEAM — The writer cites "Taylor's name being marked under active registration" for stakes-visibility and "recording in motion toward commit" for reversal-proximity. The rubric's stakes-visibility 2 example is "the stylus marks two parallel lines" — but that's @64, rated 3. @30 is the stylus moving on Taylor's name before the outcome is determined. **@33 (rated 2):** SEAM — The writer and audit both accept this as a negative-event 2: "the door stays shut" constitutes a reversal because Osmynd's unavailability registers. The attack: the rubric's reversal-proximity 2 condition requires "the beat is the breath before the move, the alignment that makes the next move possible, the closing distance." A door staying shut is not the breath before a move — it is an absence. The writer is reading the absence as an active reversal, but the rubric's reversal-proximity language is consistently action-forward (the approach, the alignment, the closing distance). The stakes-visibility 2 condition requires a "watch-cost, a public exposure, a procedural commitment in motion." A door that doesn't open creates none of those directly. The negative-event reading is a creative extension of the rubric's language, not a direct application of it. If the seam holds, the rating at @33 is ambient escalation (anti-pattern 1): the scene-frame has stakes (Osmynd unreachable), but this beat is an environmental non-event.

**@35 (rated 2, auditor MISRATED):** SEAM — The audit correctly identified this as a misrating, but the writer's rationale deserves hostile examination to confirm the auditor was right rather than merely opinionated. The writer cited body-charge ("weight shift as re-engagement signal") and reversal-proximity ("officer re-orienting toward Taylor after door detour"). Both citations fail under the rubric. Body-charge 2 requires "a held position against pressure" or "a deliberate restraint" or "a charged stillness with named load" — a weight shift back to the heel is movement, not stillness. The rubric's body-charge REJECT signature explicitly reads: "Calling routine motion body-charged. Walking across a yard is 1 unless the yard is itself a charged space." Reversal-proximity 2 requires "the breath before the move, the alignment that makes the next move possible" — but the officer re-orienting toward the clerk after a side-detour is scene management, not the breath before a new committed move. The auditor's 1 is correct. The writer's citations dress up a transitional reorientation in rubric language without satisfying either axis's ACCEPT signature.

**@37 (rated 2):** SEAM — This is the direct lead-in to @38 and the writer rates it 2 for body-charge and reversal-proximity. The seam: "taylor steps into the path of the officer's shoulder" is a movement beat, not a stillness beat. Body-charge 2 requires held position, deliberate restraint, or charged stillness — not a step. The rubric's body-charge REJECT signature says "calling routine motion body-charged." Taylor stepping into a path is directional movement, even if the destination is confrontational. If body-charge doesn't hold, reversal-proximity must carry the rating alone: the step is the alignment beat immediately before the commit, which is exactly what reversal-proximity 2 means. The seam is narrow — the reversal-proximity citation is genuine — but the writer is claiming two axes when only one may hold. Downstream: the rating is defensible on reversal-proximity alone, but the body-charge citation should be rejected on its face.

**@39 (rated 3):** SEAM — The double-tap rule permits 3→3 "only when the second 3 reverses or commits the first." The writer's defense is that @39 (feet set at maximum compression) is the held-against-turn class, distinct from @38 (letter in the air). The attack: the rubric's 3→3 suspicion is stated clearly ("A 3 followed immediately by another 3 is suspicious unless the second 3 is a double-tap"). The held-against-turn class is for the beat that precedes resolution, but the commit at @38 is itself the point of no return. @39 may therefore be climax bleed (anti-pattern 3) — the physical consequence of the commit, sustained into the next beat, rather than a held position awaiting resolution. If @39 is rated down to 2, the curve loses one 3 but gains a cleaner fall-off from the climax.

**@40 (rated 2):** SEAM — The officer unfolding the letter is rated 2 for reversal-proximity ("first move of resolution; confrontation has turned") and stakes-visibility ("letter content now matters"). ## Curve-level seam

**CURVE-SEAM:** The climax-uniqueness claim is the most vulnerable structural assertion. The dramatist's curve self-audit states "no competing cluster of equal density exists" and positions @38-@39 as uniquely the densest 3-cluster. This is true on raw count (two 3s at @38-@39 vs. one 3 at each of @24 and @64), but the rubric's climax-uniqueness test is about whether the episode's structural high point is legibly distinct — not merely numerically superior. The first rupture at @24 occurs within a dense 2-cluster (@23-@25, @28-@30), which creates a first-act peak that may compete with the climax in terms of perceived local intensity. A reader tracking tension by local density rather than peak height would perceive two comparable arcs: the @23-@30 arc (1 two-beat 3 plus five 2s) and the @37-@39 arc (1 two-beat 3 preceded by one 2). The numerical advantage of @38-@39 over @24 is one additional 3 at adjacent position — which satisfies the rubric's "densest cluster" condition narrowly. The curve-level rubric requires that "every scene peaks at the same intensity" to constitute a failure, and this episode does not reach that threshold. But the claim that the climax is clearly unique rests on a one-beat margin between the two 3-clusters, which is the thinnest possible uniqueness advantage. If any 2 in the @23-@30 block is upgraded to 3 (see the @30 vs. @36 seam), the uniqueness claim collapses and the curve-shape verdict requires reexamination.
