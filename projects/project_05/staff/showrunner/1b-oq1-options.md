# OQ-1: Arrival Mechanism and Identity Relationship

Open question: What is the mechanism of Taylor's arrival in Westeros, and what is the relationship between "Taylor" and the body/life she now inhabits?

---

## Option A — Displacement: She Is the Body, the Prior Occupant Is Gone

Taylor Hebert wakes in the body of a recently-dead smallfolk girl — Tya, approximately seventeen, a tanner's daughter from a settlement outside King's Landing — at the moment of that girl's death by fever. The body is hers from first breath. There is no prior occupant to displace; the death was already complete. Taylor's shard, finding a surviving biological substrate, reseeded into the nearest viable body at the moment of its host's death, before the tissue cooled.

She has the body's muscle memory, its calluses, its healed breaks. She does not have Tya's memories. She wakes knowing Common Tongue — she speaks it fluently from the first moment, accent intact — but she has no access to Tya's relationships, her face's history, or what debts and loyalties Tya carried. To Tya's community, she is Tya, returned strange from the fever. She cannot maintain this fiction beyond the first week. She does not try. She is Taylor Hebert wearing a dead girl's face, and she does not pretend otherwise to anyone who presses.

What is publicly knowable: she survived a fever that should have killed her, she came back wrong, she knows things Tya could not know and does not know things Tya should. The tanner's community reads this as fever-madness, or possession, or something the Stranger left behind. She does not correct them. She gives herself no Westerosi name. She is called Tya by people who knew the girl, and she answers to it as a convenience.

Building blocks most enabled: identity-without-arrival (she is wearing someone else's death, not someone else's life), the Khepri problem (she displaced nothing — the girl was already dead — but she lives in the evidence of a life that ended and she did not save), information asymmetry (she has no cover story to maintain; her strangeness is public from week one and she must work with it), legitimacy without sanction (she has a smallfolk identity by accident, not strategy, and it gives her immediate material grounding in the extraction structures the story needs to engage).

Framings most enabled: The Organizing Story, The Procedural of Reform, The Tragedy of Refusal.

---

## Option B — Blank Slate: No Prior Occupant, No Inherited Identity

Taylor Hebert is born — not woken, born — as an infant in a smallfolk family in Flea Bottom, King's Landing, approximately 107–108 AC, giving her roughly seventeen to eighteen years to grow into her power before the pre-Dance period opens. She has all of Taylor's memories, compressed and present from earliest consciousness, but she lives a Westerosi childhood. She has a Westerosi name her parents gave her — Lysa, or no name the story uses, because she stops answering to it — she has Common Tongue as her first language, she has the material and embodied knowledge of growing up poor in King's Landing, and she has no prior occupant because the body was new. Her shard reincarnated her into a life she had to live.

She reaches seventeen having spent her childhood holding Taylor Hebert's memories against a Westerosi childhood's reality, already fractured. Her powers onset at adolescence — consistent with Worm's parahuman trigger model transposed to the shard-in-new-soil — triggered by a specific trauma the story authors. By the story-open, she has had years to develop her power in secret and years to calculate what she is going to do with it.

What is publicly knowable: nothing unusual until power-onset. After onset, the strangeness is gradual and attributable to whatever cover she builds. She controls the information completely.

Building blocks most enabled: identity-without-arrival (she earned nothing; she lived a life she did not choose in a body that accumulated seventeen years of Westerosi formation over the memories she already had; which self is the real one is permanently unresolved), the shape of a life after apocalypse (she spent childhood knowing what she had done; the question of whether she deserves a second life sits under every year of it), research ethics (she had years to plan her approach before acting; her experimental method is premeditated, not improvised), fear as tool vs. fear as identity (she had time to design her monster-shape before deploying it).

Framings most enabled: The Arcanist's Bargain, The Faustian Inversion, The Horror of Being the Solution.

---

## Option C — Superposition: She Occupies a Living Body, the Prior Occupant Survives Alongside Her

Taylor Hebert surfaces inside a living person — Mira, approximately twenty, a minor household scribe attached to a lesser Reach lord's retinue traveling through the Crownlands — who does not die and does not vacate. Mira's consciousness does not persist as a separate voice, but her memories, her loyalties, her relationships, and her habituated responses are present as a substrate Taylor cannot fully suppress. Taylor has full agency and full access to Mira's memories. Mira's body has never been Taylor's and they both know it, in the only way a body can know something.

Taylor speaks Common Tongue with Mira's accent and Mira's diction until stress or fatigue drops the performance. She knows Mira's employer, Mira's debts, Mira's handwriting, and Mira's reason for being afraid of water. She does not know what to do with any of this. She cannot return Mira's life to Mira. She cannot use Mira's life without becoming Mira to everyone who knew her. She walks away from the retinue within two weeks, abandoning Mira's identity completely, and has no name from that point forward that anyone gave her.

What is publicly knowable: Mira disappeared from a lord's retinue. The lord does not care enough to investigate. No one is looking for her. Taylor moves anonymously.

Building blocks most enabled: identity-without-arrival at maximum register — she is not wearing a dead girl's face or a blank body; she is occupying someone else's ongoing personhood and cannot return it; the ethical weight of this is immediate and irresolvable. The Khepri problem (she displaced someone who did not choose displacement; she did not decide to do this; it was done by her shard; she is complicit in something she did not consent to do). The pull between protecting smallfolk and using them as variables — established as a structural pattern from the first scene.

Framings most enabled: The Tragedy of Refusal (she starts the story having already committed an irreversible ethical violation she cannot remediate), The Horror of Being the Solution, The Faustian Inversion.

---

## Recommendation

**Option A.**

The brief asks for a story about a woman who builds a fear-shape deliberately, leverages her power as arcane research substrate, and stops a war by cowing both sides. All three of those require Taylor to be operating from a grounded social position — known, legible, already present in the smallfolk world — not concealed or infiltrating or managing a cover identity. Option B buries the story's opening under seventeen years of managed interiority. Option C front-loads an ethical crisis (the displacement of a living person) that immediately competes with the story's actual engine — the research, the organizing, the fear-building — and risks making the whole structure about the displacement rather than what Taylor does with her time in Westeros.

Option A gives her a smallfolk body from the start, no nobility adjacency to spurn (because there is nothing to spurn; she begins at the bottom), and a public strangeness she does not have to manage — it is already known, already attributed to the fever, already part of how her immediate community reads her. This is the condition under which the "identity-without-arrival" building block does its best work: she is not passing, she is not hiding, she is visibly not-quite-Tya from week one, and the question of who she actually is becomes the question every smallfolk character in her orbit has to answer for themselves. That is story engine, not backstory. The Khepri weight arrives undiluted — she did not displace Tya, but she lives in Tya's dead body and she has to decide what she owes that, which is exactly the calibration problem the brief's Faustian register needs.
