The stone behind me is the cold the dusk laid down and the night pressed deeper, and I take it through the overdress and the linen because moving off it would tell the yard a thing I do not want the yard to know. The bird is on the gatepost. I put it there. Down the line that runs from this wall to that post, I am holding it the way a hand holds a wire that has not yet been asked to carry weight, and the wire is humming clean — body forward into the four-point grip, head set on the road, the small held tilt that is not roosting and is not rest. The road north is empty. The road north stays empty. Half a league up the dark, the gatehouse lantern reads as a smear at the distance a point-source loses its point — the amber bleed where Harrenhal has not gone to bed and is not going to, the one warmth in a field of cold stone and frozen earth, and I am watching the smear through the stillness on the gatepost because I cannot watch the smear from here. Underneath: the yard's own birds at the lowest weight the feed delivers, the small things in the seams of the wall, the frozen ground giving back nothing through the soles. The clock is running. The cost is on a hook behind a door that is not yet open. The morning has not started for anyone but me, and the only instrument the yard holds is a bird I have set on a post to look at a light I cannot see past.
A mouse-shape steps in the seam at my left hip — wall-weight, the small one, repositioning at the pace of a thing that has had no reason to learn caution — and the passive feed takes the spike of it the way the feed takes any movement against a still field, too loud for the half-second of the crossing, the warmth of it brief against the cold mass of the wall before it sets down again into the next seam. I do not turn. I do not lift the hip off the stone to watch what my eye could not have seen anyway, because following a mouse with the head would tell the yard's own perimeter the wall has a mind on it that knows where to look, and the yard does not need to know. The wall takes another measure of the warmth I have been giving it and I let it take the measure. The small thing finishes the seam. The feed comes back to its still plane. The wire to the post is still humming the one note. The smear at half a league has not moved.
The smear changes shape. Not because I have moved — the head is where the head was, the back is where the back was, the wire to the bird is humming the same one note it has been humming — but because the one warm point at the road's end has acquired a neighbor: a second amber bleed beside the first, imprecise at half a league the way the first one is imprecise, but unmistakably separate, a count of two where there was a count of one. I take the count. I hold it. Then the third lights, and the third is the data — not the third light itself, the gap between the second and the third, the interval my body has the answer to before the head has a name for what the answer means. A watch change has its own rhythm: the drawn-out hand-off, the overlap, the tired man's pace through a thing he has done a thousand times. What I just watched is faster than that and differently shaped — fast sequential ignition, three points lit close together because the work that needs them needs them close together, the horse and the saddle and the gate lit at the cadence of a man preparing to ride. Departure. Imminent. Not the interval of a watch's rest, minutes — and the morning that has not started for anyone but me has just started for someone at Harrenhal, and that someone is mounting up. I cannot read who, or how many, or which way. Those come when the gate opens and something moves down a road that is still an empty pale track in the dark. The bird is the instrument for that. The bird is still on the post. The wire is still humming. The cost-window is still inside the clean five minutes. I have not moved. The board at the end of the road is no longer still.
I let the bird off the post — small interior unlatch, no hand-sign, no shoulder-shift, the four-point grip releasing the stone the way a held breath leaves — and the wire changes before the post does, the wingbeat coming in under the channel and the road tilting beneath the lift, the cost-window closing clean inside its five minutes and filing itself away. The post is empty behind the bird; the road runs pale under it; the smear is not a smear at fifty feet up and closing — it is surfaces under lights, and what was inference becomes legible at the rate the distance shortens. Three lights, yes. Three lights over more than three men: two at the saddle of one horse working the girth and the bridle in the cadence of hands that have done it a thousand times, a third at the gate-leaves with a bar already half-lifted out of its brackets, the seam between the leaves opening on the inside dark of the passage; and a second horse at loose tether against the inner wall, saddled, waiting the way a second horse waits when a man has not yet decided whether he rides alone. The work faces south. The horse at the gate stands head-out toward the road — toward the road I am at the sept-end of — and the gate is being opened to let it through, not closed against anything coming in. The bird narrows on the approach and the board's last quiet piece resolves: not a watch, not a drill, a rider for the south road and a second horse held in case the rider is two.
