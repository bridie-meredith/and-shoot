# s01e01

*I had won my world by becoming a hive of bugs, and I had not meant to wake. Three days later I woke anyway, in another body — a tanner's daughter dead of fever, in a village outside a king's city, the year before the Dance of the Dragons. The bugs came with me. Three hundred metres of them.*

*I'd kept a log out of habit, since before. What happened, what it cost. The clinical part of me had crossed with the bugs.*

*The first month had been awkward — the septon denied a miracle, my parents took me back in once they'd settled on 'falsely declared dead,' and the village settled on 'Tya who came back wrong.' They weren't wrong about the wrong part.*

---

I woke. The three-hundred-metre sphere arrived before my eyes did — the count already running on the flies in the thatch. I exhaled. I straightened my spine.

My father came into the room. My mother followed and set the morning bowl on the table — porridge and salt; the exits had already been mapped, and the bowl had been waiting on him. He stopped and looked at me like I was a stranger he hadn't placed yet. The Watch had been coming through the village more since I'd come back, and the cost of that was settling on him.

I reached for the salt. He pivoted to my mother. I drew the salt to me. He pivoted back, and his eyes settled on me at the angle Tya — the daughter who'd been here before me — used to hold her chin at. I hadn't adjusted for that.

The dogs came into the yard.

The chin lifted on its own — Tya's old angle, remembered by the body and not by me. I let it. My father stilled. His eyes stayed on my hands and his face didn't shift. He'd reached his verdict. I might as well have been any stranger he was still working out.

The latch trembled, and the beetles caught it a half-second before the door swung open. I turned to the door. My father tilted his head. My mother faced the door. He stepped out into the yard.

I lowered the bowl. My breath left me before the bowl met the table.

I opened the log — scrap parchment my mother had stopped asking about. I wrote down the salt. I didn't write what it had cost my mother. I closed the log.

---

After breakfast I went out to the yard.

The flies took the perimeter — the count I'd been holding since I got back. The beetles spread along the yard wall. I walked the boundary. The wasps relayed the return on the south wall. I walked the boundary again. I exhaled.

---

Mid-morning, my mother came back in. She set the afternoon bowl down. She opened her mouth.

The first note was a song Tya knew. The spiders in the rafters didn't know it. She sang the second. She sang the third —

She dropped the song. Her hands stilled on her apron, and her gaze held my face. The silence after the third note was the shape Tya should have filled.

I held her eyes. It was the only honest thing I had to give her — honest by what I wasn't saying.

She set the bowl down. She turned to the wall — there wasn't any more daughter left in me for her to look at. Then she left the room.

---

By afternoon, my father gave me the yard-edge task — his hands didn't lift from the work as he spoke it. He routed my mother and the neighbour-boy. I stepped to the yard edge. He crossed the yard. I lifted the feed bucket. He crossed the pit. I crossed the yard perimeter. He routed them both again. I stilled — I had already counted the steps to the gate, the angle of the feed bucket, the cost of staying.

---

Before sundown, the reeve came through the gate. (Our reeve was the lord's bookkeeper for village debts and the lord's hand for village peace, in that order.) The exits re-ranked themselves: the back gate moved up the list.

He spoke to my father. My father spoke back.

I crossed the yard. My shoulders set down and back. The reeve slowed.

He spoke to my father again. My father answered.

Then the reeve left.

---

Not long after, the lord's-man came through the gate at a parade beat. Different rank than the reeve, different errand — the lord's-man rode from the lord himself, and his hands were for ink and seals, not ledgers. The record book in his hand was a door already shut.

He spoke to the reeve. The reeve spoke back.

He opened the book, wrote, closed it. I was a line of ink now, in a record I wouldn't be allowed to read.

Then he was gone.

---

The next morning, the elder came. He spoke to my father in the tone he used for strangers he placed. Then he turned it on me. I would have refused if refusal had been on offer.

I faced him. I spoke. He spoke back. I stilled.

His eyes went past me to the road before I lifted the pack. The gate was the last threshold that cost nothing to cross. Everything after was the city.

I lifted the pack.

My mother faced the door. Her mouth parted on a word and the word did not come. Her face held the wood like she'd held other things she wouldn't turn to watch.

My father set his eyes on the work and did not lift them to the gate.

I crossed the yard gate. My gaze did not return to the door.

The elder walked the road. I walked the road.

---

We made King's Landing by midday — a city I had already named, in a season I had not yet reached. The alley mouth ahead was the first navigable passage into the immediate block, into Flea Bottom proper. The block filled with flies, higher than I'd extrapolated from the village. The beetles spread to the market-side junction. The wasps spread to the Fish Gate margin. I walked the alley; the canopy cut the light. The room the elder had paid for had two exits and one window and a roofline I could reach the spiders to. I'd cleared it before I set the pack down.

---

Once the pack was down, I walked the perimeter. The beetles spread along the eastern-quarter approach. The flies relayed the doorframe; the spiders fanned across the ceiling corners. The approach gave me the upper room. The room gave me a man talking to no one in particular — a maester, by the chain of his voice. (Maesters were Westeros's scholar-class — half-physician, half-cataloguer, the chained learning the realm ran on.) He spoke to the room. I was the listener the room didn't contain. The beetles relayed the sound back.

---

I walked the full perimeter. The flies took the Fish Gate margin, the beetles the market junction, the wasps the south wall. The spiders returned the room's ceiling-corners, and the corners gave me the shelf, the corners gave me the page, the corners didn't give me the words.

---

By evening the maester crossed the room above mine — his hand reached the record-table before his gaze left the south wall. He spoke aloud, and what he said landed in a part of the log I wouldn't annotate.

The beetles relayed a footfall along the south wall: the warder's. The rhythm matched the patrol's third lap, and the patrol's third lap was not due.

I straightened my spine.

He laughed. He wasn't laughing at the room. He was laughing at me.

The beetles fell silent. The silence was the shape of what he'd just looked at. I'd felt an arrival come this way before.

I opened the log. I wrote the entry. I closed the log.

---

Down at street level, the Watch crossed the Fish Gate margin — the gold cloaks, the city's patrol of last resort, in what should have been routine cadence. The cadence was two beats off. The flies confirmed it — the patrol was moving on a count I hadn't seen before.

A dock-runner entered the Fish Gate margin. The flies marked her. Her eyes cut to the alley mouth before her feet moved, and she pivoted. It wasn't evasion of the Watch. She was reading me — watching to see what I'd do. She left the margin.

My feet held. Moving would commit evidence, and anything I did would be the wrong move.

---

Within the hour, the elder came back, this time with the dock-runner. She came up to the market-side junction and spoke to him. He turned to me. His shoulder turned from the exchange before my answer landed. He was placing me into the network like he'd placed me into the labour web. The weight was different.

I faced the dock-runner. The Watch had cleared the margin thirty paces east.

She spoke. I answered. My hand stayed still at the pack-strap. The word I gave the runner was the word I had given before, to a person I could have burned and did not. It was the first thing I'd said in this city that I couldn't take back. The city would route it before nightfall.

She left the junction.

I exhaled. I opened the log. I wrote it down. I closed the log.
