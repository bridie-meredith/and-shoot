*I had won my last world by becoming a hive of bugs, and I had not meant to wake. I woke anyway in another body — a foreign woman with no name in this city, in King's Landing, in the year before the year before the year the dragons would dance. The flies came with me. Two hundred metres of them, dense as the air. The first thing I decided was that nothing I had done at the end would be done here.*

Three weeks in this ward and I have learned the specific weight of doing nothing. I sleep in the angle of a Hook alley where the drain runs and the air is full of small bodies I refuse to use. I have a count. I hold the count at its edge by the cost of holding it, every hour I am awake. The arrangement is that nobody here knows my name and I know nobody's name, and the arrangement is what I am paying for.

I came here to be useful without taking control. That is the rule I set before any of the rest of it. The rule is what I am holding the flies in for. The rule is older than anything in this city. The rule is what I have left.

The drain water threaded the angle-gap in the Hook — the lane-warren of Flea Bottom, King's Landing's slum-district where transient smallfolk go to be unremarkable — and I kept still in it, what Flea Bottom called a ward (a trade household that took in a parentless child for shelter and light work, not warm and not cruel) registering at the edges of the lane in the quiet before the morning trade began. The tallow smoke crossed the stitch-house lane, its smell arriving low and thick over the ambient drain-water reek. I held the feet — the feet hold the only discipline she brought from before that still does what it was built for — and the holding cost a quarter of her at the count's edge, and she paid it on principle every morning. The insects swelled at the edge of my awareness, and the swell was the suppression slipping by a quarter measure; she had pulled it back before the count completed. The angle-wall narrowed the lane at my back. I exhaled.

The fish-cart blocked the lane — a handcart of the kind the morning fish-trade ran through the Hook from the river-side gate — crosswise enough that the lane-mouth folded the crowd behind it before anyone had thought to push. The ground had carried the child's breath before the crowd had registered the absence of one. The crowd compressed, and with it came the shift underfoot: cobblestone-underfoot-pre-compression giving way to the pressure of bodies on bodies, the uneven lane-floor suddenly legible through boot-leather as flesh weighed against flesh. I held the feet again, the count steady at its ceiling. The lane-mouth pressed the crowd and the lane-mouth pressure was already priced — the dozen she would need, and the second dozen she would not. The insects propagated and the propagation went where she had told it to go, and the telling was a thing she would not name. The nearest dozen bodies yielded. The gap propagated outward from the child's collapsed shape. I faced the child.

I raised my voice. "Fever. Not the croup." I lifted my hands open at shoulder height, a shape the crowd could read as instruction. "She needs air. Stand back." The circle held itself. "Who knows her? Fetch them."

After the gap closed, the crowd came apart the way Flea Bottom crowds did. The fish-cart man faced me — watching, not moving, the way a man watches something he is still deciding whether to name. The two women at the lane-mouth's edge faced the lane, their attention making a shape I had learned to recognize as accounting. Oswyn of the Mudway — the Mudway man, a Hook ward-elder who had stood thirty years on these streets and named what he saw without explaining why he had noticed — moved to take the lane-mouth, the hands settling at his apron-front with the stillness of a man who had finished arriving. The child cleared the lane. The gap closed behind her.

I faced the alley-mouth. The tallow smoke layered the lane-floor in the flat dawn light. I set the body to the alley-mouth so the stitch-house lane would not need to be registered again. Oswyn lifted his chin — the chin-lift filed her in a category she recognized the shape of without needing the country's name for it — and the elder had taken the lane-mouth a week before he would have the word for it, the chin-lift the way it lifts in the country's older stories when a word has been waiting for a person and the person has finally arrived to fit it. Behind him, the stitch-maker's ward — a Hook girl of eleven who watched cloth before she cut it, and watched people the same way — faced me, her eyes leading her head toward me before the rest of her had decided to look; the ward's face had found hers, and she filed the registration in the same fold where she kept the things that did not enter the ledger.
