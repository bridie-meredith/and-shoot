*I had won my last world by becoming a hive of bugs, and I had not meant to wake. I woke anyway in another body — a foreign woman with no name in this city, in King's Landing, in the year before the year before the year the dragons would dance. The flies came with me. Two hundred metres of them, dense as the air. The first thing I decided was that nothing I had done at the end would be done here.*

Three weeks in this ward and I have learned the specific weight of doing nothing. I sleep in the angle of a Hook alley where the drain runs and the air is full of small bodies I refuse to use. I have a count. I hold the count at its edge by the cost of holding it, every hour I am awake. The arrangement is that nobody here knows my name and I know nobody's name, and the arrangement is what I am paying for.

I came here to be useful without taking control. That is the rule I set before any of the rest of it. The rule is what I am holding the flies in for. The rule is older than anything in this city. The rule is what I have left.

The drain water threaded the angle-gap — the Hook, the lane-warren of Flea Bottom, King's Landing's slum-district where transient smallfolk go to be unremarkable — and what Flea Bottom called a ward, a trade household that took in a parentless child for shelter and light work, not warm and not cruel, had its lamp still burning at the stitch-house end of the lane. The tallow smoke crossed the stitch-house lane from the west side, the smell of it arriving before the morning trade had properly started. The feet hold the only discipline she brought from before that still does what it was built for. She held them where they were. She stood with her back to the angle-wall and held the count at the subsistence range, the swell was the suppression slipping by a quarter measure, and she had pulled it back before the count completed. The holding cost a quarter of her at the count's edge, and she paid it on principle every morning. The angle-wall narrowed the lane at its northern pinch and she exhaled against it, the count holding.

The fish-cart blocked the lane — a handcart of the kind the morning fish-trade ran through the Hook from the river-side gate — crosswise and stuck, the crowd already banking up behind it. The ground had carried the child's breath before the crowd had registered the absence of one. The crowd compressed, the cobblestone-underfoot-pre-compression giving way to the press of bodies from both sides, flesh against flesh in the narrowing. She held the feet. The lane-mouth pressure was already priced — the dozen she would need, and the second dozen she would not. The insects propagated outward from the subsistence range, the propagation went where she had told it to go, and the telling was a thing she would not name. The nearest dozen bodies yielded. The gap propagated through them, a few centimetres, then a few more. She faced the child on the lane-floor.

She raised her voice. "Fever. Not the croup." The crowd had a focus now and she used it. "She needs air. Stand back." The gap was real, the bodies were holding it, and she used the last of it. "Who knows her? Fetch them." She lifted her hands and held them open where the crowd could see them.

After the gap closed, the crowd came apart the way Flea Bottom crowds did. The fish-cart man turned to face her, standing at the edge of where the crowd had been, reading the remainder. The two women nearest the lane-mouth did not disperse immediately, watching the direction she was not watching. Oswyn — the Mudway man, a Hook ward-elder who had stood thirty years on these streets and named what he saw without explaining why he had noticed — took the lane-mouth, moving from the mouth of the Mudway alley into the rescue site in a single settled step, the elder had taken the lane-mouth a week before he would have the word for it. The child cleared the lane, steered by a woman who had identified herself by coming forward. The gap closed behind them. She faced the alley-mouth opposite, setting the body so the stitch-house lane would not need to be registered again. The tallow smoke had settled to the lane-floor in the still morning air, layering against the cobblestones in the lower half-metre. Oswyn lifted his chin in the direction where she had stood. The chin lifts the way it lifts in the country's older stories when a word has been waiting for a person and the person has finally arrived to fit it. She recognized the shape of the category without needing the country's name for it. The chin-lift filed her in a category she recognized the shape of without needing the country's name for it. Wren — the stitch-maker's ward, a Hook girl of eleven who watched cloth before she cut it, and watched people the same way — faced her; the ward's face had found hers, and she filed the registration in the same fold where she kept the things that did not enter the ledger.
