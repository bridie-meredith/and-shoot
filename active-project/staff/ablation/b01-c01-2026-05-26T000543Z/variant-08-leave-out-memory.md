*I had won my last world by becoming a hive of bugs, and I had not meant to wake. I woke anyway in another body — a foreign woman with no name in this city, in King's Landing, in the year before the year before the year the dragons would dance. The flies came with me. Two hundred metres of them, dense as the air. The first thing I decided was that nothing I had done at the end would be done here.*

Three weeks in this ward and I have learned the specific weight of doing nothing. I sleep in the angle of a Hook alley where the drain runs and the air is full of small bodies I refuse to use. I have a count. I hold the count at its edge by the cost of holding it, every hour I am awake. The arrangement is that nobody here knows my name and I know nobody's name, and the arrangement is what I am paying for.

I came here to be useful without taking control. That is the rule I set before any of the rest of it. The rule is what I am holding the flies in for. The rule is older than anything in this city. The rule is what I have left.

The drain water threaded the angle-gap at the base of the stitch-house wall, the way it did every morning in the Hook — the lane-warren of Flea Bottom, King's Landing's slum-district where transient smallfolk went to be unremarkable (what Flea Bottom called a ward — a trade household that took in a parentless child for shelter and light work, not warm and not cruel). The tallow smoke crossed the stitch-house lane, coming on from the rendering-house three doors west. Taylor held the count at its feet — the range-edge was a focus, not a pain, the suppression slipping and held by the argument she made to herself every morning, which was the argument of repetition, which preceded the needing. The swell was the suppression slipping by a quarter measure; she had pulled it back before the count completed. The insects swelled at the count's margin and the holding cost a quarter of her at the count's edge, and she paid it on principle every morning. The angle-wall narrowed the lane here, pressing the air. Taylor exhaled.

A handcart of the kind the morning fish-trade ran through the Hook from the river-side gate — a fish-cart — had blocked the lane crosswise, its wheel caught at the angle-wall. The ground had carried the child's breath before the crowd had registered the absence of one. The crowd compressed, cobblestones pushing unevenly underfoot at the angle-wall side, the press of flesh adding a new weight to the lane floor, the tactile register shifting from stone to the density of bodies stacked at a narrowing. Taylor held the count at its feet. The lane-mouth pressure was already priced — the dozen she would need, and the second dozen she would not. The insects propagated out through the press of bodies — the propagation went where she had told it to go, and the telling was a thing she would not name; the prohibition crossed before it was filed, the instinct preceding the ledger entry, the deployment preceding permission, and the account was opened without ceremony. The nearest dozen bodies yielded. The gap propagated outward from the child on the ground, the crowd parting in the first-crack register of something that had no word yet in this lane. Taylor faced the child.

She raised her voice. "Fever. Not the croup." She looked at the nearest faces. "She needs air. Stand back." The crowd held a breath. "Who knows her? Fetch them." She lifted her hands, palms out, the gesture completing the diagnosis.

After the gap closed, the crowd came apart the way Flea Bottom crowds did. The fish-cart man faced Taylor and then looked away. Two women turned toward the lane-mouth and went. Oswyn Mudway — the Mudway man, a Hook ward-elder who had stood thirty years on these streets and named what he saw without explaining why he had noticed — took the lane-mouth, settling into his position with the hands settling at the apron-front, quiet, the composure of a man doing work a word would do in a week. The child cleared the lane, handed toward a known door. The gap closed behind her. Taylor faced the alley-mouth, setting the body in a direction that excluded the stitch-house lane so the stitch-house lane would not need to be registered again. The tallow smoke layered the lane-floor, continuous from the morning's first crossing, unchanged. The elder lifted his chin — he had taken the lane-mouth a week before he would have the word for it, the chin-lift filing Taylor in a category she recognized the shape of without needing the country's name for it, the chin-lift as accounting not judgment, the witness who kept the incomplete account, the gap between what was seen and what had a word composing itself in the ward's air; the witch-label was assembling, stage one, a foreign woman who knew before anyone told her, and the place would hold the event in its texture permanently.

Wren — the stitch-maker's ward, a Hook girl of eleven who watched cloth before she cut it, and watched people the same way — faced Taylor, her eyes leading her head in the direction of the woman who had opened the gap, noticed before having a word, perceptual orientation before naming, the ward's face finding Taylor's; she filed the registration in the same fold where she kept the things that did not enter the ledger.
