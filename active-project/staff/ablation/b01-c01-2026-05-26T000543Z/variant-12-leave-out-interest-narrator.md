*I had won my last world by becoming a hive of bugs, and I had not meant to wake. I woke anyway in another body — a foreign woman with no name in this city, in King's Landing, in the year before the year before the year the dragons would dance. The flies came with me. Two hundred metres of them, dense as the air. The first thing I decided was that nothing I had done at the end would be done here.*

Three weeks in this ward and I have learned the specific weight of doing nothing. I sleep in the angle of a Hook alley where the drain runs and the air is full of small bodies I refuse to use. I have a count. I hold the count at its edge by the cost of holding it, every hour I am awake. The arrangement is that nobody here knows my name and I know nobody's name, and the arrangement is what I am paying for.

I came here to be useful without taking control. That is the rule I set before any of the rest of it. The rule is what I am holding the flies in for. The rule is older than anything in this city. The rule is what I have left.

At the angle-gap in the Hook — the lane-warren of Flea Bottom, King's Landing's slum-district where transient smallfolk go to be unremarkable — the drain water threads through the pinch-point where the cobblestones run unevenly along the angle-wall side. The stitch-house lamp was still burning at dawn, and you could hear the trickle before you could see it. What Flea Bottom called a ward — a trade household that took in a parentless child for shelter and light work, not warm and not cruel — had its lamp burning too, further in, behind the smoke.

The tallow smoke crossed stitch-house lane, and with it came the smell: lane-ambient going over into tallow-smoke-onset, the kind of morning smell that settled into the stone before the foot-traffic started. The feet hold the only discipline she brought from before that still does what it was built for — she kept them planted at the angle-wall, weight even, two hundred metres of flies held at their edge by the cost of the holding. Three weeks in the drain-angle. The argument she made to herself was the repetition; the argument had gone on long enough it preceded the needing.

The angle-wall narrowed the lane from the west side. She exhaled into the tallow-smoke air, and held.

Then a handcart of the kind the morning fish-trade ran through the Hook from the river-side gate — a fish-cart — blocked the lane crosswise at the bottom of the slope. The lane stopped moving. Through the cobblestones came the breath of a child somewhere in the compression, shallow and fast. The crowd compresses, flesh against flesh, the unevenness of the cobblestones suddenly immediate through the soles, pressure distributing sideways with nowhere to go. She kept her feet where they were. The lane-mouth pressed from the north; the crowd had no corridor to enter and no reason to yield.

Then the insects propagated. It happened before she filed it — before the ledger entry, before permission. The prohibition cracked without being acknowledged as cracking. The nearest dozen bodies, pressed at the angle-wall, felt something they could not have named and shifted. The gap propagated outward from that first yielding, bodies making room the way bodies do when the information arrives below the level of a decision.

She moved through the gap and found the child on the stones. Her breath emptied out.

She raised her face and her voice into the lane. "Fever. Not the croup." The crowd's noise dropped a register. "She needs air. Stand back." The standing-back happened. "Who knows her? Fetch them." She lifted both hands so they could see she was not holding anything.

After the gap closed, the crowd came apart the way Flea Bottom crowds did. The fish-cart man, caught crosswise in the lane, turned to look at her with the particular blankness of a man deciding whether what he'd seen was any of his business. The two women nearest the child faced the lane-mouth, not her — which was a kind of answer. The crowd thinned.

Oswyn — the Mudway man, a Hook ward-elder who had stood thirty years on these streets and named what he saw without explaining why he had noticed — took the lane-mouth. He had come from the Mudway alley junction, which put him at the point where the rescue site was visible from two approaches. He stood there. The child cleared through; someone had known her and came. The gap closed.

She faced the alley-mouth. The tallow smoke had settled low on the lane-floor by then, a layer that moved when someone's foot broke it. She did not look back at the stitch-house. The chin lifts the way it lifts in the country's older stories when a word has been waiting for a person and the person has finally arrived to fit it — Oswyn's chin lifted. His hands settled at his apron-front, the composure of a man making a category in real time, not a judgment. A word had been assembling itself in the ward's accounting — foreign woman who knew before anyone told her, who had moved through people as though the space were already cleared — and the chin-lift was the accounting. He had not named it to himself yet. He did not need to.

Wren — the stitch-maker's ward, a Hook girl of eleven who watched cloth before she cut it, and watched people the same way — faced her from the stitch-house doorway. Her eyes led her head. She had been there from the beginning, or close enough to the beginning that she had a face now, and the face was filed.
