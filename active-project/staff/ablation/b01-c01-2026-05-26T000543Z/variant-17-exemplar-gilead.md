*I had won my last world by becoming a hive of bugs, and I had not meant to wake. I woke anyway in another body — a foreign woman with no name in this city, in King's Landing, in the year before the year before the year the dragons would dance. The flies came with me. Two hundred metres of them, dense as the air. The first thing I decided was that nothing I had done at the end would be done here.*

*Three weeks in this ward and I have learned the specific weight of doing nothing. I sleep in the angle of a Hook alley where the drain runs and the air is full of small bodies I refuse to use. I have a count. I hold the count at its edge by the cost of holding it, every hour I am awake. The arrangement is that nobody here knows my name and I know nobody's name, and the arrangement is what I am paying for.*

*I came here to be useful without taking control. That is the rule I set before any of the rest of it. The rule is what I am holding the flies in for. The rule is older than anything in this city. The rule is what I have left.*

The drain water threaded the angle-gap that morning the way it had threaded it the morning before, and the morning before that, in the lane that ran behind the Hook — the lane-warren of Flea Bottom, King's Landing's slum-district where transient smallfolk go to be unremarkable — and what Flea Bottom called a ward (a trade household that took in a parentless child for shelter and light work, not warm and not cruel) was the lamp burning two doors up at the stitch-house, which I had taken to noting the way one notes a thing one does not intend to need. Tallow smoke crossed the lane from somewhere out of sight, the kind of smell that has not yet declared itself a smell — only an alteration of what was there before, which had been nothing in particular, the lane-ambient I had stopped registering after the first week. I held the feet. That is the way I had taught myself to put it. Not the flies, not the count, not the range — the feet, the only discipline I had brought from before that still did what it had been built for, which was to keep a thing in place by paying for the keeping every hour without remission. The insects swelled. I had pulled them back before the count completed, but the swell was the suppression slipping by a quarter measure, and I logged the slippage the way one logs anything that is going to come asking later. The angle-wall narrowed the lane. I exhaled.

Then the fish-cart blocked the lane — a handcart of the kind the morning fish-trade ran through the Hook from the river-side gate — and the ground transmitted the child's breath before the crowd had registered the absence of one. I have come to think that I knew it the way the ground knew it, which is to say without language, the soles of my feet reading the cobble before any face in the lane had turned. The crowd compressed against the cart's crosswise blockage, and I held the feet again, harder, because the lane-mouth pressure was already priced — the dozen I would need, and the second dozen I would not — and the insects propagated outward from where I stood without my having decided that they should, or without my having decided in the language the decision required, which is a distinction I will not be able to defend later and have not tried to. The propagation went where I had told it to go, and the telling was a thing I would not name. The nearest dozen bodies yielded. They yielded the way water yields, which is the way bodies yield when something below the floor of their attention has been asked of them and they have answered before knowing they were asked. The gap propagated outward through the press. I faced the child.

I raised the voice.

"Fever. Not the croup." I said it the way one says a thing one needs the crowd to stop hearing as the worse thing. "She needs air. Stand back." I lifted the hands then, which was the gesture the lane wanted in order to make sense of the gap that had opened in it. "Who knows her? Fetch them."

After the gap closed, the crowd came apart the way Flea Bottom crowds did. The fish-cart man faced me without speaking, the way a man faces a thing he has not finished thinking about. Two women faced the lane, the cart, each other; their faces did the accounting their mouths had not been asked to do yet. The Mudway man — a Hook ward-elder who had stood thirty years on these streets and named what he saw without explaining why he had noticed — took the lane-mouth without hurry, the way a man takes a position he has decided is his, and the taking was the answer to a question nobody had put to him in words. The elder had taken the lane-mouth a week before he would have the word for it. The child cleared the lane, carried by a woman who had answered the fetch. The gap closed.

I faced the alley-mouth. I set the body to the alley-mouth so the stitch-house lane would not need to be registered again — that was the lie I told the part of me that would have to keep coming back here, and I told it without believing it, which is the form most of my lying takes now. The tallow smoke layered the lane-floor in a way it had not done before the press, settling where the press had been, marking the place out. Oswyn Mudway lifted the chin. The chin lifted the way it lifts in the country's older stories when a word has been waiting for a person and the person has finally arrived to fit it; the chin-lift filed me in a category I recognized the shape of without needing the country's name for it.

The stitch-maker's ward — a Hook girl of eleven who watched cloth before she cut it, and watched people the same way — faced me from the doorway of the lamp-lit house two doors up. She faced me the way she faced cloth before deciding the cut. I had been registering her since the first morning and had not put it in the count, and she had been registering me, I now understood, for at least as long.
