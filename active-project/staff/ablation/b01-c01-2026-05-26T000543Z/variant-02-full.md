*I had won my last world by becoming a hive of bugs, and I had not meant to wake. I woke anyway in another body — a foreign woman with no name in this city, in King's Landing, in the year before the year before the year the dragons would dance. The flies came with me. Two hundred metres of them, dense as the air. The first thing I decided was that nothing I had done at the end would be done here.*

Three weeks in this ward and I have learned the specific weight of doing nothing. I sleep in the angle of a Hook alley where the drain runs and the air is full of small bodies I refuse to use. I have a count. I hold the count at its edge by the cost of holding it, every hour I am awake. The arrangement is that nobody here knows my name and I know nobody's name, and the arrangement is what I am paying for.

I came here to be useful without taking control. That is the rule I set before any of the rest of it. The rule is what I am holding the flies in for. The rule is older than anything in this city. The rule is what I have left.

In the angle-gap where the drain ran, water threaded the narrow seam between wall and cobblestone — the Hook, the lane-warren of Flea Bottom, King's Landing's slum-district where transient smallfolk went to be unremarkable — and below the gap the trickle was the only sound before the ward woke (what Flea Bottom called a ward — a trade household that took in a parentless child for shelter and light work, not warm and not cruel). The holding cost a quarter of her at the count's edge, and she paid it on principle every morning. At the stitch-house lane, the tallow smoke crossed from the lamp still burning at the lintel, carrying the smell of rendered fat over the pre-dawn air where no smell had been. The feet held the only discipline she brought from before that still did what it was built for. The insects swelled at the count's edge, and the swell was the suppression slipping by a quarter measure; she had pulled it back before the count completed. The angle-wall narrowed the lane where it bent, pressing the morning geometry tight between the stitch-house side and the drain-cut wall. She exhaled, and the count settled, and the morning resumed.

A handcart of the kind the morning fish-trade ran through the Hook from the river-side gate blocked the lane crosswise, stopped at an angle that left no useful gap on either side. The ground had carried the child's breath before the crowd had registered the absence of one — a small body down between two sets of adult legs, still enough that the nearest strangers were still deciding whether to step around it. The crowd compressed, flesh pressing against flesh, the cobblestone underfoot shifting from the ordinary unevenness of the lane to something denser as bodies stacked at the blockage. She held the count at its edge and did not move her feet. The lane-mouth pressure was already priced — the dozen she would need, and the second dozen she would not. The insects propagated out from the count's suppressed edge, threading through the crowd in the directions she had not named; the propagation went where she had told it to go, and the telling was a thing she would not name. The nearest dozen bodies yielded — a step left, a half-turn, a shoulder dropped without knowing why — and the gap propagated through the compression, opening a corridor where there had been none.

She faced the child. The girl was down on the cobblestones, flushed and shaking, and the crowd had not yet agreed on what was happening.

She raised her voice.

"Fever. Not the croup." Clear, flat, giving the information before the panic could organize itself around the wrong one. "She needs air. Stand back." The nearest bodies moved; the information carried further than the voice did. "Who knows her? Fetch them."

She lifted her hands, palms out, the gesture that meant she was not claiming ownership of the problem — only solving the immediate geometry of it.

After the gap closed, the crowd came apart the way Flea Bottom crowds did. The fish-cart man stood his ground and looked at her with the particular expression of someone deciding whether to be grateful or offended; she let him look. Two women had turned toward the lane-mouth together, their backs to the child now that the child had air. Then the Mudway man took the lane-mouth — the elder, a Hook ward-elder who had stood thirty years on these streets and named what he saw without explaining why he had noticed, his hands settling flat against his apron-front with the quiet of someone who has already completed an accounting and is only waiting for the numbers to finish arriving. The child cleared the lane; someone had known her, or someone who knew her had been close enough. The gap closed. She faced the alley-mouth at the lane's far end, setting her body in the direction that did not require the stitch-house lane to be registered again.

The tallow smoke had settled into the lane-floor by now, pooling in the angle-gap where the drain ran, threading around the cobblestones in a low layer that smelled of the stitch-house's morning work beginning again behind her. The chin lifts the way it lifts in the country's older stories when a word has been waiting for a person and the person has finally arrived to fit it. The elder lifted his chin, the elder had taken the lane-mouth a week before he would have the word for it, and she recognized the shape of the category he was building without needing the country's name for it.

Then the stitch-maker's ward faced her — a Hook girl of eleven who watched cloth before she cut it, and watched people the same way — her eyes leading her head toward Taylor by a half-beat, the face orienting before the rest of her had decided to orient. She filed the registration in the same fold where she kept the things that did not enter the ledger.
