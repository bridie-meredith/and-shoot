*I had won my last world by becoming a hive of bugs, and I had not meant to wake. I woke anyway in another body — a foreign woman with no name in this city, in King's Landing, in the year before the year before the year the dragons would dance. The flies came with me. Two hundred metres of them, dense as the air. The first thing I decided was that nothing I had done at the end would be done here.*

*Three weeks in this ward and I have learned the specific weight of doing nothing. I sleep in the angle of a Hook alley where the drain runs and the air is full of small bodies I refuse to use. I have a count. I hold the count at its edge by the cost of holding it, every hour I am awake. The arrangement is that nobody here knows my name and I know nobody's name, and the arrangement is what I am paying for.*

*I came here to be useful without taking control. That is the rule I set before any of the rest of it. The rule is what I am holding the flies in for. The rule is older than anything in this city. The rule is what I have left.*

The drain-water threaded the angle-gap where the lane pinched against the wall, and it had been threading there since before the city had a name to give the lane, and would still be threading there, I supposed, after the lane forgot which name was its own. There is a sound a city makes in the hour the sun has not quite arrived at, a sound that is not silence but the absence of any one thing loud enough to be remarked. In that hour the trickle was the loudest thing I could place, because I had taught myself to place it, and because nothing else in the geometry of the Hook — the lane-warren of Flea Bottom, King's Landing's slum-district where transient smallfolk go to be unremarkable (what Flea Bottom called a ward — a trade household that took in a parentless child for shelter and light work, not warm and not cruel — was a thing I would learn the meaning of later, and at a price) — wanted to be placed.

The smoke came across from the stitch-house lane, tallow and not wood, a smell I had not known to expect three weeks ago and now did not need to look up to identify. A lamp burned in the stitch-house window. A lamp had burned in that window every morning I had been here. Someone, somewhere two lanes over, was beginning the day by drawing thread through cloth, and the smoke of the small flame they worked by was crossing toward me, mixing into what I had been calling the lane-ambient until the moment it stopped being ambient and started being tallow.

I held the feet. I had held them for three weeks now, and the holding was the only discipline I had brought from before that still did what it had been built for. The feet were a count and the count was at its edge, and the edge was where I had decided to keep it. I had decided this on principle, every morning, because the alternative was to decide it on principle the next morning instead, and the next, and the chain of those mornings was the rule, and the rule was what I had left.

The insects swelled. It was a small swell, not the kind anyone but I would feel, a quarter-measure where the count had relaxed before I caught it relaxing, and I caught it and pulled it back, and the count completed itself the way I had told it to complete. The swell was the suppression slipping by a quarter, and the quarter was the argument I made to myself, made so often now it preceded the needing.

The angle-wall pressed the lane narrower as the lane bent. I exhaled. The exhale was not relief and not weariness; it was the small punctuation a person who is paying attention puts at the end of a thing she has done correctly, so the next thing she does will know where to begin.

———

Then there was the cart.

It was a handcart of the kind the morning fish-trade ran through the Hook from the river-side gate, and it had stopped crosswise in the lane where the lane had no width to spare for crosswise, and the crowd that had been moving through the lane was no longer moving. I did not arrive at the cart. The insects already had it. The ground had carried the child's breath before the crowd had registered the absence of one — a small breath, shallow and frequent, traveling up through the cobblestone the way breath travels when the body that holds it has gone down low to the stone — and what I understood, before I had decided to understand it, was that a child was on the ground beneath the press of bodies and the child was the one who could not get out.

The crowd compressed. The lane that had been narrow was narrower by the bodies in it, and the bodies in it were narrower by their wanting to be elsewhere, and the cobblestone that had been a cobblestone underfoot was now a cobblestone with people on it, which is a different thing. I was still holding the feet. I had not let the count go. The lane-mouth pressed the crowd inward from the far end, because a lane has two ends and at this hour both ends had a current, and the currents were meeting where the cart had stopped. The pressure was already priced. I had counted, without counting, the dozen I would need, and the second dozen I would not.

The insects propagated. I will not describe what that looked like to me, because what it looked like to me was the inside of a habit I had spent three weeks pretending not to have, and the propagation went where I had told it to go, and the telling was a thing I would not name. The nearest dozen bodies yielded. They did not know why they had yielded, and I did not need them to know. A gap opened in the lane where there had been no gap, and the gap propagated the way the insects had, outward in a small considered ring, and the ring made room.

I faced the child.

I raised the voice. I had not spoken in this city for more than the small purchase-words of three weeks, and the voice when it came was steadier than I had any right to expect.

"Fever. Not the croup."

The words went out into the lane the way the gap had gone out, taking their own room.

"She needs air. Stand back."

The bodies that had yielded yielded again, and the gap widened by the breadth of two arms held up, and I held my hands up, and the hands were the thing the crowd could see, and the seeing was what the hands were for.

"Who knows her? Fetch them."

———

After the gap closed, the crowd came apart the way Flea Bottom crowds did. There was no decision in the coming-apart, only the small habitual easing of people who have stood near a thing they did not want to stand near and now do not have to. The fish-cart man faced me. He looked at me the way a man looks at a thing he has not yet decided is his business, and then he looked past me, because he had decided it was not, and he bent to the cart's tongue.

Two women stood at the lane's far side and faced the lane, not me, which was the way I would have wanted to be looked at if anyone had asked me. They were composing what they had seen into something they could carry home in their mouths, and the composition was already underway, and the composition was not, yet, accusatory. It was accounting. It would be accounting for some time before it became anything else.

The Mudway man — a Hook ward-elder who had stood thirty years on these streets and named what he saw without explaining why he had noticed — took the lane-mouth. He took it the way he might have taken a place at a table that had been waiting for him to sit down. He had taken the lane-mouth a week before he would have the word for it, but the word would come, because the word was the kind of word the city's older stories had been keeping in a drawer for the person who would fit it.

The child cleared the lane on the arms of someone who knew her, and the gap that had been the gap closed, and the cobblestone was a cobblestone again, with the morning's ordinary business beginning to use it. I faced the alley-mouth. I set the body to the alley-mouth because the stitch-house lane was a place I did not want to need to register again, and a body has only so many directions it can be facing at one time, and the direction I chose was the one that turned the stitch-house lane back into geometry instead of address.

The tallow smoke had not stopped. It layered along the lane-floor now, low and slow, the way smoke does when the air above has been disturbed and the air below has not yet noticed. It was the same smoke. The smoke did not know what had happened. There is a kindness in the not-knowing of small physical things, which I have always tried not to mistake for the larger kind.

The Mudway man lifted his chin. He lifted it the way it lifts in the country's older stories when a word has been waiting for a person and the person has finally arrived to fit it. He did not speak. He did not need to. The chin-lift filed me in a category I recognized the shape of without needing the country's name for it.

And then there was the girl.

The stitch-maker's ward — a Hook girl of eleven who watched cloth before she cut it, and watched people the same way — had come to the mouth of the stitch-house lane and was facing me. She was small. She had been watching the morning the way she watched cloth, which is to say with a quiet that was not absence, and she had seen what she had seen, and she was looking at me now without yet having a word for what she had seen, and the looking was the first thing in three weeks that had reached me without being asked first whether it might.

I did not look away.
