The morning the bread-seller's wife stopped coming I noticed it from the way her husband stood. He stood at the back of his cart with a loaf in his hand and did not move it. The loaf had been in his hand long enough to go cold and he had not felt the going.

I had a customer in front of me wanting two onions and the rind of a hard cheese. I gave her the onions. I gave her the rind. I did not weigh either. She did not ask. She had been watching too.

I have known Errich twelve years. His wife may have died in the night, or she will die today, or she lives and the news of her has not yet reached his hand. I could not tell which from where I stood. It was not my business which.

I picked up the knife and started in on the apples. The hands know the apples after twelve years. Sometimes I think a thing through and sometimes the hands have decided ahead of me. They had set three apples aside, the bruised ones, in a small heap by the corner of the board, before I had told myself I was going over there.

I did not look at him. Looking would have been a kind of asking, and he would have heard the asking. There is a wrong way to come to a man whose wife may have died in the night. The wrong way begins with looking.

The first bell came. I set the knife down. The apples were not finished. I went around the cart.
