# World Notes — Decided Constraints
# Internal. Written in step 1a. One constraint per line. Law/Lore/Behavior prefix where applicable.

## Premise (decided from brief)

Lore: setting is Westeros — ASOIAF continent, feudal, dragon-bearing.
Lore: era is ~99 AC, thirty years prior to the Dance of the Dragons (129–131 AC) — late reign of Jaehaerys I Targaryen, the Old King, the Conciliator.
Lore: the dynasty is at peak — Jaehaerys's long peace is in its final decade; the Great Council of 101 AC (succession after Prince Baelon's death) is on the horizon as a structural inevitability, not yet triggered.
Lore: Targaryen dragons are alive and numerous in this period (peak count); riderless dragons exist on Dragonstone; the realm has not seen Targaryen civil violence in living memory.
Lore: Westeros is structurally feudal — Seven Kingdoms under one Iron Throne; House → bannermen → smallfolk hierarchy; Faith of the Seven dominant south of the Neck; old gods north; maester network as cross-realm institution.
Lore: protagonist is Taylor Hebert (Worm canon character) reincarnated into this world. She arrives carrying her own memory, ethical disposition, and operational habit. Her body is Westerosi.
Behavior: the genre register is "social warrior escalation" — organizing-as-engine, coalition-building as primary tactic, recursive escalation of stakes as narrative spine. Not chosen-one fantasy. Not court-romance. Drama lives in collision between Taylor's organizing logic and Westerosi institutional physics.

## Implication-class constraints

Lore: smallfolk are politically and legally subordinated; Westerosi feudal physics treats them as ambient terrain, not constituency. Taylor's instinct to treat them as agents is the central friction.
Lore: travel is slow, information is bottlenecked; maester-ravens are the fast-network and run through institutional hands.
Lore: religion is a coalition substrate — Faith of the Seven is the only cross-lord institution that reaches smallfolk at scale.
Behavior: "social warrior" is not a costume — Taylor pays bodily and psychic costs for organizing in a world that punishes organizers.

## Anchored-by-canon constraints

Lore: Robert's Rebellion is ~190 years in the future from window-open. It is irrelevant to this story's horizon. The Dance is the looming structural catastrophe, 30 years out.
Lore: Aerys II, Tywin, Rhaegar, Robert, Ned, Lyanna are unborn or many generations away. Do not reach for them.
Lore: contemporary historical actors include — Jaehaerys I (king, aging, ~70), Alysanne (queen, aging), Septon Barth (recently deceased or last days; his administrative/scholarly legacy is fresh), Prince Baelon Targaryen (heir presumptive, will die ~101 AC), Prince Viserys (Baelon's son, future Viserys I), Princess Rhaenys (Aemon's daughter, the "Queen Who Never Was" claim line), Prince Aemon (Jaehaerys's eldest son, killed 92 AC — already gone by window-open), Lord Commander of the Kingsguard Ryam Redwyne or successor, the Hand (post-Barth — possibly Rego Draz or successor era).

## Resolved from 1b (attempt 2 — both audience and dramatist accepted)

Lore: Taylor retains her full Worm-canon swarm-sense and active multi-body insect control. Foreign insect ecology produces persistent cognitive friction (unfamiliar species, unfamiliar distributions, sensory textures she has to re-calibrate over months). Power level is real and eventually potent; the friction is ecological/cognitive, not power-cap.
Lore: Taylor arrives in a Westerosi child body at approximately age 7–8.
Lore: Taylor's pinned arrival year is ~84 AC. She is approximately 17 at series close (Great Council, 101 AC).
Lore: Taylor's psychological baseline is mid-canon Worm Taylor (post-Leviathan / post-Echidna era). Her internal failure modes — self-isolation, distributed control as substitute for trust, Dinah's debt as live moral wound, willingness to lie to allies when she calculates it necessary — are present and active at arrival.
Lore: Taylor is reborn smallfolk: daughter of craftsman or minor tradespeople in a Riverlands river-market town (Gods Eye / Blue Fork tributary canvas). No house name, no legible authority, no institutional cover.
Lore: Taylor has full meta-foreknowledge — she read the ASOIAF books and watched the show in her prior life; she recognizes "Targaryen" within her first weeks of consciousness in the new body; broad-strokes accurate, specific dates and minor characters fuzzy. She knows the Dance is coming in roughly 30 years.
Lore: magic in Westeros at 99 AC is rare and institutional — dragons are Targaryen-only and geographically distant; wargs are northern rumor; glass candles are Citadel legend; no daily supernatural friction outside dragon-bearing households. Taylor's swarm-sense reads to others as uncanny / low-grade madness against an otherwise mundane backdrop.
Behavior: Westerosi witnesses read Taylor's swarm-sense through Westerosi superstition — old gods, fear-of-the-dark, Faith-of-the-Seven-as-ward — not as generic strangeness. Watch item at execution.
Lore: series end-state is the Great Council of 101 AC. Council is the stress-test where costs become final, not validation. The Dance follows regardless of Council outcome; catharsis before the Dance is structurally prohibited. What survives Council survives in damaged, diminished, or transformed form.
Lore: cost-shape of escalation is asymmetric and migrating — Taylor pays personally early; coalition pays mid-arc; institution pays late. Cost migrates outward as Taylor's organizing becomes more effective.
Lore: ignition point is ~86 AC, Taylor age ~9. The swarm rises (involuntarily/semi-voluntarily) against a tax-collector retinue coercing a smallfolk woman in the market town. The lord's steward records a note: this is the first institutional record of Taylor as anomaly. Suppression-as-policy (not incident response) must be reached no later than mid-S3.
Lore: Taylor's Shard is present in this world, dormant at Entity-communication level, active at behavioral-weight level. It cannot run conflict-escalation cycles (no Entities, no parahuman ecology) but it weights her toward confrontation and direct action — options that escalate feel mildly right; options that de-escalate feel mildly wrong. Taylor reads the bias as her own judgment. The Shard does not add new failure modes; it makes existing ones harder to correct.
