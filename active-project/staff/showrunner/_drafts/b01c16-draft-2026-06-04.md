## Scene 1 (b01c16s01)

Taylor is mid-circuit on the west-precinct coverage pass when she reaches the corner of the
chandler's storehouse where Septon Halvard keeps what passes for his sept. The corner is as it
always is: the cleared space between the storehouse wall and the rendering-yard fence, two crates
stacked as kneeling-benches, the reed-mat that Halvard sets out when someone is coming to die.
There is no one dying today — the mat is rolled and standing against the wall. There are three
people in the clearing who are not dying: a woman with a child who has stopped growing the way
children sometimes stop; an old man Taylor has read through the precinct-feed before, gout-swollen
right foot, slower than he was in the fall. And Halvard. Halvard is not looking at her yet. He is
finishing something with the old man — a low exchange, pausal, the specific register Taylor has
placed by now: the slowed-down version of a conversation, the kind that leaves room for someone to
find the word they are reaching for. Taylor does not change her route. She enters the clearing on
the diagonal she always enters it on — the coverage pass runs through this corner, which is one of
the precinct's thickest-density clusters for illness-indicators, and she has been stopping here on
rotation for eight months. She is not here for Halvard. She is here because the insects in the
storehouse eaves give her the bodies in the clearing and two lanes adjacent without her needing to
be elsewhere. The old man straightens. Halvard's head comes up. Taylor has already pulled the feed
from the eaves down to passive and is checking the rendering-yard approach without looking at it;
the information arrives in the same register as the ward noise. She does not stop moving. She does
not stop.

## Scene 2 (b01c16s02)

Halvard is beside her before she reaches the rendering-yard fence. Not blocking — he is not a
blocking kind of man — but matching her pace at the margin of the cleared space, the way he has
done before. He does not open with a question. He opens with a statement in the slower register:
"The ones who lived through the autumn. The woman with the chest-complaint in Muggers' Row. The boy
from the fishmonger's lane." He names them in the specific way he has — not claiming them, not
demanding gratitude for them, setting them down the way you set down evidence that is not in
dispute. "I have not counted. It is not my count to run." A pause. "The count I run is what it costs
them, after. What they understand about how they survived. Some of them understand things about this
precinct that I did not think anyone who arrived the way you arrived would know." Taylor has heard
the shape of this before. She has the answer — she has had it since the third time he found her at
this corner, which was six months ago, which was before she named the contempt. The answer is: yes,
the people she keeps alive are worth keeping alive; yes, the mechanism is imperfect; yes, the
accounting runs where it runs. The answer addresses what Halvard is saying. The answer is correct
and the answer is also beside the point, because what Halvard's counter-argument addresses is the
contempt — it addresses the question of whether the contempt is justified — and the contempt and the
continuation are the same fact. He is not wrong about the people he named. She knows that. The
argument is not wrong. The argument addresses the wrong thing. Taylor runs the position-arithmetic
while Halvard is still speaking, the way the accounting does when there is a pause long enough for
it: what the position is currently worth to her, what it costs to hold it, whether the number on the
left has moved relative to the number on the right since the last time she ran it. The number on the
left has not changed. The number on the right has. The position is worth less to her now than it
costs to hold. Not by a margin that triggers a different accounting. By enough to notice. She
continues holding it. The position will continue to be held until the arithmetic goes somewhere it
cannot come back from, and it has not done that yet. Halvard finishes the passage he was in. He
stops walking and faces her — not blocking, but stopping, which is different. "I am not asking you
to do differently," he says. "I am asking you to know what you're doing."

## Scene 3 (b01c16s03)

Taylor does not answer. Not a pause before answering — a stop, the kind the body makes when it has
finished weighing something and the weight has resolved. Halvard is still standing with his face
toward her. He is close enough that the compound eyes in the storehouse eaves give her the specific
quality of his attention: the particular stillness of a man who has said the thing and is now
waiting to see what it does. She can see what she would say if she were going to say it. The answer
is: yes, I know what I am doing, I have always known, the accounting has been running longer than
you have been watching it, and the knowing is not the problem. She does not say it. She does not say
it not because she has no answer but because saying it requires treating the argument as live, and
she has confirmed that the argument is not live — what Halvard's question asks for is a response to
the contempt, which is correct, and what she is doing does not rest on the contempt. The contempt
and the continuation are the same fact because the continuation predates the naming: she was running
the architecture before she had the word for what she thought about the people it served, and the
word's arrival changed nothing about the route. Halvard is still there. He has not moved. He is in
the middle of having said a thing and waiting. Taylor turns. Not away from the argument — away from
the man, which is different, and which is the thing the body does when the mind has already
finished. She walks back across the clearing toward the rendering-yard approach, not hurrying, the
same pace she came in with. The reed-mat is still rolled against the wall. The crates are still
stacked. Halvard's voice does not reach after her — he does not call out, which she had known
without knowing she had known it: this is the kind of man who does not call after people. The
compound eyes in the storehouse eaves carry him standing in the cleared space until Taylor is at the
rendering-yard fence and the angle-line has moved him to the eaves' edge and then out of it. She
brings the feed back to coverage density and runs the next segment of the circuit. What has not
happened: she did not suppress the recognition. She looked at it — the argument, the counter she had
ready, the question Halvard was actually asking — and she walked away from the person holding it up.
That is not the same thing. She knows the difference. She has the accounting for both. The feed moves
on. The circuit continues. The sept-corner is behind her.
