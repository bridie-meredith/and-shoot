# 1b Options — Open Question Resolution
# ~99 AC, late Jaehaerys I. Era-correct. World-notes compliant.

---

## OQ-1: Taylor's powers in Westeros

Option A — Taylor retains full swarm-sense and control, but the insect ecology of Westeros is foreign enough that her mental map is persistently wrong: species she has no names for, distributions that defy her expectations, sensory textures that feel like static until she re-calibrates over months. Control is real and eventually potent; the friction is purely ecological and cognitive, not power-level.

Option B — Taylor's power is attenuated: she has reliable swarm-sense (ambient insect-awareness, passive) but active control is inconsistent, fading in and out depending on density and proximity, and exhausting when she forces it. She cannot run a large swarm the way Skitter could. She can direct small clusters and perceive through them, no more.

Option C — Taylor's power is absent. She arrives with memory and disposition only. The swarm-sense is gone; what remains is the habit of thinking in distributed systems, the parasite-awareness, the tactical read — all now expressed purely through social and political action.

Advocate: Option A. The brief's "social warrior escalation" is served by a protagonist whose power is real but costly to deploy — the foreign-ecology friction means she cannot lean on it as a crutch without effort, which keeps organizing as the primary engine, while the latent threat of an actual swarm-controller provides escalation fuel when institutions stop responding to social pressure. Option C reduces her to a competent mundane, which softens the genre register. Option B risks making the power feel vestigial rather than meaningful.

## OQ-2: Reincarnation arrival point — life stage

Option A — Taylor arrives in an infant body, with full adult cognition present from the first weeks of life. The opening arc (~2–3 seasons of internal material) is the decade of enforced helplessness: watching, learning the language from scratch, mapping Westerosi social physics before she can act on any of it. Her first operational move does not come until she is ~8–10.

Option B — Taylor arrives in a child body of approximately 7–8 years old. She already speaks the language badly (as a child would), has some motor competence, and is socially legible enough to be in rooms where things happen. She has ~3–4 years of low-visibility operational window before puberty changes her threat profile. Organizing begins in this window.

Option C — Taylor arrives in a young adult body of approximately 16–18 years old. She is immediately operational, linguistically fluent, and physically capable. The reincarnation horror (somatic discontinuity, proprioceptive wrongness) is acute and front-loaded into the early episodes, then transitions rapidly into political action.

Advocate: Option B. The brief calls for escalation, which requires a runway. Option B gives her enough time under the radar to build infrastructure before she becomes visible as a threat, which is where the escalation engine ignites. Option A's decade of helplessness is structurally rich but risks a slow-burn opening that the "social warrior" register must wait too long to deliver. Option C compresses the reincarnation horror too much and loses the "usefulness of being underestimated as a child" building block.

## OQ-3: Taylor's psychological baseline at arrival

Option A — Taylor arrives as post-Gold-Morning Khepri-form Taylor: apocalypse-survivor, instrumentalized everyone, ethically hollowed to the point of having surrendered her own identity to win. She is not recovering — she is starting over in a world where the catastrophe she prevented means nothing. Her organizing in Westeros is a second attempt at meaning after the first burned everything clean.

Option B — Taylor arrives as post-Khepri-recovery Taylor: quieter, scarred, aware of what she became, trying to be done with fighting. The reincarnation reads as a cruel joke. She resists organizing for the first portion of the story, then is dragged back in by circumstances she cannot watch unfold without acting. The arc is reluctant re-engagement.

Option C — Taylor arrives as mid-canon Taylor (post-Leviathan / post-Echidna era): still operational, not yet Khepri, the full warlord-organizer in harness. She has grief and damage but is not broken. She arrives in Westeros with her capacity intact and her instinct to fix things running hot.

Advocate: Option C. Mid-canon Taylor is not undamaged — she is operational despite specific, live internal failure modes, and those failure modes are the fuel. Self-isolation is her default: she solves problems alone, recruits instrumentally, and pulls back from intimacy the moment it creates a vulnerability she cannot control. Distributed swarm-control as a substitute for trust is structural: she manages people the way she manages insects — efficiently, at a distance, with contingency plans she does not share. Dinah's debt is a live moral wound; Taylor knows she used a child and has not resolved whether the math was acceptable. She lies to allies when she calculates it is necessary. These are not obstacles to her organizing — they are the mechanisms by which OQ-9/C's "coalition pays" is delivered. She cannot sustain the relationships her project requires, because she cannot stop running the control calculus on the people who trust her. The world resisting her is the external friction; she resisting genuine interdependence is the internal drag. Both are present in mid-canon Taylor. Option A's Khepri-hollowness forecloses this dynamic entirely — a protagonist who has already burned through the failure modes has nothing left to spend. Option B's reluctant-re-engagement delays the engine. Option C gives the story a Taylor who arrives capable and willing, whose internal failure modes play out in real time against the demands of coalition-building — which is exactly the shape OQ-9/C requires.

## OQ-4: Birth station

Option A — Taylor is reborn smallfolk: daughter of a craftsman or minor tradespeople in a market town or river-port. Her constituency is immediately around her. She has no legible authority, no house name, no protection. Every institutional interaction is adversarial from below.

Option B — Taylor is reborn into a household of a hedge knight or sworn retainer: low minor-arms legibility, occasional castle-access, not quite smallfolk but functionally indistinguishable to lords. She has just enough proximity to the institutional layer to watch it operate while still organizing from outside it.

Option C — Taylor is reborn into a minor noble house: a third or fourth daughter of a lord with perhaps fifty sworn swords and two villages. She has a name, restricted movement (marriage expectations), and access to the Faith and maester networks by default. Her organizing can work through institutional channels as well as around them.

Advocate: Option A. The brief's "social warrior" is an organizer who fights upward from the constituency she serves. Born smallfolk, Taylor has immediate skin-in-the-game and zero institutional cover — every gain is contested and every loss is personal. Option B softens this by giving her marginal cover. Option C risks turning the story into a noble-reformer narrative, which is a different genre. The friction that makes escalation meaningful is maximum at Option A.

## OQ-5: Geographic anchor

Option A — The story begins in the Riverlands, specifically a river-market town on the Gods Eye or the Blue Fork. The Riverlands are a crossroads: multiple great houses press on them, the Faith is dense, smallfolk population is high, and the region's history of being contested terrain is baked into the local political texture. Information and travelers flow through, which gives Taylor early exposure to wider Westerosi politics without requiring her to travel.

Option B — The story begins in the Reach, in an agricultural town within the sphere of Oldtown or a lesser Reach house. Faith of the Seven is dominant and operationally dense — septons are everywhere, the institution is local. The Reach's wealth and stability make the smallfolk's subjugation feel more comfortable and thus more insidious. The organizing fight is against contentment as much as cruelty.

Option C — The story begins in King's Landing itself, in the lower city, Flea Bottom or the dockside wards. Maximum institutional density: the Red Keep is visible from anywhere, the Faith Militant's memory is recent (Maekar's era suppression is ~60 years prior), and the smallfolk population is vast, anonymous, and already networked in informal ways. The crown is a proximate antagonist rather than a distant one.

Advocate: Option A. The Riverlands gives Taylor a contested-terrain canvas where multiple institutional vectors (Tully bannermen, itinerant Faith, river trade networks, occasional Targaryen crown interest) intersect without any single one dominating. This creates the multi-front organizing environment that the escalation register requires. Option C compresses too much — King's Landing makes every escalation immediately visible to the crown, which shortens the runway before suppression. Option B's stability risks making the antagonist too comfortable and the escalation too slow.

## OQ-6: Foreknowledge — does Taylor know "ASOIAF" as a fiction?

Option A — Taylor has full meta-foreknowledge: she read the books and watched the show in her previous life and recognized "Targaryen" within her first weeks of consciousness in the new body. She knows the Dance is coming in ~30 years. She knows the Great Council. She has plot-level information that is roughly accurate at the broad-stroke level and fuzzy at the detail level (she does not remember every chapter, and she was never certain about the exact year of things).

Option B — Taylor has partial foreknowledge: names ring bells, specific events are foggy, but she has general impressions. She knows enough to be alert to certain names (Targaryen, the Dance) without being able to predict specific outcomes. She treats her foreknowledge as unreliable and plans around it rather than from it.

Option C — Taylor has no foreknowledge: she arrives in a world with no referents and must learn it cold. The knowledge-as-burden building block does not apply; what applies instead is pure disposition — she organizes because she cannot stop herself, not because she knows what is coming.

Advocate: Option A. The brief's escalation register is enriched by a protagonist who knows the catastrophe is coming and is organizing against it anyway, knowing she probably cannot stop it. Full foreknowledge makes her choices legible to the worm-canon-pedant audience while creating dramatic irony for ASOIAF-familiar readers. Option C loses this layer entirely. Option B hedges in a way that neither delivers the dramatic irony nor removes it cleanly.

## OQ-7: Magic and the wider weird

Option A — Magic is rare and institutional: dragons are real but Targaryen-only and geographically distant (Dragonstone, King's Landing, the Dragonpit). Wargs exist in the North but are rumor. Glass candles are Citadel legend. Taylor's swarm-sense reads to others as either uncanny intuition or low-grade madness. No daily supernatural friction.

Option B — Magic is present but uneven: Taylor brushes against functional magic in its ~99 AC form — a warg-touched shepherd encountered in the Riverlands, a septon who actually receives something when he prays, a maester who knows a glass candle works even if he won't say so. The world is porous to the weird. Taylor's power fits into this ambient strangeness rather than standing alone.

Option C — Magic is operationally visible: Taylor encounters actual magical systems early — a skinchanger, a ghost grass rumor, a Targaryen with a bond to a young dragon. The story registers as fantasy-adventure alongside its political-thriller spine.

Advocate: Option A. The "social warrior escalation" register is clearest when Taylor's power is anomalous against a backdrop of functional mundanity. If magic is Option B or C, her swarm-sense risks being read as just another magical thing in a magical world, which dissolves the friction of her specifically being an outsider system operating in a world with different rules. Option A keeps her power strange and isolating.

## OQ-8: Series end-state and dramatic horizon

Option A — The series closes before the Dance, resolving Taylor's social-warrior project in the final years of Jaehaerys I: her coalition has changed something real (a policy, an institution, a local structure of power), or has been destroyed, or both. The Dance is on the horizon but never arrived. Scope: tight, 2–3 seasons.

Option B — The series closes at or through the Great Council of 101 AC. Taylor's organizing work collides with the realm's succession crisis — the Great Council is the moment when who controls the institutional channels matters most. Her project either feeds into the Council dynamics or is consumed by them. Scope: medium, 3–4 seasons.

Option C — The series closes at or through the Dance of the Dragons (129–131 AC). Taylor lives to see the catastrophe she knew was coming; her organizing either survives the civil war in some form or is destroyed by it. The final act is what happens to a social movement during a dynastic war. Scope: long, 5+ seasons, requires Taylor to be an old woman at close.

Advocate: Option B. The Great Council is the structural hinge that the era provides: it is the moment when the question "who decides" becomes existential for the realm, and it is exactly the arena where Taylor's work — who has standing, who has voice, what institutions can be pressed — becomes most legible as having mattered or not. Option A resolves too privately; the world does not test her work at scale. Option C requires a 30-year narrative that risks losing the escalation's momentum.

## OQ-9: Cost-shape of escalation

Option A — Taylor pays in body and mind: organizer-as-martyr register. The costs are hers to carry — physical injury, isolation, grief, psychic damage from operating without a shared frame of reference. The coalition she builds survives longer than she does in full capacity. Audience emotional contract: tragedy of the willing sacrifice.

Option B — The coalition pays: the people she organizes are the ones burned for it. Taylor survives each escalation because she is marginally harder to destroy than the smallfolk she organizes, but the cost is always paid by the people around her. Audience emotional contract: ensemble drama, grief accumulation, moral weight of leadership.

Option C — Both pay, asymmetrically, in sequence: early escalations cost Taylor personally; mid-arc escalations cost the coalition; late escalations cost the institution (the Faith fractures, a lord's house is weakened, a Targaryen patronage relationship breaks). The cost migrates outward as Taylor's organizing becomes more effective, until the institution's counter-escalation costs more than hers does. Audience emotional contract: institutional thriller with tragedy braided in.

Advocate: Option C. The brief's "escalation" implies a ratchet — stakes rise and the nature of the cost changes as the conflict matures. Option A risks a martyrdom arc that forecloses the institutional-thriller register. Option B risks diffusing protagonist stakes into ensemble diffusion. Option C delivers the escalation as structurally meaningful: early personal cost establishes that the fight is real; coalition cost in the middle establishes that leadership carries weight; institutional cost at the end establishes that the escalation has actually changed the power balance. This is the shape that serves "social warrior escalation" most directly.

## Ignition Point

The child-window (~7–10, given OQ-2/B arrival at ~7–8) is when Taylor first does something visible-to-power. The question is not whether it happens — it must, or the institution never wakes up and opening seasons run flat. The question is what it is and who sees it.

Decided: the ignition is a swarm event, not a social act. Taylor's first legible-to-power moment is involuntary or semi-voluntary — the swarm responds to threat or distress before she has chosen to act, making the event undeniable and her control of it immediately suspect.

Three concrete options for the specific ignition:

Option I-A — A tax collector's retinue is moving through the market town collecting arrears. A smallfolk woman is being physically coerced; Taylor's swarm rises without a decision — the collector's horses bolt, two of his men are stung badly enough to need treatment, the collection is abandoned for the day. The local reeve reports upward that a girl was at the center of it. The lord's steward makes a note.

Option I-B — A river-ferry crossing goes wrong: a barge takes on water, several passengers (Taylor among them) are in the river. The swarm guides survivors to a sandbar via sound and movement — Taylor's direction is visible to the ferryman and to a Faith septon traveling with the party. The septon's letter to his sept-mother mentions "a child who commanded the river-creatures." The letter is passed to a traveling maester.

Option I-C — A local bully-network among children (sons of the reeve and his sworn men) corners Taylor in an alleyway. The swarm response is disproportionate and controlled — she does not use it to hurt, but the precision of it is witnessed by an adult, a cloth-merchant who was watching from a window above. He mentions it to the wool factor he trades with, who trades in information.

Decided: Option I-A. The tax-collection context makes the ignition politically readable from the first moment — it is not "child with strange power" but "child with strange power that activated against institutional extraction." The lord's steward who makes the note is the first institutional record. The ignition point is ~age 9, approximately two years after arrival. Planning teams treat ~86 AC as the ignition-year anchor.

## Shard Status

Decided: the Shard still pushes, but differently.

In a world without Entities and without parahuman conflict-ecology, the Shard has no optimization target. It cannot run the conflict-escalation loop it was designed for — there is no cape war to fuel, no Event to harvest. What remains is the base behavioral nudge: Taylor is pulled toward confrontation, toward escalation, toward solving problems through direct action when a slower path exists. The Shard does not whisper; it weights. Options that avoid conflict feel mildly but persistently wrong. Options that escalate feel mildly but persistently right. Taylor does not know this is happening. She reads it as her own judgment. The Shard is not driving the story; it is a thumb on the scale that makes her internal failure modes harder to correct and makes de-escalation cost more than it should.

This is a premise decision. Planning treats the Shard as present, dormant at the Entity-communication level, and active at the behavioral-weight level. No further resolution needed before series planning.

## Coherence Check

The advocated bundle: **A / B / C / A / A / A / A / B / C** (OQ-1 through OQ-9).

Coupling notes:

OQ-1 (Option A: full power, foreign-ecology friction) × OQ-3 (Option C: mid-canon Taylor, operational) × OQ-9 (Option C: asymmetric migrating cost): these three cohere strongly and tightly. Taylor arrives capable; the swarm-sense is real enough to become a threat the institution eventually must answer; the cost migrates outward as she becomes harder to suppress by ordinary means. OQ-3/C's internal failure modes — self-isolation, distributed control as substitute for trust, Dinah's debt, lying to allies — are the specific mechanism by which OQ-9/C delivers "coalition pays." She cannot sustain the relationships her project requires. The people around her pay for her control calculus. This is not a conflict between OQ-3 and OQ-9; it is the engine. No structural conflict.

OQ-4 (Option A: born smallfolk) × OQ-5 (Option A: Riverlands) × OQ-7 (Option A: magic rare): these cohere. A smallfolk girl in a Riverlands market town with an anomalous insect-sense reads as uncanny rather than magical in a low-magic environment — the institution's first reading is "strange child" not "sorceress," which gives her runway before the threat-read escalates. No conflict.

OQ-2 (Option B: arrives at ~7–8) × OQ-8 (Option B: closes at Great Council, ~101 AC): Taylor arrives at ~84 AC (pinned; record in world-notes as decided fact). She is ~7–8 at arrival, ~17 at the Great Council close. Runway is approximately 15 years of story-time. Ignition at ~86 AC (age ~9, per Ignition Point section). Escalation must reach institutional-cost level — the point where suppression is a policy decision rather than a local response — no later than mid-S3, or the collision at the Great Council lands underweight. The planning team treats ~84 AC arrival as a hard anchor. World-notes must record it before season planning begins.

OQ-6 (Option A: full foreknowledge) × OQ-8 (Option B: closes at Great Council): Taylor knows the Dance follows regardless of what the Council decides. Her organizing in the final seasons carries the irony that she is not preventing the catastrophe — she knows she cannot. The Great Council close is not a victory. It is a stress-test: the moment when the work she has built is put under institutional pressure and costs more than she expected. What survives survives in damaged, diminished, or transformed form. The Dance follows. Any catharsis before the Dance is unearned and structurally prohibited. The Council close must be staged as "costs become final" — the coalition's debts come due, Taylor's internal failure modes deliver their consequence, and what remains is legible but reduced. The "tragedy of correct diagnosis" framing holds: she is right about what is coming, and being right costs her and everyone around her.

Conflict flag — OQ-4 (Option A: smallfolk) × OQ-6 (Option A: full foreknowledge): a smallfolk child who recognizes "Targaryen" and knows the Dance is coming will have almost no institutional channel through which to act on that knowledge. This is a feature, not a bug — it is precisely the "knowledge without credential" friction the expansion named — but it means the first ~3 years of operational capacity are spent building the credibility to be heard at all. The planning team should treat this as a pacing constraint on early seasons: no institutional access until she earns it.

No combination in the advocated bundle is internally contradictory. The dominant risk is pacing: arrival pinned at ~84 AC with Great Council close at ~101 AC gives a ~17-year story-time runway across 3–4 seasons. Ignition at ~86 AC (age ~9) establishes institutional awareness early. Escalation must reach institutional-cost level — suppression as policy, not incident response — no later than mid-S3 or the Council collision lands underweight. Taylor's internal failure modes (OQ-3/C) must begin extracting coalition cost no later than S2 mid-arc, or the Council close has no earned weight behind it.
