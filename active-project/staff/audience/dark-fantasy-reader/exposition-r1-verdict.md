# Verdict reasoning

I sit back and wait to be convinced. Exposition either carries the weight of the world or it patches over gaps with words. The three entries here are sparse, which is what I want. I'm not looking for explanations. I'm looking to know whether the orienting text is honest about the cost of what it's describing.

**exp:1 @0 (prior-episode-bridge).** "I had set myself a rule when I came back into this body and this city — I would never again reach into a living mind, never again make a person move. I keep the count of every cost I cannot pay back in a ledger by lamplight. So far the rule has only been theory. This morning the city's hired men come down the Hook looking for bodies to conscript, and theory ends."

This is right. The bridge doesn't explain the prior life — it names the rule and the distance since the rule was set, and then it closes the distance in the last sentence. "Theory ends" is the bill arriving. "The city's hired men come down the Hook" — they don't know or care about Taylor's rule. The sweep is an external force that doesn't pause for the protagonist. That's the correct flavor: the world moves without her permission. "Came back into this body" carries the resurrection as a weight, not a revelation — that's the not-naming this kind of fiction earns the right to use when it handles the prior-displacement as cost rather than backstory.

The bridge also plants the ledger, which is the chapter's terminal image. That's correct structuring: the orienting text seeds the close before the opening gun fires. ACCEPT.

**exp:4 @4 (pressed-labor-sweep).** "When the city's hired watchmen need hands for a public work, they sweep a poor district and conscript whoever cannot prove they are wanted elsewhere. In Flea Bottom there is rarely anyone who can."

The mechanism has teeth: "conscript whoever cannot prove they are wanted elsewhere" is a specific class-physics. Wanting-elsewhere is the social credential; Flea Bottom doesn't issue it. That's Westerosi-specific social pressure, not generic menace. The gloss reads as institutional knowledge of a world that was there before the protagonist and will be there after. I don't flinch at the mechanism itself.

But the construction is inconsistent. The first sentence uses the generic frame — "a poor district" — and the second sentence names the specific district without bridging the gap. "In Flea Bottom there is rarely anyone who can" assumes "Flea Bottom" is known. For a cold-join reader (the bridge at @0 exists for them), the district has not been named. The bridge says "this city" and "the Hook" but not "Flea Bottom." A gloss that defines a concept in generic terms and then immediately applies the specific name without apposition is leaving the reader with a half-oriented frame — they understand the sweep but not where they are standing. In a world that is supposed to feel specific, a location name that passes through without being grounded reads as set-dressing shorthand. That's a hot button for me.

The fix is the auditor's own suggestion: "In Flea Bottom, the city's poorest ward, there is rarely anyone who can." This does not explain the world — it locates the reader inside it. REVISE.

**exp:5 @23 (ledger).** "The book I keep by lamplight — a running account of every cost my actions make that I cannot pay back."

Twenty words. First-person. Em-dash fold. "Cannot pay back" is the weight-bearing phrase: the ledger isn't bookkeeping, it's a record of permanent debt. That's the correct orientation for what follows at @23-@29 — the accounting scene has to carry as a real reckoning, not as a filing habit. The framing tells me why before the bone shows me what. The gloss does not over-explain; it states the function and stops. ACCEPT.

**Verdict: REVISE.** The exposition graph is otherwise clean. One entry needs a targeted repair: the embedded "Flea Bottom" in exp:4 must be given an inline appositive so the gloss doesn't contradict its own orienting function. The repair is minimal. The other two entries are correct in form and register.
