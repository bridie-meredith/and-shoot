# Cold-read — b01-c02 — 2026-05-26 (cherry-pick)

Reader: first-time, uninformed. One pass at reading pace.

## 1. EVENTS

Honestly, I had to guess at most of these. What I think happened, in order:

1. A narrator who controls (or "reads") insects — flies and beetles — has been mapping a slum-like place called "the Hook" by sensing where bodies cluster, who's sick, who's hungry, who crosses what thresholds.
2. There was an earlier event (referenced in italics at the top) where a crowd broke open around someone called the "stitch-maker's ward," hands went up, and afterwards the narrator stopped feeding people for three weeks and started "listening" instead. Some kind of moral line was drawn.
3. The narrator pushes the range of their insect-network past a four-hundred-body "ceiling" and pays some cost (a "dropout").
4. Over days, the insects detect a woman — a "junction-signature" — crossing the same thresholds repeatedly. The map has no category for her.
5. The narrator stands at a drain angle, the woman walks past (I think?), the narrator yields the alley-mouth to her, and something is filed in the "ledger" without being signed.
6. Later (afternoon), the narrator re-runs the map, finds her again as a "junction-lane void," and draws some kind of line — possibly the same kind of line that preceded whatever happened at the stitch-maker's ward.
7. The narrator presses against the drain angle, exhales, and the chapter ends with the "accounting" closed.

I could not tell you with confidence who the narrator is, who the woman is, what the stakes of "drawing the line" actually are, what "feeding" meant in the opening, what world this is, or what the rules of the insect-control are. I could not tell you what the "stitch-maker's ward" actually refers to — a place, a person, a guild?

## 2. JEOPARDY

Gestured at, not felt.

The text is *insistent* that there are stakes — there is a "line," there is a "rule," there is a "cost," there is something the narrator must not become, and the woman is somehow a re-occurrence of a prior wrong. But because I don't know what the prior wrong was, what crossing the line would do, or what the narrator wants, the insistence registered as a tone rather than a tension. I could feel the *posture* of jeopardy without locating it.

The line "the hand drew where it had drawn before" and "what a watching street would find a word for, and I did not give it the word" is the closest I came to feeling stakes — but it required me to assume the narrator had previously done something terrible, and the assumption never converted to specifics.

## 3. CAUSALITY

Mostly no. Sentences chained by ledger-vocabulary repetition, not by cause.

The grammar of the chapter is: a thing is read, a thing is "filed," a thing is "closed," a thing "costs" something. But the actual mechanism is opaque. The "insects returned the function-signature — the body that knotted the junction, the body the alleys gave onto, the body the map was about to name" is a sentence I read four times and still couldn't say what *happens* — does the narrator decide something? Do the insects do something? Is the woman tagged? Is she harmed?

The pivot point — the moment where the narrator does or doesn't cross the line — happens in language so abstracted ("the gesture moved in a hand that knew the motion, from another accounting under another architecture, and the hand kept going") that I genuinely could not tell whether the narrator did the bad thing or refused to. I think they did it. But I'm guessing.

## 4. PAYOFF

No. The ending does not land for a first-time reader.

I understand that something has been decided or done — there is weight, the narrator presses against the wall, exhales. But what was decided, what was risked, what changed — I cannot say. "The last one cost more to close than the others. The cost did not appear in the closing." is meant to be the climactic beat. For me it was a sentence about a sentence about a sentence.

If I had read the prior chapter I might know what "closing an entry" means in practice. As a cold reader, it's a transaction with no values filled in.

## 5. CONTINUE?

No.

Not because the prose is bad — it's deliberate and the cadence is real — but because after seventy lines I have no character to care about, no concrete event to remember, and no question I am being invited to answer. I have a *mood* (something is wrong, the narrator is doing something they shouldn't, a woman has been catalogued). The mood is well-made. But I would put the book down here.

## 6. ONE-LINE SUMMARY

A bug-controlling narrator surveils a slum, notices a woman who doesn't fit their map, and either does or does not do the thing they previously vowed not to do — all rendered in a vocabulary of ledgers and counts that holds the reader at arm's length from what is actually happening.

## Bluntness note

The chapter is doing something formally interesting — the whole thing reads like an accountant's interior monologue while watching a stakeout, and the moral weight is meant to live in the gap between the bookkeeping language and the violence (or near-violence) underneath. I can see the move. I respect the move.

But it asks the reader to supply the stakes from somewhere — either from prior chapters, or from inference about what "the line" actually means. As a cold reader I don't have that supply, and the chapter does not give me a hook to hold onto. The italicized opening is the only place where I get a near-explicit gloss ("Reading was not directing. I had not crossed the line."), and even there the antecedents — what reading is, what directing is, what the line separates — are abstract.

What worked: the cadence. The repetition of "the threshold," "the ledger," "the count," "the angle" has a real incantatory pull. The "I drew the line at the ceiling I had just paid for" landed as a sentence even when I didn't know what it meant.

What didn't: nouns. There are almost no concrete physical nouns that would let me see the place. "Tallow smoke," "stitch-house east wall," "brick," "drain" — these are the only solid things I can picture. Everything else is "the body," "the junction," "the count," "the entry." The woman — the supposed central figure of the chapter — is never described. I don't know her age, her clothes, her posture, her face. She is only ever "the junction-signature." For a chapter that turns on a moral act against a person, that person has to exist as a person at some point, and she doesn't.

Register issue: "subsistence-reading," "function-signature," "junction-signature," "connector-type," "accounting," "coverage map" — the technical vocabulary is dense and almost entirely undefined. A reader who arrived here cold would need a glossary.

I'd give this chapter to a workshop and ask: who is the woman? What does the narrator *do* to her, in physical terms? What happens next, in the world, because of what was done? If the answers are "I'm withholding for effect," I'd say the withholding has eaten the chapter.
