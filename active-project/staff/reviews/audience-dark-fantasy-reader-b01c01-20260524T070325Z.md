## 1. Did you finish the chapter?

Mixed. I read every sentence — it's twenty-seven lines, you don't skim out of a chapter that short, you just slow down or speed up. I sped up through the middle. Lines 3 through 10 are one long held breath where almost nothing moves except her count, and I started skipping over the noun-clusters to find where the world was going to push back. It pushed back at the girl. I came back for the girl.

## 2. Where did your Threshold Discipline fire?

Two places. The card's discipline lives in `hot_buttons` and `fatigue` — both fired.

- **fatigue / setting-stops-feeling-hostile** — fired at line 7: *"Every warm body in the block was legible at the work-level density I was not working against."* The sentence is doing a lot of grammar to tell me she's safe inside her own perception. Flea Bottom is supposed to be hostile terrain. For three or four lines in a row it stops being hostile — it becomes a workshop she's measuring. I started skimming.
- **hot_button / magic-as-reliable-tool (flag)** — fired at line 5: *"The sense ran the walls and the walls gave it back unchanged, because nothing here had called for what I was not doing."* The power reports cleanly. No noise, no cost, no uncertainty. It's the sense of someone using a tool they trust. The chapter knows this is a problem — the girl's last line is the chapter naming the problem — but the prose itself spent six lines treating the power as reliable before the girl arrived to undermine it. Flag, not walkout. The girl saved it.

A near-miss third: the watch in line 11 — *"I held the eyes."* — could have been an enemies-pause-at-convenient-moments flag, but the watch isn't the enemy here, they're scenery she's calibrating against, so I let it pass.

## 3. What did you like?

*"There were flies on the meat-stall," she said. "There weren't any on your hand."*

That's the chapter. A child in a tenement ward arriving at the protagonist's secret through the one channel the protagonist's instruments don't cover: insects, the body, the smell of meat. The narrator runs a sensor net and the world routes around it through a nine-year-old who watched. The next line — *"What the girl saw was what the read should have returned — the girl arrived at it without the insects"* — is the protagonist conceding that her tool failed and a child's eyes didn't. That is the world pushing back in a way that costs something. I exhaled.

## 4. What did you dislike or distrust?

*"the anonymous copper-star transaction purchasing entry."*

Line 1, last clause. I distrust this voice. It's the voice of someone naming an economic mechanism in the same breath as paying the keeper — the protagonist is narrating Flea Bottom like a systems analyst and the prose is letting her. Westeros doesn't talk like that. Flea Bottom doesn't talk like that. The whole opening paragraph is a glossary entry braided into an action — "stone tenements packed around hook-channels," "no titles and no guild rolls," "subsistence-class permanent" — and it's the wrong flavor of shock-absence the card warns about: adaptation as explanation rather than fear of the specific place. I would have trusted the chapter sooner if Taylor had paid the keeper and said nothing about what the copper star was for.

## 5. Would you read chapter 2?

Yes. The girl is a debt the chapter just opened, and I want to see what it costs to close it — or to leave it open.

## 6. One sentence to a friend.

"It's quiet in the wrong way for most of it, and the protagonist talks like a spreadsheet, but a barefoot girl walks up at the end and tells her there were flies on the meat and not on her hand, and the whole chapter rearranges around that — I'd keep going."
