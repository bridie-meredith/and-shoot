## The 8 answers

**1. What happened?**
A woman who runs some kind of surveillance/protection operation in a poor district ("the Hook") comes down her usual morning route and finds her path blocked by a handcart at a sept corner. There she has an exchange with Septon Halvard, a local Faith figure who tends the sick. He delivers a moral argument about a man who works for a corrupt house on bad terms; the woman recognizes the argument as a mirror of a compromise she herself has made. She counters by naming a dead child the slow/principled way failed to save. They take each other's measure, resolve nothing, and she leaves. It is essentially a single static conversation that crystallizes a moral standoff the protagonist is already living inside.

**2. Causality — why does she stay?**
This is the chapter's deliberate central question and it is handled VERY explicitly — almost over-explicitly. The text repeatedly states that her body's instinct was to leave/route around, that staying was "the more expensive option," and that "I paid it." It is named at least three times (lines 17, 35, the closing leaving "before a resolution that was not coming"). So her staying is unmistakably MOTIVATED — but it is motivated by assertion rather than by felt dramatic pressure. I was TOLD she chose to stay and that it cost her, more than I FELT a pull keeping her there. It reads as a thesis about staying rather than a person unable to walk away. Followable, yes. Organically motivated, only partly.

**3. The argument — did it land?**
The two sides are clear and I could follow both. Septon's side: working within a crooked system on its terms only entrenches the crookedness; the slow refusal costs more now, less later, and at least the debt doesn't grow past where you can see its end. Her side (implicit, then stated via the dead child): the slow principled way let a real child die that her compromised system could have saved. Neither "wins" — explicitly so; they "resolved nothing." That non-resolution feels right and earned; it's the honest outcome. The weakness is that the argument is delivered as nearly pure abstraction — ledgers, accounts, intervals, architecture, "compounds at its own rate" — so it lands as an idea-clash more than a human confrontation.

**4. The dead child — concrete?**
Yes, there is a specific named death and it is the load-bearing beat. The Cobb girl, Wenna, six years old, of Pig-Tallow Lane in the Hook. Died of fever two seasons/years back, before the protagonist's coverage existed to route a maester-call; it took eleven days, and by the time someone could carry the call it was a burial, not a call. This is genuinely concrete: name, age, lane, cause, duration, and the specific systemic failure. It is the strongest, most grounded beat in the chapter. Slight risk: it is delivered in the same ledger-register as everything else ("the founding entry," "the first name in the count"), which threatens to abstract it — but the specifics (Wenna, six, eleven days) survive that and land.

**5. Two children — one or two named?**
TWO. (a) Derry — the carter's boy, up the lane, two days into a fever, ALIVE, the occasion of the cart blocking the way. (b) Wenna Cobb — six, Pig-Tallow Lane, DEAD two years back, the body count's founding entry. They are clearly distinct and I did not conflate them: Derry is the living child at the scene's open; Wenna is the dead child the protagonist names as her counter-argument. The chapter actually uses Derry well as a live, present stake that sets up the abstract argument before Wenna grounds the protagonist's reply. No confusion.

**6. READABLE axis.**
I could follow it start to finish. The prose is dense and abstract and I had to slow down — particularly the opening italic paragraph (lines 1) and the "two accountings stood in the cold" stretch (lines 27, 31), which pile ledger-metaphor on ledger-metaphor. I re-read line 27's closing ("an older shape: a smaller door that had stayed shut on someone I could not reach in time") because it gestures at backstory I don't have. But I never lost the thread of what was physically happening: woman, cart, septon, argument, dead-child counter, departure. Followable.

**7. AIRLESS axis.**
This is the chapter's real exposure. There IS a body and it does breathe in a few specific places: the ground-cold coming up through the soles (line 21), the feet that begin to shift toward the lane and the heel settled back, weight pressing the cobble's edge (line 35), "my breath went out before the name did" (line 23), the septon's hands going still on his knees (line 29), the body simply arresting "the way it does when a thing you have been carrying gets set down in front of you" (line 13). Those are real, inhabited, felt. But the connective tissue — and especially the middle argument and the protagonist's interior — reads heavily as apparatus reporting itself: "the insect-feed had him placed," "a node the count had logged a hundred times," "the architecture of the thing I had built," "I named the body count," "the leaving was the filing." The narrator processes her own experience in surveillance/accounting vocabulary so relentlessly that the person is partly buried under the system she runs. The middle argument has SOME body (the planted feet, the cold) but is at risk of being a seminar — the feet are doing the work of keeping it embodied, and they nearly carry it. Verdict: alive at the edges, airless in the connective interior, but the load-bearing dead-child beat itself is carried by concrete specifics and a felt breath. Net: it breathes just enough.

**8. CONTINUE?**
Barely-yes. The dead-child beat and the honest non-resolution are genuinely compelling and I want to know what compromise she's actually running and why — but the relentless ledger/apparatus register is fatiguing and I'd hope the next chapter gives me more body and less accounting.
