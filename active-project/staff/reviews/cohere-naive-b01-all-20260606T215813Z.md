# Naive cold-read — b01, all 20 chapters

I read this straight through as one continuous stretch, bringing nothing from outside the page. Below are the eight verdicts, then an overall impression.

## Q1 — Voice/register consistency — PASS

This is unmistakably one narrator across all twenty chapters. The first-person voice — clipped declaratives, the obsessive ledger/accounting metaphor, the "I held the feet" / "the count closed" / "I lifted the stylus" rhythm — is held with remarkable discipline from Chapter 1's "I have a count. I hold the count at its edge by the cost of holding it" all the way to Chapter 20's "the not-stopping carrying the whole of it." The italic chapter-opening preambles are a stable structural signature too. There is no chapter where I felt a different hand took over. If anything the consistency is *too* perfect — the voice never relaxes, never varies its texture — but as a question of "does it feel like one narrator," it is a clean PASS.

## Q2 — Setup→payoff inventory — CAUTION (LOAD-BEARING)

The prose makes several promises early. The receipts are mixed.

LANDS:
- **The rule ("be useful without taking control," Ch1) → its erosion.** The whole spine. The escalation from "patterns, not persons" (Ch3-4) to handing up four named ward-elders (Ch6) to routing Corwick (Ch10) to the full-city fortnight deployment (Ch18) is a clean, well-tracked payoff. The rule is named, compromised by increments, and the final chapter pays it off ("the same routing... in a different place for reasons that did not hold").
- **Sera's protection (Ch3) → the standing consideration** that recurs as the price of every deliverable through Ch20. Consistent.
- **The Cobb girl / Wenna, "the first name in the count" (Ch4, Ch7) → the founding justification** that the whole accounting is built to answer. Paid off thematically.
- **Wren / the stitch-maker's ward (Ch1 onward) → the one withheld name → the final loss.** Set up insistently (the "print held longer," the eastern gap kept blank for her) and paid off devastatingly in Ch20 when the fire scatters the insects in exactly the gap kept open for her. This is the strongest setup→payoff arc in the book.

DROPS / UNRECEIPTED:
- **The opening line: "I had won my last world by becoming a hive of bugs, and I had not meant to wake."** This promises a prior-life identity and a catastrophe. It's gestured at repeatedly ("a war whose dates I carry and do not say aloud," "the thing I did when the world was ending") but the actual content is *never* delivered — deliberately, but a naive reader is left with a permanent withheld card that never turns over. Borderline; reads as intentional mystery but undeniably a promise not cashed within these 20 chapters.
- **Aemond (Ch8, Ch15).** Introduced with weight twice — "the apparatus moving the largest dragon in the sky," then a full training-yard set-piece in Ch15 — and then the narrator explicitly says "Aemond's arrival changed nothing I was doing." He never pays off as a character; he's a thematic prop (a body shaped like a coming weapon). The Ch15 chapter spends real page-budget on a payoff that the narrator herself defuses.
- **Otto Hightower** is named as the engine behind everything but never appears on-page; he stays an abstraction. Acceptable for this structure, but a reader wanting to *meet* the antagonist never does.

CAUTION because the central arc lands cleanly but two heavily-weighted setups (the prior-world catastrophe, Aemond) are either deferred indefinitely or self-cancelled.

## Q3 — Calendar/time legibility — CAUTION

Time-passage is *trackable* but it's done through prose I had to work for. Early chapters anchor on a recurring almanac — "salt-day, the second of the fishmongers' three," the sept name-days (Maiden, Father, Crone's stretch), the bay-wind "turning damp." That's atmospheric and I could feel weeks accumulating. But the larger spans jump in the preambles with numbers I have to trust rather than feel: "Three weeks" (Ch2), "Two months on" (Ch7), "Nine circuits" (Ch10), "eleven months" (Ch15), "seven months" (Ch19). By the back half the elapsed time is asserted in the italic frames rather than dramatized, and the figures don't always reconcile easily on a first read (Ch15 says "eleven months," Ch19 references a "seven-month contact," Ch20 references "eleven months" again). I never got lost, but I was tracking a stated clock, not an felt one. CAUTION.

## Q4 — Character-presence accumulation — CAUTION (LOAD-BEARING)

EARNED / CARRIED:
- **Wren** is the model case — felt from Ch1, indexed deliberately across nearly every chapter, never named in deliverables, and her final endangerment lands precisely because she's been carried so patiently. Fully earned.
- **Septon Halvard** is genuinely earned. He recurs (Ch7, Ch13, Ch16) as the moral counter-voice, and each return deepens. By Ch16's "I am asking you to know what you're doing," he has real weight because the prior encounters paid in.
- **Jarvis Coin** and **Oswyn Mudway** accumulate well as functional fixtures.

ARRIVES COLD / UNDEREARNED:
- **Corwick** (Ch8-10) carries a lot of plot weight — his routing-up-the-line is a major moral beat — but he arrives essentially as a gait-signature and a posture-class. The narrator insists he's "a whole man laid down circuit by circuit," yet the reader never gets a *person*; he's a coverage-node the prose tells me to feel for. His detention is meant to land hard and lands abstractly.
- **The east-of-water-gate courier** (Ch14) has the identical problem — eleven weeks of tracking asserted, "the low-set left eye" offered as the one human detail, then detained. Same structure as Corwick, same cold arrival.
- **Aemond** (see Q2) arrives with fanfare and then is told to not matter.
- **Daven, Elder Norren, Aldric** appear primarily as accounting entries; Aldric at least gets a courtroom scene (Ch13) that humanizes him.

The pattern: the *moral-witness* characters (Wren, Halvard) are beautifully earned; the *plot-instrument* characters (Corwick, the courier) arrive cold at exactly the moments the book needs them to feel earned, and the narrator's accounting-register actively works against making them felt. CAUTION, leaning toward the negative on the load-bearing instrument characters.

## Q5 — Sensory texture distribution — FAIL

There is real sensory grounding, and it is excellent when it appears: the tallow smoke pooling at floor level and not rising (Ch6), the wax "gone from soft and warm under the thumb to set firm" (Ch9), the cold coming up through the boot-soles, the bay-damp beading on dry stone, the iron dish holding residual warmth after the burned page (Ch11), the smell of the burn reaching the outer wards "ahead of any heat or visible smoke" (Ch20). These are genuinely embodied. But they are heavily front-loaded and thin out badly. By the middle chapters (Ch9-12) and especially Ch17-19, the prose is dominated by *abstracted* sensing — "the feed returned," "the count filed," "the column closed," "geometric not contested," "the architecture routed around it." Whole paragraphs proceed without a single thing seen, smelled, or touched by a body. The recurring tactile anchor (a cold hand on stone/rail/board) gets repeated so often it stops grounding and becomes a tic. The sensory budget is real but unevenly distributed, and the back half lists/abstracts far more than it grounds. FAIL on distribution.

## Q6 — Apparatus-register cumulative load — FAIL (LOAD-BEARING)

This is the book's biggest problem read end-to-end. The accounting/instrument/ledger register is the voice's signature, and in a single chapter it's striking. Across twenty chapters it accumulates *far* past my tolerance. Nearly every action is rendered in the same vocabulary: feed, count, column, ledger, entry, filed, routed, node, coverage, parameters, register, threshold, the breach-column, the cost-side, the source-field. By Ch11-12 I was reading paragraphs that are almost entirely abstract bookkeeping — "I ran it in motion and closed the arms one at a time. The Jarvis arm closed... The Oswyn arm closed... Third arm — the contacts —". The metaphor never varies, never gets a vacation, never lets a scene breathe in plain human terms for long. Worse, the emotional climaxes are *all* delivered in this same register — contempt "files as a finding," love for Wren is "a column I would not let price," grief is "the distance the point did not travel down to the page." The technique is deliberate and occasionally piercing, but cumulatively it flattens twenty chapters into one tonal note and made the back third genuinely fatiguing to read. This is the clearest FAIL.

## Q7 — Genuine sub-section vs glued-together chapters — CAUTION

It reads as a single designed arc, not random chapters thrown together — the rule's erosion, the Wren thread, the Halvard returns, and the escalating scale all build deliberately. So it is *more* than glued-together. But it also reads as *over-uniform* in a way that betrays the seams: every chapter follows nearly the identical template (italic preamble → a single observed incident → a ledger-closing scene at the feed-station → a withheld entry), and the chapters are conspicuously similar in length, shape, and rhythm. That sameness is the tell that these were produced as discrete units to a fixed pattern. A real book sub-section would vary its chapter architecture — a long chapter, a short one, a scene that doesn't end at the ledger. Here the machine of the chapter-template is visible. So: it coheres thematically (more than glued) but the production-template shows through (less than a fully organic sub-section). CAUTION.

## Q8 — Close-of-section pleasure — CAUTION

The ending is well-judged on its own terms. Ch20's collapse — the king's death, the apparatus decommissioning her "function" with no person addressed, the fire scattering the insects in the exact gap she kept open for Wren, the walk out at dusk — is a genuinely strong, thematically-earned close. The final image ("the gate gave onto the road at dusk... the not-stopping carrying the whole of it") is good. I *did* want to know what happens to Wren, and whether the prior-world catastrophe ever gets named — so the book left live hooks. But my appetite for *more* is dampened by the register-fatigue (Q6): by the end I admired the ending more than I hungered for the next stretch, because twenty chapters in the same accounting-voice had worn me down. Satisfied by the ending's craft; not actively pulling for the next section. CAUTION.

## Overall impression

This is a tightly controlled, single-voiced piece of work with a real spine: a woman who swore not to take control building exactly the machine of control she fled, paid for one increment at a time, with one withheld name (Wren) carrying the whole emotional load to a strong final collapse. The Wren and Halvard threads are genuinely earned and the setup→payoff on the central rule lands cleanly. But read as twenty chapters in one sitting, the relentless accounting/feed/ledger register accumulates past tolerance and starves the back half of sensory air, while the plot-instrument characters (Corwick, the eastern courier) and a few heavily-flagged setups (the prior world, Aemond) arrive cold or self-cancel. It coheres as a designed arc but the uniform chapter-template and the single tonal note keep it from breathing like an organic stretch of a novel.
