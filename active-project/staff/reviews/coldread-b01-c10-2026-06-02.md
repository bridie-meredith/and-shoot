# Cold-read — b01-c10 — 2026-06-02

First-time reader, text only. Blunt.

## 1. EVENTS
- A courier (Jarvis) sets a sealed packet (Otto's wax seal) on a "morning stone." The narrator reads it. The packet states that an existing arrangement is now formally a "function": the narrator delivers the lower city's intelligence up the line, in exchange for continued protection of someone named Sera.
- The packet's second item is a *description* of a man — no name — whom Otto's people want identified. The narrator realizes they already know this man: Corwick, whom they've secretly tracked ("body-mapped") for months in a private record never shared with Jarvis.
- The narrator transcribes Corwick's movement-data into the channel ("the conversion priced and paid") and routes it up the line. This is framed as the decisive, irreversible act.
- Two days later: on a routine surveillance circuit, the man's usual spot on the road is empty. A pair of Gold Cloaks (city watch) are now posted, at rest, at the junction where his corridor emptied. The narrator infers Corwick was taken/removed.
- At day's end the narrator opens a ledger, accounts the gain (Sera safe, channel operational) against the cost (Corwick gone), writes him in as a "closed entry," closes the ledger. Stares at his lingering signature in the feed-record. End.

So: one decision, one delivery, one off-screen consequence, one reckoning. Almost everything physical happens *inside* the narrator's surveillance apparatus or is inferred — Corwick's removal is never shown.

## 2. JEOPARDY
Yes, but it's already spent. Corwick is at risk and by mid-chapter is presumably dead or captured — but we never see him, never hear him, and learn of his fate only as an empty spot on a road. Sera is under a protection deal but is offstage and never characterized; her stakes are asserted, not felt. The narrator faces moral/complicity jeopardy (they've handed a man to a war machine), which is the real stake — but it's internal, not bodily.

## 3. CAUSALITY
The causal chain is clear and clean: packet → recognition → delivery → disappearance → reckoning. I always understood *why* the next thing followed. What I could NOT pin down: *why the narrator complies.* "Sera's protection" is the stated lever, but Sera is a bare name with zero presence, so the coercion has no weight. I'm told the narrator had no choice ("no version of the sentence I could fold back into a question") but not shown the cost of refusing. The motive is asserted.

## 4. PAYOFF
The ending — closing the ledger, then being unable to stop looking at the dead man's signature — is a real, earned turn: the bureaucratic act of "closing" vs. the thing that won't close in the narrator. That landed for me as the one genuinely emotional beat. But it's thin, because the man it mourns was never a person on the page — just a "geometry," a "posture," a "mark." I felt the narrator's residue more than I felt the loss.

## 5. CONTINUE?
Barely yes — I'd turn one more page to see who Sera is and what the narrator becomes, but mostly out of obligation, not pull.

## 6. ONE-LINE SUMMARY
A surveillance operator, coerced by a protection deal, hands a man they'd quietly tracked for months over to a war machine, then sits with the fact that the man is gone and the record of him isn't.

## Confusion notes
- I was confused about the *physical setting*. "Morning stone," "feed-station," "the circuit," "insect-feed," "the channel" — I think this is a near-future/SF surveillance operator, but it could be fantasy. "Gold Cloaks / King's Landing" is explicitly Westeros, which clashes hard with the techno-surveillance register. I couldn't reconcile the world.
- The prose is dense, abstract, and repetitive — "the way X did Y the way it did Y." It kept me at arm's length the whole time. I never had a clear body in a clear room.
