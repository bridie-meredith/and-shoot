## Residual concerns — adjudicated

### 1. 2-rung below target band (15.6%): is the 20% floor a hard requirement or guidance? Does the cross-facet contract still hold? Should a screen-writer kickback be issued?

The 20% floor is guidance, not a hard requirement. The rubric's frequency test reads: "A distribution outside that band suggests systemic miscalibration — investigate before shipping." It is an investigation trigger, not an automatic reject. The Phase 4 distribution of 15.6% 2s results from four honest downward revisions of genuine anti-pattern violations. No surviving 2-entry is inflated; the 1-rung excess reflects the episode's genuine scarcity of pre-gaze-fix charged beats and its clean post-climax release structure.

Cross-facet contract: the 2-rung at 15.6% creates a real stitcher-gradient compression. The stitcher's rendering density scale depends on the gradient between 1s and 2s — with 79.2% of beats at 1, the compressible pool is large and the "normal rendering" pool is thin. Downstream facets that cluster on 2-beats (loudness flags, audience-interest flags) will find fewer anchoring positions than the rubric's design assumes. This is a structural limitation of the source material, not a miscalibration of the tensometer file.

Screen-writer kickback: one is warranted as a producer note, not as a blocking action. The specific gap is the pre-gaze-fix approach sequence (@1-@22), which runs 22 consecutive 1s — an extended flat-field that the rubric permits only with dramatist-flagged exception ("scene-as-respite / scene-as-transit"). No such flag is present. The rubric's scene-level shape rule states "No flatlining. A scene of 30+ beats with no 2s or 3s is a scene that does no dramatic work." The @1-@22 run is 22 beats, below the 30-beat trigger, so no mandatory kickback is required. However, for downstream facets to have adequate gradient, noting that a future episode revision could benefit from one additional charged beat in the @1-@22 approach is a legitimate producer observation. This is not a shippability blocker; it is a flagged structural limitation for and-wrap.

**Verdict:** 20% floor is guidance; cross-facet contract holds under the limitation; screen-writer kickback is a producer note, not a blocking requirement.

### 2. @33 negative-event 2: does the rubric's reversal-proximity language admit negative events?

The rubric's reversal-proximity 2 language does not explicitly admit negative events. The ACCEPT signatures are: "the beat is the breath before the move, the alignment that makes the next move possible, the closing distance." All three descriptors are action-forward. A door staying shut fits none of them directly. The Phase 4 defense correctly pivots to stakes-visibility as the primary axis, which does hold. Reversal-proximity does not hold on plain rubric text and should not be cited as a supporting axis in the shipped file.

**Verdict:** Rubric does not admit negative events under reversal-proximity at current V2 text. @33 stands at 2 on stakes-visibility alone. The reversal-proximity axis-citation should be removed from the filed entry's annotation. Rating does not change.

### 3. @60 body-charge POV question: is body-charge POV-restricted or open to antagonist posture?

The rubric's body-charge axis is not explicitly POV-restricted. The V2 rubric reads: "Does the beat carry physical investment — held breath, locked posture, deliberate freeze, or sudden release?" The subject is the beat, not the protagonist. The rubric's worked examples are predominantly from the protagonist's posture, but the worked examples are calibration anchors, not exhaustive scope definitions. The ACCEPT signatures do not name a subject restriction. The REJECT signatures name "stillness inflation" and "calling routine motion body-charged" — neither restriction is POV-based.

However, the Phase 3 seam correctly identifies a real ambiguity: every rubric example of body-charge 2 and 3 is from the protagonist's physical investment. The antagonist's angled foot is territorial posture that loads the scene-frame. The rubric's stillness-inflation anti-pattern explicitly warns that "the scene-frame, not the beat, carries the load" in the case of ambient environmental pressure. The officer's angled foot maintaining a territorial stance while the scene continues is closer to scene-frame load than to beat-specific body-charge as the examples demonstrate it.

**Verdict:** The rubric does not explicitly POV-restrict body-charge, but the preponderance of examples and the anti-pattern structure suggest the intended scope is the participant whose investment is the beat's primary content. For @60, the officer's angled foot is a marginal case: it can be read as scene-frame environmental pressure (1) or as a body-charge hold with the officer as subject (2). The Phase 4 defense's hold on body-charge is defensible but not airtight. @60 stays at 2 because the rubric does not prohibit it and the defense identifies the held-against-what on screen. This is a rubric gap that V3 should close with an explicit statement on POV scope for body-charge. Flag for rubric revision, not for entry correction.

### 4. @39 double-tap 3: held-against-turn confirmed or climax bleed?

Confirmed as held-against-turn. The proto-line sequence is unambiguous: @38 = Taylor's action; @39 = Taylor's positional hold; @40 = officer's response. The officer has not responded at @39. The held-against-turn class requires "the next beat will commit one way or another" — @40 is that next beat and it does commit (officer unfolds the letter). @39 is structurally the held-breath beat. ### 5. @48/@64 internal-consistency: is the distinction rubric-grounded enough to ship?

Yes, with a caveat. The distinction ("@48 documents a prior turn; @64 IS the turn") is rubric-grounded under stakes-visibility 3's "registration IS the event of turning" language. The practical grounding: at @48, the determination was externalized at @43 (the handoff); at @64, no prior beat declared the parallel-marks outcome. A facet consumer reading the tensometer in isolation will see two stylus-commit beats rated differently (@48=2, @64=3) and needs the axis-citations to understand the distinction.

The distinction is rubric-grounded enough to ship if and only if the axis-citations are explicit in the filed facet file. The current Phase 4 inline annotations achieve this for @48 ("DEFENDED at 2: @48 documents a prior turn; @64 IS the turn") and for @64 ("DEFENDED: distinct from @48 — @64 is the turn; @48 documents a prior turn"). These annotations must survive into the shipped file verbatim. If the annotations are stripped to the bare format (`<id> @<n> <scalar>`), the distinction becomes invisible to downstream consumers.

**Verdict:** Distinction ships; annotations must ship with it. A producer note for state-update co-citation at @64 (strongly expected) and the absence of such expectation at @48 (already documented at @43) should accompany the file.

## What worked / what to forward

The five-phase tensometer tuning run produced two things the dialogue and loc-state runs did not. First, a curve-level audit as a distinct second-pass instrument: the dialogue and loc-state runs reviewed per-entry accuracy, but tensometer added the SHAPE-OK / SHAPE-FAIL curve verdict as a structural check that is independent of per-entry accuracy and catches episode-level failures a correct per-entry pass can miss. Second, the adversarial-seams pass (Phase 3) generated a formal seam inventory with strength classifications — a methodology that could be applied to any facet tuning run and would catch the sustained anti-pattern clusters (speech-beat, stillness-inflation, ambient-escalation) earlier, before the writer-fork rather than after. What transferred from dialogue and loc-state: the round-trip protocol (writer produces → auditor reviews → seams → defense/revise → final adjudication) proved equally productive for a scalar facet as for binary facets. What is new and unique to tensometer: the frequency-band test as a systemic miscalibration detector (not available in binary facets), the curve-shape verdict as a structural guarantee that no per-entry pass can substitute for, and the cross-facet contract section as an explicit pre-ship gate — the clearest statement to date of what a locked facet owes to its downstream consumers.
