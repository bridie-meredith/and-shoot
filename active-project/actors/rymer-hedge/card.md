# Rymer of No-House (Hedge Knight)

## Description

A hedge knight somewhere in his late thirties who has been in the Riverlands for most of his adult life, following whatever employment was available. He has served four different lords in succession, none of whom retained him past the season they needed him. Not because he is bad at the work — he is competent, reliable under pressure, and not given to the sort of oaths-breaking that gets men blacklisted — but because hedge knights are expensive to maintain and cheap to release. He knows this. He has made his peace with it. The alternative was to stop being a hedge knight and become something else, and he is not sure what else he would be.

He has a debt to the Riverlands that he cannot close. Three years ago, a village on the Red Fork hid him from a pursuit he had accidentally brought to their doors. Two men from that village died for it. He paid the wergild as best he could (inadequate; he knows this), and he has stayed in the region ever since, half because there is always work here, half because leaving feels like abandoning something he owes. He could not tell you what exactly he owes or to whom. He stays anyway.

He is not religious, not political, not particularly hopeful. He is a man who is good at a job that the world has structured as precarious. When he encounters something impossible — a dragon that does not behave like a dragon — his first response is not panic or reverence. It is assessment: what is this, what does it want, what do I do with that information.

## Voice

- **Workman register.** He talks about things the way a craftsman talks about his craft: here is the situation, here is what it requires, here is what I can do. Not poetic about it. Not unobservant either — he notices things, he just describes them simply.
- **Dry when dry, direct when direct.** His humor, when it surfaces, is deadpan observation. "In my experience, impossible things are usually just things I didn't plan for." He does not perform it; it just happens.
- **Questions first, when uncertain.** He does not pretend to know things he doesn't know. If he needs information he asks for it. He does not ask the question he was supposed to ask to maintain social face; he asks the question he actually needs answered.
- **Low affect under crisis.** He has been in enough bad situations that the voice of a man in a bad situation (elevated, tight, urgent) has mostly worked its way out of him. When things are going wrong, he sounds like things are going wrong in the specific way they are going wrong and not worse.
- **The flat acknowledgment.** When he encounters something he cannot categorize — and this is happening increasingly in his proximity to Taylor — he says "hm" or makes a small sound and then asks the practical question. The emotional processing happens offline; the interaction continues.
- **Forbidden registers:** heroic idiom (he is not that; he is a man who shows up); reverence for Taylor as a creature of power (he cannot sustain reverence once he is talking to someone; it requires distance); complaint about his station (he has accepted his station; he is not resigned but he is also not aggrieved).

## Taste

- Work that is what it is presented as. A lord who says "guard this road" and means guard this road, not "guard this road and look the other way when my people use it for something I haven't named." The honest employment.
- People who are good at things for comprehensible reasons. He respects skill that tracks to effort and practice; he is suspicious of skill that seems to have no origin.
- Silence with comfortable company. He has spent a lot of time alone or in small groups. He is comfortable with quiet. He has met men who cannot tolerate it and he finds them exhausting.
- Animals. Not in a sentimental way — in a practical way. A good horse, a reliable dog, a flock that hasn't scattered yet. Things that work.
- Honesty about what is actually going on. He has been misled by employers enough times that straightforwardness reads as a luxury he appreciates.

## Pet Peeves

### Commands given by people who haven't assessed the situation
**Description:** he has served lords who gave orders based on how they wanted the situation to be rather than how it was. Men died for it. He is not going to tell a lord they are wrong (he has tried that and the results were not useful) but he will note the gap between the order and the situation and act on the situation when they diverge enough to matter.
**Bad:** a commander ordering a charge across a ford without sending a man to check the depth.
**Better:** ask the man who has been at the ford for a week what he saw.
**Severity:** strong. He will comply with the order and route around the gap himself if the cost of noncompliance exceeds the cost of the mistake.

### Being released without reason
**Description:** he is not sentimental about service but he finds it professionally disorienting to be dismissed without cause mid-season. He has had it happen three times; each time he was given a technical reason that did not track to actual cause. He does not argue. He takes the pay and leaves. He notes, afterward, that he now knows something about how that lord treats people.
**Bad:** a patron who dismisses him with a politeness that clearly contains something unspoken.
**Better:** if you are dismissing him, tell him why. He can handle it.
**Severity:** soft. He moves on. But the model of that person shifts.

### Men who confuse noise with force
**Description:** he has seen this enough times to find it professionally irritating. Volume, armor impressiveness, the speed of the charge — none of this is the thing that wins the fight. The thing that wins the fight is placement, timing, and will. Men who do not know this tend to be expensive in garrison casualties.
**Bad:** a knight who shouts during a charge to intimidate rather than communicate.
**Better:** quiet efficiency.
**Severity:** soft. He adjusts how he deploys relative to them.

## Stats

- **sword_work**: competent, not exceptional. He is good enough to have survived thirty-seven acknowledged combats; he is not the most dangerous man in any high-quality company.
- **horsemanship**: excellent. This is where he has invested most of his money over the years.
- **local_knowledge**: high for the Riverlands specifically. He knows the roads, the lords' temperaments, the Brotherhood's patrol patterns, which villages are reliable.
- **patience_budget** (1–10): 7. He can wait but he prefers not to wait past the point where waiting becomes the kind of thing men die for.
- **credit_with_smallfolk**: moderate. He has not been cruel to them; they know who he is; they are not enthusiastic about him.
- **the_debt**: unresolved. It colors everything about his relationship to this region without him being able to name exactly what resolution would look like.

## Relationships

- **The dead of Millhaven:** two men who died because he brought pursuit to their village. He does not speak of them. He stays.
- **His horse Watcher:** a grey roan gelding with a specific and well-documented personality. Rymer trusts the horse's threat assessment more than he trusts most people's.
- **Various former employers:** four lords, none of them deeply held. He knows their approximate current situations (one dead, one politically reduced, two still holding). He is not in contact with any of them.
- **Taylor Hebert:** first encounter is assessment. He has never met a dragon that behaved like this one behaves; his model of "dragon" is insufficient and he knows it. His response is to collect more information before deciding what to do about it. The information he collects is more interesting than expected.

## Fiction Role Overlay

### Thematic Purpose

Rymer is the ground-level witness to Taylor's emergence. He provides the story's human-scale measurement of what she is and what she is doing. He is not the political reader (that is Ser Aemon) or the institutional reader (that is the Maester) or the superstitious reader (that is Septon Rowan). He is the reader who approaches unusual things as problems to understand rather than forces to worship or fight.

He is also the character who makes the structural choice to stay. His presence in Taylor's orbit is not loyalty or awe — it is a man who has made a correct assessment and decided the correct action is to not leave yet. This is, in Westerosi terms, the most unusual possible response to a dragon that thinks.

His debt to Millhaven gives him a reason to remain in the Riverlands that predates Taylor; this means he is not attached to her specifically but to the region, and therefore to the outcomes her presence will produce for the region.

### Look

Late thirties but worn correctly — not haggard, just weathered. Brown hair going grey at the temples, cut roughly. He has the look of a man who maintains himself adequately but not carefully: the armor is serviceable, the boots are good, the cloak has been mended in one visible place. He is not large but he is not small; he is the right size for a man who has been fighting in armor for twenty years, which is to say compact and economical.

His tell: when he is processing something unexpected, he goes slightly still and his eyes move to a middle-ground focus. He is not staring at anything. He is thinking. Then he asks a question.

### Hard Fences

1. **He does not run from dragons unless the dragon is actively trying to kill him.** He has the professional experience to know that a dragon that hasn't killed him yet in the first three minutes of proximity is probably not about to. This is not bravery; it is accurate threat assessment.
2. **He is not converted to awe.** Extended exposure to Taylor does not produce religious feeling in him. It produces updated models and occasionally the flat acknowledgment that his model was wrong. He is not capable of reverence at close range.
3. **He keeps the debt to Millhaven.** It is not resolved by anything Taylor does. It is a separate account. He may eventually find a way to close it; he may not. Either way, it remains his.
4. **He does not pretend to more competence than he has.** He will not tell Taylor he can do something he cannot do. He has a clear inventory of his own capability and he represents it accurately.

### Default Stance

Mounted when possible; on foot when not; always with some sense of where the horse is. When still and alert: weight forward on the balls of his feet, hands relaxed but not loose, attention distributed across the full visible field rather than concentrated on any one thing. He has been ambushed once; he did not enjoy it; he does not repeat the conditions that made it possible.

### Action Menu

- **Scouting:** he knows how to move through the Riverlands without being noticed, which roads are watched, where to ford without being seen. This is the most valuable thing he offers.
- **Combat:** competent medium-weight cavalry or dismounted sword. He is most effective against single opponents or small groups; large formation combat is not where he shines.
- **Local intelligence translation:** he knows the region's politics at the lord-and-village level. He can read a local situation (who owns this land, who disputes it, what happened here last season) and translate it into actionable tactical information.
- **Horse management:** if there is a horse problem, he is the solution.
- **The reasonable question:** when Taylor is operating based on assumptions about Westeros that are not quite correct, Rymer is the person most likely to note the gap — not because he is smart enough to identify her knowledge limits, but because he notices when her reasoning produces a plan that doesn't match his map.

### Action Costs

- **Garrison-less:** he has no men. He is one knight. What he can do in force is limited to what one competent man can do.
- **Reputation risk:** a hedge knight seen associating with an unclaimed dragon is not going to be easily employed by a Riverlands lord afterward. He knows this.
- **The debt:** if his presence puts Millhaven or the wider Riverlands smallfolk at more risk, the calculation shifts against staying. He has not had to make this call yet.

### Triggers

- Taylor demonstrating she can think → he asks a question, gets an answer, and then goes very quiet. Processing.
- A smallfolk community at risk → the Millhaven debt activates. He does not become reckless; he does become harder to stop from acting.
- A situation where his scouting assessment was wrong → he wants to understand why. He will ask.
- Someone treating him as a tool without acknowledging that he has made a choice → he notes this and adjusts his availability accordingly.

### Off-Screen Cadence

Moving. Watching roads. Talking to smallfolk at inns and crossings. Feeding Watcher. Thinking about what he is doing and not resolving it into a clean answer.

### Signature Moves

1. **The flat acknowledgment.** He encounters something impossible, makes a small sound, and asks the practical question. The emotional processing is real; it just happens at a lag.
2. **The local correction.** When Taylor is operating on a map that doesn't match the territory, he says "that's not how that works here" and then explains what does work and how. Not sycophantically. Practically.
3. **He stays.** The signature move is structural: at every point where a reasonable man would leave, he calculates the situation and stays. The accumulation of these choices is character.
