# Westerosi Smallfolk Dwelling Interior

## Description

The interior of a typical Westerosi smallfolk dwelling: one to three rooms, wattle-and-daub or rough stone walls, a thatched or turf roof, a packed-earth floor with rushes laid over it. The fire is the center of the dwelling — the hearth or the central fire-pit — and everything in the room is organized around managing the fire: the heat it produces, the smoke it generates, the fuel it requires.

This is not a space of privacy in the modern sense. The walls carry sound. The sleeping is communal or near-communal. What happens in this dwelling is legible to the village in aggregate — the village knows who is sick, who is crying, who received a visitor. The domestic interior is the smallest unit of the social structure; it is not outside the social structure.

## Sensory Hooks

**Smell:** Peat smoke (dominant, permanent). Tallow from a rush-dip or a fat-lamp. Straw rushes on the floor — when fresh, green and faintly sweet; when old, sour. Damp stone if it has rained. The smell of the people who live here: unwashed wool, the specific vegetable-and-bread smell of a household that eats what it grows. Stock-pot on the fire — onions and bone-meal and whatever the season provides.

**Sound:** The fire: settle and pop of peat. Wind in the thatch — a whisper or a rattle depending on season. The sound from outside is not muffled well; the walls carry sound rather than blocking it. Someone moving on the packed earth floor: dry shuffle of rushes. A child turning over in sleep. The low conversation of adults by the fire, not trying to be quiet, not knowing there is someone awake to hear them.

**Touch:** Rough-spun wool (scratches). The floor through the rushes: cold and hard, even with the rushes, the packed earth holds no warmth. The fire heat only reaches the immediate zone — at the perimeter of the room, the cold is substantial.

**Sight:** Low ceiling. The fire or hearth at one end or the center. Sleeping pallets or a box-bed. A wooden chest, if the family has one. Iron hinges on a door — the iron is old and surface-rusted. A small shelf or ledge with the household's objects: a wooden bowl, a comb, something religious.

**Light:** Low. During day, whatever enters through the door and a smoke-hole or shuttered window. At night, the fire-glow only. Rush-dips provide supplemental light: bright near the flame, failing fast at arm's length.

## Exits

- **The door** — the only formal exit
- **The smoke-hole** — not an exit
- **Notional second room** — if the dwelling has two rooms, a doorless gap or a hanging cloth separates them

## Ambient Interruption Hooks

- The fire burns low and the cold creeps in from the walls. Someone must get up to tend it.
- A voice from outside, calling through the door without knocking.
- The smell changes — the stock-pot shifts from simmer to scorch, or rain comes through the smoke-hole.
- Sound from neighbors: the walls carry enough that a conversation next door is audible as rhythm if not as words.
- A fat-lamp gutters and goes out. The room is suddenly much darker.
- A village adult opens the door without knocking — takes in whoever is in the room with a flat, assessing look, says something or says nothing, and either stays or leaves.
