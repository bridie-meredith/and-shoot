# Westerosi Smallfolk Dwelling Interior

## Description

The interior of a typical Westerosi smallfolk dwelling: one to three rooms, wattle-and-daub or rough stone walls, a thatched or turf roof, a packed-earth floor with rushes laid over it. The fire is the center of the dwelling — the hearth or the central fire-pit — and everything in the room is organized around managing the fire: the heat it produces, the smoke it generates, the fuel it requires.

This is not a space of privacy in the modern sense. The walls carry sound. The sleeping is communal or near-communal. What happens in this dwelling is legible to the village in aggregate — the village knows who is sick, who is crying, who received a visitor. The domestic interior is the smallest unit of the social structure; it is not outside the social structure.

For a newly-reincarnated person: this is the waking-space. Rough spun wool, dirt under the nails, a body smaller than expected. The first inventory happens here, before the village is encountered.

## Sensory Hooks

**Smell:** Peat smoke (dominant, permanent). Tallow from a rush-dip or a fat-lamp (animal fat, slightly rancid, with a guttering note when the draft changes). Straw rushes on the floor — when fresh, green and faintly sweet; when old, sour. Damp stone if it has rained. The smell of the people who live here: unwashed wool, the specific vegetable-and-bread smell of a household that eats what it grows. Stock-pot on the fire — onions and bone-meal and whatever the season provides.

**Sound:** The fire: settle and pop of peat. Wind in the thatch — a whisper or a rattle depending on season. The sound from outside is not muffled well; the walls carry sound rather than blocking it. Someone moving on the packed earth floor: dry shuffle of rushes. A child turning over in sleep. The low conversation of adults by the fire, not trying to be quiet, not knowing there is someone awake to hear them.

**Touch:** Rough-spun wool (scratches). The floor through the rushes: cold and hard, even with the rushes, the packed earth holds no warmth. The fire heat only reaches the immediate zone — at the perimeter of the room, the cold is substantial. A water-skin, if there is one near the bed-space, is cold to the touch. The beams overhead, if the dwelling has them: hand-hewn, irregular, reachable in a short adult's arms or a child's arms extended.

**Sight:** Low ceiling — the space is taller at the center (fire-smoke must rise) than at the walls. The fire or hearth at one end or the center. Sleeping pallets or a box-bed (the box-bed is a relative luxury — a frame that contains the heat). A wooden chest, if the family has one, for storing anything of value. Iron hinges on a door — the iron is old and surface-rusted. A small shelf or ledge with the household's objects: a wooden bowl, a comb, something religious (a Seven-pointed star scratched in wood, or a carved figure). Rush-dip holders or a fat lamp niche in the wall.

**Light:** Low. During day, whatever enters through the door (kept open in good weather) and a smoke-hole or shuttered window. At night, the fire-glow only. Rush-dips provide supplemental light: bright near the flame, failing fast at arm's length. The dwelling is never fully dark at night if the fire is maintained; it is never fully bright.

## Exits

- **The door** — the only formal exit. Typically a heavy wooden door with an iron latch; opens outward. The door faces the direction of the common or the nearest neighbor. An adult opens it easily; a child of nine must lean into it.
- **The smoke-hole** — not an exit in the functional sense. In the roof, or above the fire-space. The draft from the smoke-hole controls the fire's behavior and the room's smoke level.
- **Notional second room** — if the dwelling has two rooms, a doorless gap or a hanging cloth separates them. Sleeping and food storage.

## Ambient Interruption Hooks

- The fire burns low and the cold creeps in from the walls. Someone must get up to tend it. This is the ambient discipline of the space: maintenance is constant, not optional.
- A voice from outside, calling through the door without knocking. The village does not knock at smallfolk doors; knocking is a formality that belongs to a different stratum.
- The smell changes — the stock-pot shifts from simmer to scorch, or rain comes through the smoke-hole. The dwelling is responsive to the outside in ways the occupants manage by habit.
- Sound from neighbors: the walls carry enough that a conversation next door is audible as rhythm if not as words. A dispute, a child crying, a man coughing in the night — all of this is present.
- A fat-lamp gutters and goes out. The room is suddenly much darker. Relighting requires a coal from the fire, a specific object, muscle memory that a newly-arrived consciousness does not yet have.
- A village adult opens the door without knocking — to check the fire, retrieve a borrowed tool, or simply see who is awake — takes in whoever is in the room with a flat, assessing look, says something or says nothing, and either stays or leaves. The private-planning window collapses. This is not hostility; it is the dwelling's actual social permeability.

## Quality Notes

**Generic Westerosi scope:** Not tied to a specific named village, lordship, or region. Region-specific texture should be added via project-scope override. The dwelling described is in the median of Westerosi smallfolk canon — not the worst poverty (no floor rushes at all, no door) and not the relative comfort of a prosperous farmer (glass window, a second hearth). It is the typical.

**Canon source:** ASoIaF across the series (smallfolk interiors in Arya and Sansa chapters, particularly Book 2 Arya-in-the-Riverlands material); *A Knight of the Seven Kingdoms* for ground-truth road-and-village texture; *Fire & Blood* for Dance-era social context. The domestic interior is consistently described as low, dark, warm-near-the-fire, cold at the edges.

**Era note:** Dance of the Dragons (~130 AC). Smallfolk dwelling construction is essentially unchanged across the ASoIaF timeline for this stratum.
