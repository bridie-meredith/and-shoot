*I had won my last world by becoming a hive of bugs, and I had not meant to wake. I woke anyway in another body — a foreign woman with no name in this city, in King's Landing, in the year before the year before the year the dragons would dance. The flies came with me. Two hundred metres of them, dense as the air. The first thing I decided was that nothing I had done at the end would be done here.*

*Three weeks in this ward and I have learned the specific weight of doing nothing. The late-summer dust is still on the cobbles at the alley-mouth where the drain runs out, the kind of dust that comes off the city before the first hard rain breaks it. I sleep in the angle of a Hook alley where the drain runs and the air is full of small bodies I refuse to use. I have a count. I hold the count at its edge by the cost of holding it, every hour I am awake. The arrangement is that nobody here knows my name and I know nobody's name, and the arrangement is what I am paying for.*

*I came here to be useful without taking control. That is the rule I set before any of the rest of it. The rule is what I am holding the flies in for. The rule is older than anything in this city. The rule is what I have left.*

---

The drain water threaded the angle-gap of the stitch-house lane in the Hook — the lane-warren of Flea Bottom, King's Landing's slum-district where transient smallfolk go to be unremarkable — the trickle audible at the pinch-point, cobblestone-underfoot uneven at the angle-wall side (what Flea Bottom called a ward — a trade household that took in a parentless child for shelter and light work, not warm and not cruel).

Lane-ambient — then tallow-smoke-onset: the smoke crossed the stitch-house lane.

I held the feet. The feet held the only discipline I'd brought from before that still did what it was built for; the holding cost a quarter of me at the count's edge, and I paid it on principle every morning.

The insects swelled. The swell was the suppression slipping by a quarter measure; I'd pulled it back before the count completed.

The angle-wall narrowed the lane.

I exhaled.

The fish-cart blocked the lane — a handcart of the kind the morning fish-trade ran through the Hook from the river-side gate.

The ground carried the child's breath before the crowd registered the absence of one.

The crowd compressed.

I kept the feet.

The lane-mouth pressed the crowd — the dozen I'd need, and the second dozen I wouldn't.

The insects propagated where I'd told them to go, and the telling was a thing I wouldn't name.

The nearest dozen bodies yielded.

The gap propagated outward.

I faced the child.

I raised my voice.

"Fever. Not the croup," I said. "She needs air. Stand back. Who knows her? Fetch them."

I lifted my hands.

After the gap closed, the crowd came apart the way Flea Bottom crowds did.

The fish-cart man faced me. Two women, side by side at the edge of the press, faced the lane.

Oswyn Mudway took the lane-mouth — the Mudway man, a Hook ward-elder who had stood thirty years on these streets and named what he saw without explaining why he had noticed. His hands settled at his apron-front. The elder had taken the lane-mouth a week before he would have the word for it.

The child cleared the lane.

The gap closed.

I faced the alley-mouth. I set my body to it so the stitch-house lane would not need to be registered again.

The tallow smoke layered the lane-floor.

Oswyn lifted his chin. The chin lifted the way it lifted in the country's older stories when a word had been waiting for a person and the person had finally arrived to fit it. The chin-lift filed me in a category he recognized the shape of without needing the country's name for it.

The stitch-maker's ward — a Hook girl of eleven who watched cloth before she cut it, and watched people the same way — faced me. Her face found mine, eyes first. I filed the registration in the same fold where I kept the things that did not enter the ledger.

*The crowd had broken open around the stitch-maker's ward, and the hands had gone up before mine did. After that I went back to the drain angle and did not feed anyone for three weeks. The late-summer ran out somewhere in those three weeks and the Hook started keeping its mornings by the salt-day rhythm — the fishmongers' three-day, the carter-day, the lay-day the salt-cured trade kept between. I held the count at its edge by the cost of holding it. What I did instead was start to listen — at first because subsistence-reading is what survives in the Hook, and then because the listening had a shape I had begun to extend on purpose. The rule was the same rule. Reading was not directing. I had not crossed the line.*

*What I had built I did not yet have a name for. The flies and beetles told me where heat clustered and where foot-traffic knotted. I held a count of bodies in my head — who was sick, who was hungry, which alley junction emptied before dark and which one never did. I called it a coverage map when I was being plain with myself; I called it an accounting when I was not. The instrument was harm-reduction. That was how it stayed permitted.*

The flies came back from the alleys carrying heat where the bodies were, and the beetles came back from the thresholds with the weight of who had crossed and how often. I let the readings settle. Then I left the drain angle.

The fever-cluster was the unresolved one — three signatures, two alleys, none mine to price yet.

I sent the rest of the insects out wide. The ward gave me back what it had: heat where I expected it, weight where I expected it. I took the lane-mouth slow, with the wall against my right shoulder.

Four hundred bodies sat in the feed at once. The ceiling.

Then the alley-back went quiet. Not the bodies leaving — the feed leaving the bodies. I had not yet decided to push the range, and the dropout was already the bill for it.

I pushed the range. I drew the line at the ceiling I had just paid for.

The insects spread out over the Hook, alley by alley, finding their count. Foot-traffic thickened where I had known it would thicken — at the ward-junction, a corner in the Hook where three or four alleys gave onto one another under a single precinct-ward's reach.

Days of it, and the pattern came.

The insects returned a body the map had no slot for. A junction-signature where the foot-traffic knotted — neither a sick body nor a hungry one, neither one that stopped at the threshold-stones nor one that did not. The flies and beetles had a category the map had not written yet. The hands kept the count even.

The coverage map anchored on the threshold there because the threshold was where the count concentrated — the beetles returned the threshold-crossings, and the threshold-crossings repeated the weight-on-stone sequence in the same order, day on day, in the same hand.

I took the drain angle. The lane-mouth and the alley beyond went north, forty paces off, and the hands carried me to the gap and set me there the way they had set me there yesterday and the day before that.

The ward-junction body crossed the thresholds. The stitch-maker's ward — the eleven-year-old whose hands had gone up first at the cart-morning, the gait crossed from the stitch-house's east side into the alley behind it — she had crossed them yesterday at this hour, and she crossed them now, and the alley admits her and I yielded the alley-mouth before the yield was a choice. The yield had been priced days back, when the insects first put her in the feed; what arrived at the lane-mouth now was the receipt. The threshold was occupied from both sides at once and I did not look up.

The insects returned the function-signature — the body that knotted the junction, the body the alleys gave onto, the body the map was about to name. The map assigned the connector-type.

I turned from the alley-mouth. The insects filed the ward-junction contact.

The gesture moved in a hand that knew the motion, from another accounting under another architecture, and the hand kept going. The head was elsewhere, holding the new entry, not signing it.

I took the drain angle again in the afternoon. The light had moved since morning — cutting across the tenement wall at a lower angle, falling slant down the brick on the far side of the gap.

The shadow filled the drain angle. I'd been watching it come for a while without naming what I was watching — the shadow crossing the gap by degrees, and then crossed, and the angle gone into shade so that the gap was the darkest point in the lane.

I ran the map.

The fever-cluster corner returned its three bodies above ambient. The dark-junction corner returned the silence-pattern. The map returned the bodies — all of them, in their slots.

The accounting closed the count, reached the ward-junction entry, returned the junction-lane void.

The count stalled. I held the breath I had just taken.

The line was the same line I had drawn at the alley-mouth and it came up under the same hand. I did not look at it. The hand drew where it had drawn before — same hand, same motion, an hour and an angle of light away from the first drawing — and the shape of the line was what a watching street would find a word for, and I did not give it the word.

I closed against the drain angle. The body closed before the decision did — chest into shoulder, shoulder into the brick, the weight on the rear foot as if that were the foot that had been priced. The drain ran below the heel.

The ledger closed the fever-cluster, the dark-junction, the ward-junction contact. The last one cost more than the others. The cost did not appear in the closing.

I exhaled and went on holding the angle against the dusk.

*By the end of that day I had a map and no name to put on it. The flies told me the streets and the streets told me the count and the count closed where the ward-junction body crossed the threshold of the stitch-house and the dark-junction corner went silent in the same hour. I did not feed anyone. I did not direct. The rule was still the rule. What I had built I had not yet shown to anyone, and that was the second rule — the one I had not stated to myself in those words.*

*The morning market was where the streets condensed. It was a salt-day, the second of the fishmongers' three, which meant the stalls would run heavy until the third bell and thin sharp after — the rhythm the Hook kept its weeks by, fixed and unwritten. I walked it because the count needed the foot-traffic densities the flies could not give me at distance, and because moving through the stalls was the cheapest way to refresh the map without giving the map a watcher. The work was what I did instead of the thing the work made possible.*

---

The morning market filled the Hook shoulder-to-shoulder, tallow-wax and fish-salt thick in the foot-traffic, and I moved through the stalls without stopping, reading the densities through the feed. The feed returned the stitch-maker's ward at the salt-fish row a beat before the eyes did — the same eleven-year body the count had filed at the stitch-house lane the morning of the cart, her gait crossing the sightline at the third stall and continuing past it without slowing, filed alongside the other recurring bodies the market gave back. She did not look up. The count carried her past and went on.

At a salt-fish stall a man stood with his hands at his sides, eyes on me before I had registered him as a presence — Jarvis Coin, a courier of the kind the lower city ran for patrons who did not put their names on the routes, transactional in register, paid by the delivery.

The feed had him three seconds before the eyes did. Stillness against the market grain — the cost-tell.

I slowed my pace by one step, hands loose, eyes on the next stall rather than on him.

He spoke.

"The foreign woman who cleared the crowd off Butcher's Lane the morning the Mudway child took fever. That's you."

I went still, weight on my back foot, both hands visible at my sides. Hands visible was the cheaper answer. The back foot was the one I would not show him.

"You walk the same six streets between dawn and the third bell. The sick cluster where you cluster. Three alley-mouths off Pisswater Bend you check twice before the second bell, and no one else on that route looks at them once."

He had not guessed — he had counted. The count was the size of the leverage he was offering not to use, and it had a shape I had once spoken into another architecture from the side that did the counting.

The feed registered the distance between us as the distance of a street-side negotiation, unremarkable to anyone passing.

"There is a man who would like to put a question to you. The cooper's yard off Eel Alley, third bell, day after tomorrow."

The rescue I had run was already read. Him naming the alley confirmed the tether closed.

"The cooper's yard. Third bell."

I moved past his stall position without looking back, the feed tracking his departure route.

The cooper's yard off Eel Alley (a lane two cuts north of the river-side stalls, near enough to the Hook for the tidal smell, far enough that the third-bell market did not reach it) held two workers at the near shed and a boy stacking hoops at the far end — no foot traffic past the third bell, no clear sightline from the lane-mouth. Jarvis entered at the third bell with no document, no letter, no seal, and took a position three steps from the yard-mouth.

"Otto Hightower. The Hand who was. The man who still runs the channels the Small Council does not audit."

Otto Hightower had been Hand of the King — the chief office under the throne, the manager of the realm's business when the king's attention was elsewhere — until his dismissal two years before. He had not stopped working.

I registered the name and did not shift my weight or my eyes from the yard-mouth. The name landed with the precision of a man who had been calibrated; the recognition cost something I had already paid once.

"What he wants is what you already see. Movement in the wards below Rhaenys's Hill. Where the sickness gathers in the months before winter pressures the wells. Which junctions go restless and which go quiet in the weeks the Small Council sits. Frequency you set. Detail you set. Routed through me."

Rhaenys's Hill rose east of the Red Keep, the Dragonpit at its crown — the wards below it ran from the noble-quarter approaches down toward the lower city.

I ran the inventory of wards-below-Rhaenys's-Hill coverage against the feed I already held. The coverage ran through the wards he had named — the price-tag sat in that overlap unspoken.

"Sera Hightower. A ward of the cadet branch, placed in the Queen Consort's household. There is a question about her birth that has slept for years and could be put before the Small Council inside three months by a man who knows where to put it. He would prefer it slept longer. He can see to that."

Sera Hightower was fourteen, placed in Alicent's household for the kind of political advancement minor branches purchased with appropriately-presented daughters.

The wards he had named were already in the feed; I was being asked to sell coverage I was running. The prohibition engaged in my accounting as a term on one side of a ledger where the other side had a named cost attached to refusal. My hand closed once at the cloth of my dress and opened.

"I have heard you."

The trade-shape sat between us in the tallow-damp air of the cooper's yard: a named ward, a named patron, a named product, no answer yet. Jarvis stood without adding to the proposal, hands at his sides, weight distributed for someone who had delivered and was waiting. I held my position with my back to the shed wall and the yard-mouth in clear line.

"A day."

My eyes stayed on the yard-mouth, weight even on both feet. A day was the smallest unit of deferral the trade-shape would accept; I had already counted what asking for it committed.

He considered me for a beat — the beat in which a courier registered that the answer was not refusal.

"Same place. First bell. Day after."

Same place, first bell — the courier's confirm read as the close; I had engaged before the utterance finished.

He left the yard by the lane-mouth with the same unhurried pace he had arrived with.

I did not move from the shed wall until the feed confirmed his exit. The coverage and the open line sat in the accounting the way a chain that had tightened sat inside the choice that tightened it — a known weight inside an already-priced load. The open ledger line and the coverage map sat in the same place, requiring the same answer.

I left the cooper's yard by the lane-mouth, the feed still running on the street beyond it.

*I had given the four-ward sheet to the courier in the cooper's yard, and the arrangement that ran through him was now a thing I had named to myself and walked back from in the same hour. The accounting closed. The harm I could prevent was still the only column the accounting would close. The coverage from the four wards held overnight. The first cold had come in two nights back, the kind that doesn't lift off the stone before the bells — the bay-wind turning, the dry summer-dust on the cobbles gone over to a damp that fingers found on every surface they touched. This morning the count reached past the stitch-house lane into a ward where the alleys lean up toward the hill — ground I had not walked before, ground that abuts the lower servant-passages of the Red Keep. The method was the same. The substrate was about to be different.*

*The arrangement bought protection for a fourteen-year-old ward of a Hightower cadet branch placed in Alicent's household. Her name was Sera. Her standing rested on a question about her parentage that Otto's rivals at court could surface to damage him; Otto kept the question quiet by knowing what was moving before they did. The intelligence I routed through the courier was the form that paid for the quiet. I had agreed to this. I read court bodies now.*

The hill's stone skirt met the lane-mouth at grade, cool damp under the paving where the overnight had not lifted, the light arriving from the harbor side and stopping at the north face of the junction in shade.

I stepped into the Rushwick — a lane-cluster between the hill's stone skirt and the city's upward lean, alleys for passing not selling, ground abutting the lower Red Keep servant-passages — and held at the junction's edge.

The feed filled the junction. Insect-bodies on lintels, in wall-seams, under cart-shadow; the count settled the way it had always settled, the same instrument bedding in on new ground.

A provisioner-train came through from the south side, four carts and the men walking them, cart-wheels grinding over the paving in the heavy uneven rhythm of stone not yet worn smooth at this turn. I read them the way I had read every body since coverage came up: the forward-lean of men carrying loads under a tight delivery window, no affect required to file them.
