*I had set myself a rule when I came back into this body and this city — I would never again reach into a living mind, never again make a person move. I keep the count of every cost I cannot pay back in a ledger by lamplight. So far the rule has only been theory. This morning the city's hired men come down the Hook looking for bodies to conscript, and theory ends.*

---

The insects filled the lane before I had decided anything — a slow weight of them banking off the eaves, settling into the column the feed already showed me. The water-carrier ducked under the doorway threshold, alley-width, one body at a time, the street behind him already thickening with Watch.

I turned my reading outward. My body was catching up to a thing already filed.

I faced the alley-mouth. Past the threshold there was no cover — only the lane-depth, visible clean to the turn. When the city's hired watchmen need hands for a public work, they sweep a poor district and conscript whoever cannot prove they are wanted elsewhere. In Flea Bottom there is rarely anyone who can.

The insects closed the lane-mouths. Each opening went dense, the column blocked, and that was not a hand laid on anyone — routing was still what the prohibition let me call clean.

Wren came into the alley, the sealed lane-mouth at her back.

The Watch passed the Hook. Street-noise gave way under the column's footfall, and at the corner their sightline lost the alley.

The near witness faced the alley-mouth — lane sealed, no gap to read through, only blocked wall. Two faces held the wrongness a beat too long. The count of who had seen was set by faces I had not picked, a cost settled on people who never agreed to carry it.

I held the feet still.

Coll worked the net.

Coll lifted his eyes from the work and pulled the net up off his knees, and around him the alley had gone back to itself — the lane-openings widening again to their ordinary unblocked size, the sweep already a thing the street had finished with.

I looked toward the alley-mouth. The Watch column was well past now, foot-traffic re-establishing across the open gap.

Wren turned to me. The alley-width between us held nothing — no sealed lane, no obstruction, three paces of readable air — and she was looking at me the way someone twice her years looks at a thing already decided, her eyes coming to rest and not leaving. That look did not have a column in any ledger I kept.

I raised my eyes to meet hers. The near witness crossed the lane, room enough to pass without turning sideways. I threaded the needle.

"The flies were round you again," Wren said. "They were round you on Tickler's Lane two days gone, and the lane went quiet after. It goes quiet where you've been. I weren't looking for it. I just saw."

"The watch came through. They did not stop. That is all it was," I answered. "Go home, Wren. The street is quiet now."

I lit the lamp. A single flame, the radius tight — the ledger surface lit, and the rest of the room falling off into dark. The ledger was the book I kept by lamplight, a running account of every cost my actions made that I could not pay back.

I wrote the line.

The pen lifted before the stroke had finished. I had already counted what the entry would not hold, and the count was short. I struck the line. A decision written down and then unwritten — I had felt the weight of that kind of word before, in a tongue that outlived the hand that set it.

I underlined the entry.

I set the pen down.

My hand stayed at the ledger's edge, then drew back from it. The reach was cleared. The body could have closed it. Tonight I was certain only of the not-closing.

I closed the ledger. The page was short, and stayed short.
