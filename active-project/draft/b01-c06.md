*The Father's name day was three nights off, and the sept-bells had begun the longer count — two strokes added at every hour, audible across the Hook in the cold mornings before the carters started their work. For weeks I had been sending Otto the same kind of thing up the channel: who passed which junction, which lane went unused, the shape a week of foot-traffic makes. It bought the quiet on Sera's question and it asked nothing of me a decent person could not have folded into a morning's walk. What he asked for next would not fold so easily.*

---

The handcart had thrown a wheel across the mouth of the lane, crosswise, and there was no setting it upright before midday. I came down into a press of people who had already understood as much. They stood the way Flea Bottom stands when a thing it cannot move is in the way — patient, not frightened, shoulders turned to keep the angle. Tallow smoke pooled at foot level and would not rise. The crowd had backed up solid into the junction, body against body, and the morning that had been passable an hour before was a wall of warmth and cloth and the smell of the boiling-houses. I stood at the edge and looked for the way out.

She found me before her feet had turned. I caught the moment her eyes lifted out of the press and fixed on me, and only then did the rest of her start to move, crossing the backed-up crowd at a slant, the way a child crosses a current she has crossed before. I knew her before she opened her mouth — the stitch-maker's ward, eleven years old, the small steady profile I had seen at the cart-morning and the salt-fish row and a dozen evening closings since. A ward-body, familiar. What I could not place was the thing she was about to do.

"There's a way past. Cut before the cart, by the tallow-boiler's wall — the south court. It's narrow, but it's there. I been through."

She said it the way you set down a thing you have already carried, and she did not wait to watch me pick it up. She turned back into the press and found her own gap. A sound route from a stranger is not nothing. I followed where she had pointed.

The south court was just where she had said — a narrow slot off the tallow-boiler's wall, clear, the blocked lane behind me. I came in out of the smoke and took the wax-leaf from my coat, the small folding one I keep my own notes on, the one no channel ever sees. *Ward-resident, Hook,* I wrote, and *routine.* That was true, and it was the whole of what I wrote. The line where a name would go I left bare. I had decided that before the stylus touched the wax — that her name would not sit anywhere a careful man could one day read it. I folded the leaf and put it away, and went on with my walk.

---

The courier was waiting at the bench when I came back through, a sealed slip set out for me and the man already gone. I broke it and read it. It did not ask for the shape of a week's foot-traffic the way the asks before it had. It asked for names. The Black-faction ward elders in the Hook wards, by name — four of them. I read it through and then I read it again, because reading a thing twice is how I make sure I have the size of it.

I knew them. Four months of watching those wards, and the four were already faces to me — who they met, whose errands they ran, the particular way a man moves when he takes his instruction from somewhere other than the ward's own elders. I had been holding them as bodies, as habits. I had never set them down as names. I sorted the four out of memory, one and then the next, and wrote them in order on the blank form the channel wanted, in my own hand. Four names. Then I laid the form face-down on the bench and did not send it. The not-sending sat there between my hands and was the loudest thing in the court.

---

Before I sent it I would do the thing I always did. I flattened my own paper beside the form and set the four names down the one side of it, one under the next, the stylus the only sound now — scratch, lift, scratch — and under that sound the four of them stopped being a cluster I watched and became four men in my own letters, which is a different weight. On the other side I wrote Sera's name. Fourteen years old, a ward of Alicent's household, a girl I had never met and never would, exposed in ways she did not know she was exposed; the whole reason the arrangement existed. I drew a line under the two columns.

There was no trick in it. The cost to the four men was real and I did not make it smaller; the danger to Sera was real and I did not make it larger. Both sides stood there in my own hand and the line under them was honest, and an honest line came down on the side of sending. That was the whole horror of it — not that I had cheated the sum, but that I had not. The stylus stopped a finger's width above the page. My hand stood over what I had written and did not come down, not because anything was still in doubt but because the hand was slower than the verdict, and wanted a moment I would not get to keep. I set the paper aside.

I turned the form face-up and pressed my seal into the wax. Once the wax takes the seal there is no lifting it, and nothing on a sealed form comes back. The courier took it and was gone, the four names off the bench and into the arrangement, past my reach.

I drew out the wax-leaf again and opened it to the morning's entry — the blocked lane, the south-court route, *ward-resident, Hook, routine,* and the bare line where a name was not. The same hand had made both that morning: four names sent up the channel, and one name I had refused to write down at all. I looked at the two of them sitting there and said nothing to myself about it, because there was nothing to say I did not already know. I folded the leaf and put it away.
