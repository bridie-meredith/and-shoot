*The arrangement had a name now — a thing written into the channel, owed against the quiet I kept over Sera — and naming it had changed nothing about how the mornings ran. The man at the lower gate was three days gone: his corridor routed up the line, the detention come back to me through the flies two days after. One man, and the cost of him paid. There was still one thing I had seen and not sent on.*

---

Out at the grain-measures junction Oswyn had a cart-frame under both hands, holding it square between two carters who would otherwise have argued the order of the morning into the middle of the day. He held it without knowing he was a held thing himself — a place in a pattern, a junction I needed kept open, kept by a man who thought he was only keeping the peace over grain. Behind me, across the lane, Jarvis was already gone — the packet taken at the second bell and folded in the lane-mouth and carried up the line without ceremony, the way a thing goes when the weight of it has been there long enough that neither party names it. I had watched Jarvis leave and turned away before his step reached the lane-corner. The channel was running at load. It did not need me watching it run.

The wool-dyer, who I had asked for nothing, had found me at the cross-lane two days back and put the cart-timing word into my hand unprompted — the kind of small true account a person gives back when an earlier kindness sits unpaid between them. So the thing I would have had to go looking for arrived on its own, carried up the line by a debt I had not designed and could not have designed, only set the conditions for and waited under.

I set the stylus to the wax and wrote the lane out clean — the route the timing implied, the corridor it opened, all of it down where it would do its work. I did not write the name that had brought it to me. The lane went down; the dyer stayed off the wax. Not because I weighed the question and decided it, but because the hand came up off the board with that part of it still bare. I lifted the stylus. That was the whole of it — a hand off a board, the work going out built, the way I had built every quiet machine of mine, to run on exactly what no one had chosen to give it.

The merchant's shop sat on the Hook's south end with no relation I could see to any of it. He had been six months a steady square in what the flies brought me — a man who counted bolts in the morning, took a delivery past noon, shut his door before dark. This morning he drew back the narrow worktable at the rear of the shop in the low rushlight, and that small act of opening was the only thing it seemed to be, until a messenger crossed his threshold at an hour no order of cloth is placed. The merchant lifted a folded paper from the table. He held it over the little iron dish on the corner of the worktable, and he burned it.

The heat reached the worktable before any of it was meant to reach me. I had a few of mine in the air around that table — not many, enough — and the warmth came up through them first, then the curl of the smoke turning off the dish, the page already gone by the time I had the taste of it. The ash settled in the iron dish and the warmth sat there after, the way warmth sits in a thing that has done its one job. The thread that had run through that shop ran to Dragonstone: the island seat across the bay where Rhaenyra keeps her court and the heir's faction holds, half a day's water from the city. Something on that side had learned the channel was cut, and the answer was a burned page and not a rerouted one — the enemy already done before I was in it, the world running this ahead of me. Then the merchant squared the bolt-ticket against the table edge the way a man squares a thing he has squared a thousand times, and went back to being a man who sells cloth.

I pressed my thumbnail into the wax to mark the hour, because the mark was the small thing the hand does so the larger thing does not have to be settled. The thread I cut had been read somewhere I do not route to, read before I had finished knowing it was cut. It was a small thing set against the size of what waited behind it, and I would once have set it so and found it small — but the measure is wrong the way it has always been wrong, because the smaller wars still burn the people standing under them. I made the mark in the wax. I moved on from it.

By early afternoon the soap-lane woman came across the cross-lane at the rendering-lane mouth, open to my approach, and told me of a household taking visitors through the back-alley after dark — a different address than the blocked-drain matter, a different way in. It was the kind of thing already shaped to move. I drew the packet back into my hands, the covering coarse against my fingers, and untied it. I wrote the route — the observation set down, the precinct pattern drawn over it, the address carried not by the woman's name but by the pattern. The name stayed in my head where the dyer's was. I tied the packet shut. The older question — the one I used to hold an account like this against before I sent it on — did not come to stand in the way of any of the three motions. The packet was tied before there was a place in the order of it for the question to be.

I came into the feed-station at the end of the day and sat with the board bare in front of me, and ran the day back through one piece at a time. The packet sat sealed on the board where Jarvis had left nothing to add: the channel had carried everything upward, nothing snagged at it. I turned my hand toward the junction without reaching for him — Oswyn clean all day, the dispute below the Watch line, the grain holding the peace it thought it was holding. I wrote the contacts down: the warden's-precinct woman, the soap-lane woman with her night-visitors, the wool-dyer two days back. Each one had brought me something through a debt I had not designed and had not stopped accruing, and as the third went down on the wax the weight of the whole of it arrived all at once — three threads confirmed, the tether sitting there under my hand heavier than it had ever felt while I was carrying it through the day. And the cover over all of it held: a woman who does useful things in the ward, no patron visible above her, no structure behind. I left the two names off the wax — the dyer, the merchant's burn — and sealed the packet. I stood and took it to the door and set it on the outer ledge where Jarvis would collect it in the morning, and the lane was dark already, the last of the day's light gone off the stone, and the flies ran on without me in the wards I could not see from where I stood.
