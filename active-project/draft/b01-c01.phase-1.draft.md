I stepped into the corner-room and paid the building-keeper at the door-shadow across the entry, where he stood at the threshold-dim, the interior-corner gone dim under overcast morning backlight. Then I crossed the yard — the light shifted from the dim of the corner-room into the overcast diffuse of the yard — mud-recent under my boots, the yard-open ahead, the far-yard drain-channel marking the distance where Coll worked the net.

Coll — the net-mender who keeps the corner of the building's street face, a man in his fifties whose range of observation runs exactly one street and who asks nothing of strangers who can hold a needle — his eyes started to lift one breath before they lifted; the flies in the wall-bottom registered the eye-lift before the eye lifted. He worked the net.

I circled the block — the block read on a second pass, which courts fed which alleys, who owed the well-step, where the watch did not turn — and dropped the pack. Coll said, "There's mending if you can hold a needle." I held my feet, and I set my weight even on both feet.

I lifted the basket and Coll pulled the net; I threaded the needle, and the needle crossed the mesh.

The insects filled the block — every body within the block was legible and I touched none of them; passive held, day-long, a continuous suppression cost. The walls cooled.

The boots struck the cobbles. The city-watch passed the Hook — a curving lane at Flea Bottom's waterfront edge, the slum's lowest margin, where the most transient and least-protected smallfolk keep their rooms; the city-watch, King's Landing's gold-cloaked standing patrol, moves through on a rotation the block knows by sound — boots struck behind the wall, four spans and tracked through the feed without head-turn; staying invisible cost more in dense streets than I would have estimated; the patrol's line through the bend was older than the patrol.

I held the eyes, and Coll folded the net.

Wren — a stitch-maker's ward from two buildings over, eleven years old, kept in light work and two meals for trim-work and thread-sorting, with the close-watching habit a trade like that teaches — came in at the alley-mouth step, the street-open under afternoon overcast, and a girl came in through the swarm before she came in through the door; the entry was filed without a name above the line.

Wren said, "You walked the block three times this morning. Mistress Coll knows your name and you've not been here a fortnight."

I lifted the eyes from the mesh and I said, "There's no work here. Go on."

Wren said, "The flies were on the meat-stall and they were not on you. The stall is closer."

My hand stopped over the needle, and her eyes stayed on mine; I held the eyes, and the threshold held and what was on the other side stayed the size I would not name. She crossed the street. I lifted the needle.
